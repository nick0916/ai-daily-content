## How Teachers Unintentionally Encourage Cognitive Offloading

學生用 AI 多半是被作業條件逼的，改善條件比單純改作業設計更根本。

**Why** 多科作業擠壓、過夜趕工、指示模糊，以及讀書指南與考試不符，讓 AI 成為最快的捷徑。

**How** 作者提出課堂層級的做法：先引導練習、寫清楚的作業指示、設中間檢查點、過程與成果一起評分；並提出年級團隊協調作業量與共同備課時間。

**What** 作者認為學生主要不是因為不懂 AI 或作業邀請卸載而作弊；他在前校嘗試年級作業日程協調，效果有限，原文為個人經驗描述。

### 重點
- 課堂先做引導練習，並以離場券（exit ticket）確認學生能獨立開始作業
- 作業指示需寫明任務目的、困難處與卡住時怎麼辦，並可附範例提交
- 大型作業提前公告並設中間檢查點；讀書指南應在考卷寫完後依考試內容編寫

Perkins Harnly, &#8220;Lacquer Dipping Vat,&#8221; Unsplash I have been doing some tutoring over the past couple months, and I am starting to notice some trends with my students. They are routinely asked to do multiple complicated assignments per evening. They are routinely not given advance notice about these assignments, and in many instances they have to complete the work overnight. They are given classroom time to begin work on assignments, and to the teacher, that justifies the extended length of these assignments. They regularly encounter unclear directions and have to make decisions that directly impact their performance on graded formative and summative assessment. When preparing for tests, the problems and questions on a study guide do not neatly correspond to the actual material on the test. They must constantly negotiate the grading practices, expectations, absences, etc., in pursuit of learning and performance that helps them transition to college, trade, or profession. While thinking about these challenges, I started to play around with an idea that is connected to a lot of my work on AI-inspired instructional redesign, but pushes it even further. What if redesigning assignments in the age of AI is just one part of a larger redesign? What if the conditions surrounding an assignment matter more than the assignment itself? The good news here is that teachers have a lot of control of these processes. We will first consider the primary offenders from an individual class perspective. Then we will explore possible classroom and district level solutions. The Offenders Inside a Single Class The offenders inside an individual class coalesce around these two issues: inadequate preparation to complete an assignment, and inadequate time to complete it successfully. In each case, AI offers an easy out. Students don&#8217;t necessarily reach for AI to cheat or cognitively offload. They are simply trying to navigate a situation beyond their control. Graded homework without sufficient modeling in class. Students are asked to do work alone for a grade that has only been modeled once. A student who struggles at home can ask AI for more modeling or the answer. Missing background knowledge. Readings and sources assume context and vocabulary students lack. A student can use AI to explore deeper context or accept a generated summary as the ground truth. Overnight turnaround. Complicated work assigned without advance notice and due the next morning leaves no time for practice, iteration, and failure. Students can use AI to arrive at answers that involve less risk. Unclear directions on graded work. Ambiguous directions force students to make interpretive decisions that affect their grade. A chatbot offers a confident interpretation and usually the finished task along with it. Study guides that do not match the test. Once students learn the guide is an unreliable map, they look for a better one. AI will produce one on demand, but nothing guarantees that it will map better onto an unpredictable assessment process. Grading only the finished product. Completion grades and product-only rubrics communicate to students that the process is of little value. AI can accelerate the process by generating several products to choose and tweak en route to a final grade. Feedback that is given after the final assignment is due. A draft returned after the final is due primes students to seek input from AI. Here, the alignment issue surfaces. Will the AI feedback align with the vision that teacher has for the finished product? Counter-Moves Inside the Teacher&#8217;s Own Course Each of these has a counter-move inside the teacher&#8217;s own course, and most of these moves come down to planning. The most effective one happens prior to the assignment communicated orally in class or textually on the LMS. If out-of-class work is practice, a student needs a rough version of the skill in order to engage effectively with that work. That means guided practice in class until most students can start a problem on their own, and a quick check before the work goes home: one problem on an exit ticket tells you more than a show of hands. For readings it means front-loading context and key vocabulary, so the work at home is reading instead of decoding. A student who feels ready to practice usually practices. The challenge here is whether to grade homework, practice, and/or out-of-class work. Grades can serve as a motivating factor, but they also raise the stakes of the assignment. I eventually landed on a homework-in-class model. Students have time in class to complete practice problems. I collect and give feedback so that students can gauge their progress. Directions should be written for a student working at home, in writing on the assignment and in the LMS (no variance in language between modes of expression). Define the task and its purpose, name the hard part, and say what to do when stuck. Provide an example submission when possible. Directions that answer &#8220;what do I do if I can&#8217;t start?&#8221; remove the most common reason to open a chatbot. The challenge here is scaffolding this process appropriately. The full delineation of every step and process could easily become a substitute for student interpretation and agency. Are there times when the struggle is the point? What grading process is most appropriate for such situations? Complicated work needs advance notice and more than one night. Post major assignments with the unit calendar, and collect something en route to the final submission: an outline, a first paragraph, the first few problems. The checkpoint brings the moments of confusion into class time, where the teacher can clarify, reframe, and adjust the assignment accordingly. The challenge here is more curricular. When our curriculum includes a set number of topics, units, skills, and/or competencies, how do we open up space to elongate work cycles when the next thing is constantly pressing for attention? Some subject areas are more adaptable than others. We genuinely need a solution that works in all kinds of courses. Study guides should be built from the test, after the test is written. Period. Students regularly interpret study guides as a map for what will be on the exam. Throwing curve balls in an attempt to build higher order skills will backfire for the majority of students, building a culture of unpredictability and distrust. Here again, the challenge is calibrating the struggle on the final assessment. Is the focus out-of-box thinking? I&#8217;d argue that we want to cultivate such skills in our students, but a final assessment is probably not the place to do so. Finally, grading communicates value to students. When we grade process alongside product, we show students that both count. Grading the process also modulates the risk of the final submission. If process feedback lets the student know they are on the right track, the final product feels less like a gamble, and the temptation to offload shrinks. The challenge here is that grading the process turns the process into a product. How lightly can we grade a checkpoint and still signal that it counts? Completion credit paired with a single comment may be enough, if the students learn to wait for the comment before moving on. Offenders Beyond a Single Classroom Some offenders cannot be fixed inside a single classroom. When students encounter these issues from multiple teachers, a systemic solution may be necessary. Multiple complicated assignments per evening is a scheduling problem. Grade-level teams can agree on how many substantial out-of-class tasks students can tackle on a given evening, and share an assessment calendar that caps major assessments per day. We tried this at my previous school to limited effect. The trick is keeping the calendar up to date. Grading, late-work, and absence practices can vary from class to class, and students spend real time negotiating these different expectations. Make-up work after an absence lands on top of the regular load. Common practices across a grade reduce that negotiation, freeing students to engage with the work itself. None of the above can happen without common planning time. Coordinating calendars, aligning study guides with tests, and building checkpoints into units all require teachers to plan together. Building leaders who want less offloading have to protect that time for teachers. The Conditions of the Work Today, most school AI efforts move along two vectors, general AI literacy and assignment redesign, each framed as a safeguard against cheating and plagiarism. Both are valuable, but they rest on a weak premise. Students do not primarily cheat because they misunderstand how AI works or because a task invites offloading. Most cheating comes from the pressure of navigating multiple classes, ambiguous directions, thin background knowledge, high stakes set by complex grading schemes, and a shortage of time, energy, and motivation. Alongside AI literacy and assignment redesign, we need to look more locally, at the conditions of the work: most of them sit inside a teacher&#8217;s own course, and the rest belong to grade-level teams and the leaders who give them time to plan. The goal is a classroom where students can succeed without a shortcut.
