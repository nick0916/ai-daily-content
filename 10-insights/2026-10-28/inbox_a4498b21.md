## Should You Study The Solutions Or Solve Problems?

學習效果不取決於解題有多吃力，看解答有時反而學得更好。

**Why** 解題時若工作記憶被目標拆解佔滿，就沒有餘裕去發現可遷移的規律。

**How** 依據認知負荷理論，用範例解析（worked examples）取代自行解題，等熟練後再改用提取既有知識（schema retrieval）來練習。

**What** 文中 Sweller 的代數實驗中，看範例的一組在新題型上有四分之三解出，自行解題的一組沒有人解出；作者指出這種效果會隨熟練度反轉。

### 重點
- 代數實驗：同類題兩組表現相當，但遇到新題型時，看範例組約四分之三解出，自行解題組零人解出
- 認知負荷理論：內在負荷是學習所必需的；外在負荷（如手段-目的分析）若超過工作記憶容量，會擠掉學習所需的部分
- 新手靠啟發式逆向推導；專家直接從起點提取圖式（schema）解題，因此範例的好處會隨練習增加而減弱

 Learning requires effort. But not all effort is useful for learning. 
 Consider the following situation. You have some problem sets for a class you’re taking. You have two ways you can study them: 
 
 You can solve the problems on your own. 
 You can look at the solutions given by your teacher. 
 
 Which method would result in more general learning, letting you perform better on new types of problems? 
 
 
 
 Interestingly, in many cases the answer is actually option two: looking at the solution. And this can be true even if you’re capable of solving the problems on your own. 
 This seems to go against what a lot of us were taught about how to study effectively: the only way to build skill is by solving hard problems. No pain, no gain, right? I sympathize with this perspective because, at least until I came to understand the science, that’s largely how I felt too. 
 In this essay, I want to share with you the research that changed my mind. I want to show you what goes on inside your head as you solve difficult problems, as well as give you the key to know when problem solving works (and when it underperforms). 
 The Puzzle 
 Is it possible to solve a problem without learning how you did it? 
 In 1982 the psychologist John Sweller was conducting an experiment on problem solving with undergraduate students. 
 The problems the students were given were fairly simple: given a starting number and a goal number, the aim is to go from the start to the goal. But there are only two possible moves: either multiply the number you have right now by three, or subtract twenty-nine from it. 
 For instance, suppose you have the number 15 and you’re told to get to 16. You could multiply it by 3, to start, which gets you to 45. And then you subtract 29, which gets you to 16. Done.   
 In all the problems there was a trick. Every problem Sweller gave could be solved by alternating between multiplying by three and subtracting twenty-nine some number of times. In other words, you just had to repeatedly apply those two rules and you’d always get the answer eventually. 
 But as Sweller later recounted : 
 
 “My undergraduates found these problems relatively easy to solve with very few failures, but there was something strange about their solutions. While all problems had to be solved by this alternation sequence ... very few of the students discovered the rule ... Whatever the problem solvers were doing to solve the problems, learning the alternating solution sequence rule did not play a part.” 
 
 How could students solve these problems, but not learn the method they were using to solve them? 
 How We Solve Problems 
 Just ten years before Sweller’s experiment, future Nobel laureate Herbert Simon and Allen Newell published a landmark book, Human Problem Solving . In it, the duo studied the mental processes people use to solve hard problems. 
 They found that when people tackle a problem they don’t know how to answer, they engage in a search process through a problem space. This is a little bit like trying to navigate a maze, except instead of going through a physical space with walls and dead-ends, you’re navigating through the possible states of the problem itself.   
 In Sweller’s experiment, there were only two moves—either multiply by three or subtract twenty-nine. Of course, in most problems, there are more possible moves. A Rubik’s cube has eighteen different moves, leading to a possible total of forty-three quintillion configurations. That’s part of why just twisting the cube around randomly doesn’t solve it—there are too many possibilities. Instead, we need to do something smarter. 
 Simon and Newell found that subjects reduced this complexity by using heuristics, or general-purpose procedures for solving problems. These heuristics are innate. We don’t need to be taught them. We apply them spontaneously, even if we aren’t aware of it. 
 Simon and Newell identified several of these heuristics. But there was a particular one that Sweller thought might be playing a role in his students surprising behavior: means-ends analysis. 
 This heuristic has a fancy name, but it’s really easy to understand. Means-ends analysis starts by looking at a goal, then looking for actions that could let you reach it. If you can’t perform that action directly, you then create a sub-goal to get to the point where you can take that action. If you repeat the process enough times, you’ll get to the solution. 
 Let’s make that clearer with an example. Assume you need to get from New York to Paris. What’s a way you could get to Paris? A plane. How do take a plane? You buy a ticket. How do you buy a ticket? You can buy one from the airline at the airport. How do you get to the airport? Take a taxi. You get the idea. 
 
 
 
 Means-ends analysis works. It’s a good strategy for solving unfamiliar problems, but it has a drawback. Thinking up all those goals and subgoals takes up a lot of mental bandwidth, what psychologists call your working memory . And, if your working memory is overloaded, you may not have much capacity left for noticing useful patterns in what you’re trying to learn. 
 To Sweller, this suggested an experiment. If you could suppress means-ends analysis, would you learn to solve the problems better? 
 Problems without Goals? 
 Sweller tested this in an experiment with students learning to solve trigonometry puzzles. Essentially, he asked students to solve for the missing angle or side length of a shape, much like you probably did in high school. 
 The classic formulation for these puzzles is to designate one angle or side-length and ask the student to find it. To do that, students typically rely on means-ends analysis. Starting from the goal, they work backward to figure out what other values they need in order to calculate the answer. This approach uses a lot of working memory, which might distract from learning the actual principles of geometry. 
 To suppress this, Sweller came up with “goal-free” problems. These were the same trigonometry puzzles, but, instead of finding a particular angle, students were asked to find as many angles and side lengths as they could. 
 
 
 
 Sweller and his colleague, Elizabeth Owen, tested the students’ understanding of the puzzles by giving them a new type of trigonometry puzzle they had never seen before. The goal-free group got eight out of ten right, while the group who practiced traditional problem solving only managed three. 
 The Worked Example Effect 
 Goal-free problems helped avoid the mentally demanding means-ends analysis, but they were of limited use. The problem spaces of most real-world problems are just way too large for this approach to scale. 
 To deal with this, Sweller opted for a different technique: worked examples. These were written-out solutions to a problem, typically followed by a practice attempt on a very similar problem to ensure you actually studied it. 
 In a follow-up experiment, Sweller and colleagues tried this approach with algebra problems. Half the students were given worked examples. The other half were given the problems to solve, without solutions.   To ensure that the success rate of the two groups was matched, any student from the problem-solving group who failed to solve the problem after five minutes was just given the solution. So we know that the problem solvers wouldn’t underperform simply because they never got to the answer. 
 When tested on similar problems, both groups performed about equally. But here’s the kicker—when a new type of problem was introduced, three-quarters of the worked-example group solved the problem, while none of the problem-solving group could. 
 Far from resulting in brittle memorization, studying the worked examples seemed to result in better understanding and generalization of the technique than problem solving did. 
 Cognitive Load Theory 
 These experiments, along other experimental effects, led Sweller and his colleagues to formulate cognitive load theory . This theory basically states that learning involves at least two different kinds of demands on our limited cognitive capacity. 
 The first, intrinsic load, is the working memory that is required for learning. Without this effort, learning doesn’t happen. 
 In contrast, extrinsic load is the working memory that isn’t strictly required for learning, but may be imposed by the task. We’ve already seen one type of this created by problem solving—means-ends analysis requires you to keep all those goals and subgoals in mind as you work toward the solution. 
 But it’s not the only kind. Extrinsic load also occurs in other cases, like when you have to visually move around materials to make sense of a diagram, or if you have irrelevant information you need to ignore. 
 If the total load exceeds your working memory capacity, some of it will be dropped. And if the part that was dropped was intrinsic to learning, you’ll end up learning less. Optimizing cognitive load—ensuring you’re maximizing the mental effort spent on genuine learning—is the key to learning well. 
 Part Two: The Reversal 
 Okay, I can imagine you saying, that’s a neat theory, but it’s not how you get good at difficult skills. You don’t get good at math, programming, physics or anything just by looking at the teacher’s answer—you have to think hard and do it for yourself. 
 Recently I published a video about the power of retrieval practice . Recall strengthens memory more than review, and students learn better when they’re forced to remember with a closed book rather than rereading a text. 
 Am I contradicting myself? 
 Well, no. Because, as it turns out, the power of goal-free problems, worked examples, and even teaching itself starts to reverse as you continue to learn. 
 I wrote above that people automatically use heuristics, like means-ends analysis, when they’re facing hard problems. As it turns out, this is only partly correct. 
 Novices Use Heuristics, Experts Retrieve Knowledge 
   Novices who are encountering problems for the first time solve problems this way. As we’ve seen, this is often the only way to solve them. 
 But experts, who have learned how to solve the problem, or one like it before, have a different process. Instead of solving a problem by working backward, they can work forward from their starting point, using the knowledge they already possess to get to the destination. 
 
 
 
 Take the example from before, of trying to go from New York to Paris. When I asked how you’d solve this problem, a lot of you probably didn’t go through the kind of backwards reasoning I described of first deciding on air travel, then buying a ticket, getting to the airport, hailing a taxi, and so on. 
 Instead, you’ve been on trips before, so you didn’t need to do this. Maybe you opened up your phone and went to your favorite travel website and started looking up flights. 
 This kind of forward reasoning doesn’t have the same heavy, extrinsic load that means-ends analysis creates. By using stored knowledge in your head, what psychologists call schemas, you can automatically recognize what sort of problem you’re dealing with and apply solutions from your starting point to get to your destination. 
 For experts, solving a familiar problem doesn’t require heuristic search. It simply requires retrieving the appropriate schema from memory and using that knowledge to get you to the next step. 
 Retrieval practice strengthens memory more than review does. As a result, under these conditions, we should expect the worked-example effect to flip. As you practice more and more, the benefits of studying the solution will get weaker, and the benefits of retrieving the schema to solve the problem yourself will go up.
