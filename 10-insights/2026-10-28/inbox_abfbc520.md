## Beyond Click-Next: When, How & Why to Build Learning Games with AI

用遊戲設計原則，讓 AI 做出讓學習者真正互動的學習遊戲，而不是換皮的測驗。

**Why** 多數 L&D 團隊仍把 AI 用在產內容，只有 16.6% 的人讓學習者直接與 AI 互動，主因是不知道要求什麼、無法驗證成果，也不知道怎麼上 LMS。

**How** 依學習目標動詞對應 Gee 的遊戲原則（如 fish tank、well-ordered problems），再寫出指定玩家必須「行動」而非「選答案」的提示詞，並在上線前檢查簡單策略能否通關。

**What** 作者示範用 Claude Opus 5.5 將 NIST Cybersecurity Framework 做成城鎮防禦模擬遊戲，每關加入一個功能；文中引用 Clark 等人（2016）的研究，指出更好的設計版本效果 g=0.34，加入 scaffolding 則 g=0.41。

### 重點
- 依動詞決定遊戲原則：Explain/Predict 對應 fish tank，Apply 對應 well-ordered problems，Analyse、Evaluate、Create 各有對應模式
- 提示詞要求「玩家行動而非選選項」，且遊戲需判斷對錯，避免按鈕或選單暗示答案
- Clark, Tanner-Smith & Killingsworth（2016）：更好的設計版本效果 g=0.34，scaffolding 效果 g=0.41（原文引述）

Hey folks 👋 When L&D teams first started using AI regularly, the question I got asked most was: “ How can I use AI to make more courses and more content, more quickly? ” In 2026, content creation is still the number one use of AI in L&D ( Taylor and Vinauskaitė, 2026 ), but the questions L&D teams are asking are starting to change. This year, in my day-to-day work and on my bootcamp, one question has come up more than any other: “ How do I use AI to make the learning experience more dynamic, active and experiential? ” Our focus is shifting from using AI to produce content to using AI to innovate the learner’s experience. This seems to be part of a broader trend. Over a third of L&D professionals now say improving the quality of learning experience is one of the main things they want from AI. However, ambition hasn’t yet turned into action: only one in six of us (16.6%) use AI in ways that learners interact with directly, such as AI-based practice, feedback or coaching ( Taylor and Vinauskaitė, 2026 ). For most of us, AI is still a “back end” tool for streamlining the L&D workflow, rather than a way to improve what our learners experience. So I’ve been exploring a question: if so many of us want to use AI to innovate the learning experience, why aren’t we doing it? Three barriers keep coming up in my interviews with L&D folks: We don’t know what to ask AI for. We ask AI for learning experiences which are “innovative” and “dynamic”, but we just get content and a quiz with a shinier interface. That’s because AI fills any gap in a request with the most common example of what we asked for, and the most common example of digital learning is content followed by a quiz. When we do build something new, we don’t know how to check it. What AI builds often looks impressive, but is every rule correct? Does it teach what we intended? Could a learner succeed without learning anything at all? Most of us don’t have a reliable way to answer these questions without spending hours on QA, so we don’t bother. We don’t know how to get it into our LMS. An interactive built in a tool like Claude or ChatGPT is usually a single web page, not a SCORM package. We’re often left wondering how to upload it, track completion and see what learners actually did. Without answers to these questions, even promising prototypes rarely make it beyond a demo. This week, I’m sharing a practical way to tackle all three barriers: game design . It’s an approach I’ve been testing to help turn the ambition for better learning into something we can actually build, which actually enhances learning. In this post, I’ll show you how the approach works in practice: where game design and learning design overlap, how to match a game to a learning objective, the prompts I used to build five pro learning games with AI, and the checks I’d run before anything goes near a learner. Let’s dive in. 🚀 Where Game Design Meets Learning Design Game designers have long seen games as, essentially, learning experiences. For decades, researchers like Gee, Koster, Malone & Csikszentmihalyi have explored how games get people to take on difficult challenges, develop new skills and want to keep going. For us learning designers, the value of game design is huge. The motivation, curiosity, persistence and absorbed focus we see in gaming are exactly the behaviours and conditions we want to see (but rarely achieve) in learning experiences. By analysing the mechanisms of game design we can start to understand what sorts of design approaches make people stay with a challenge long enough to try a strategy, see what happens, use the feedback and try again. Of course, motivation, persistence and flow don’t, by themselves, lead to sunstantive learning. But they help explain why game design is worth our attention: it offers ways to sustain the effort that meaningful practice requires. Our job is to make sure that effort develops the capability we actually want. Perhaps the most influential account of the overlap between game design and learning design comes from James Paul Gee’s 2003 book, What Video Games Have to Teach Us About Learning and Literacy . His central insight is useful for us as learning designers: good games get people to tackle difficult problems, practise persistently and develop increasingly sophisticated knowledge, skills and behaviour. In this groundbreaking work, Gee set out 13 design principles which apply both to great games and to great learning experiences (you can check them out in this video 👇). Gee was also clear about what stood in the way of applying powerful game design principles to learning design: “the cost of implementing them” (Gee, 2005). Back in 2005, building a well-designed game took a development team, time and a budget that most L&D teams simply didn’t have. AI has dramatically lowered this implementation barrier. With a clear design brief, a single learning designer can now use coding-capable models like Claude Opus 5.5, GPT‐6 Astra and Gemini 4 Argon to build a playable game in minutes. What’s left for us is the part that always mattered most: choosing the right game for the objective in question, and checking that that what we created has the impact on learning that we intended. Translating Game Design into Learning Design While there’s a lot of overlap between game design and learning design, saying they’re the same thing is an oversimplification. Creating conditions that drive motivation, persistence and flow means people keep doing something, but it doesn’t necessarily mean they learn anything. Think of the hours many of us have spent on a puzzle game on our phones. We might get very good at the puzzle game, but not much else. That’s the catch: people get better at whatever a game makes them practise. So a learning game only works if the thing players do over and over is the thing we need them to learn, such as applying a policy, spotting a risk or making the right call. This is where we, as learning designers, come in. Game design gives us powerful mechanisms for motivation, persistence and practice. Our job is to decide: when a game is, and isn’t, the right answer to a learning problem which game design approach to use, based on the objective. The research shows why learning design expertise matters. Without it, learning games fall short: Looking like a game isn’t enough. On average, educational games are no more motivating than conventional instruction (Wouters et al., 2013). Why? Because most “learning games” copy how games look, not how they work. The gains come from learning design. Learning games outperform other approaches most when they’re built around principles that drive learning. Better-designed versions of the same game beat standard versions ( g = 0.34), and the biggest gains came from adding scaffolding: support that guides learners at first, then fades ( g = 0.41) (Clark, Tanner-Smith and Killingsworth, 2016). Other features that reliably improve learning from games include coaching (advice and feedback during play) and self-explanation (prompting players to explain their choices) (Mayer, 2019). Designing & Building Learning Games 101 First, a warning: if you just ask AI to design and build a “learning game”, you get a quiz dressed up in game-like costume. Sheep in wolf’s clothing: When I asked AI for a “learning game” I got what looked like a game - characters, a desk and interactive, rubber stamps — but underneath the polish it was a multiple-choice question. As ever, when it comes to designing learning games, AI needs you — specifically, the expertise to enable you to tell AI not just what to do but how to do it. To help you get started, I created a simple 3-step guide to take you from learning objective > pro game design prompt: Step 1: Write your objective as “Learners will be able to...”. For example: “ Learners will be able to spot a personal data breach in their everyday work. ” Step 2: Define your objective’s level using the associated verb, e.g. Explain or predict → level = understand . Follow a process → level = Apply . Spot or notice → level = Analyse . Decide or justify → level = Evaluate . Produce or design → level = Create . Step 3: Use the objective > game design map (below 👇) , to design and build your learning game. Objective > Game Design Map Objective level = Understand: explain or predict how something works Design a game that follows the principle of: The fish tank What it is: Players start with a simple version of a scenario or problem. They guess what will happen when they change something, then see the result. More detail is added as they get better. Why it works: Starting simple stops people feeling overwhelmed, so they can see how things connect (Sweller, 1988). Guessing first, then seeing the result, shows them where their thinking is wrong, so they can fix it. The Build: Best source document: A framework or model that explains how the parts affect each other, such as a risk model, a pricing model or a framework like the NIST Cybersecurity Framework . What to ask AI for: “Build a learning game that follows James Paul Gee’s “fish tank” principle: a simplified version of a complex system, so the player can see clearly how one thing affects another. The aim is for the player to be able to explain and predict how [system, e.g. the NIST Cybersecurity Framework] works. Model the game on a sandbox simulation game like SimCity . Start with the simplest version of the system: just two or three of its most important elements. The player sets up the system themselves, for example by switching elements on or off or adjusting settings within a limited budget. Before each [event, e.g. incident, new case, price change] , they mark on the scene what they think will happen to their own set-up. Then they run it and compare. Once their predictions are mostly right, add the next most important element. Repeat, level by level, until the player is working with the full system. Make it a game, not a quiz. The player acts on the scene, by clicking, setting, typing or building, and never chooses from a list of possible answers. No button, stamp or menu may name or hint at the answer. The game works out whether they were right from what they did, and shows what happens as a result. Check that no simple strategy, such as always saying yes or doing everything, can pass a level. Take every rule from the attached [source document] and invent only the people and scenarios.” Claude Opus 5.5 turned the NIST Cybersecurity Framework into a town-defence simulation game. Learners set up a town’s cyber defences, predict how an attack will play out, then run it and see whether they were right. Each level adds one more of the framework’s functions, as per the principle. Objective level = Apply: carry out a procedure correctly Design a game that follows the principle of: Well-ordered problems What it is: Players practise the basic steps on easy cases until they can do them without thinking. Then each new level adds an exception or a twist. Why it works: Learning the basics before the exceptions means people are never asked to do everything at once. Practice makes the basics automatic, so they can focus on the tricky cases (van Merriënboer, 1997). The Build: Best source document types: A procedure or SOP with clear steps, decision points and exceptions, like the guidance on the right of access from the UK Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO).
