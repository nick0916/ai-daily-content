## L&D Is Making More AI Video Than Ever. But Is Any of It Actually Helping People Learn?

影片製作的成本與時間已大幅下降，但學習成效是否同步提升仍未確定，關鍵在於影片該用來示範表現，而非單純傳遞資訊。 **Why** 原文引用 Synthesia 報告，指 52% 的 L&D 團隊已用 AI 製作影片，其中 88% 認為主要價值是省時，作者則質疑是否真的在幫助學習。 **How** 作者整理學習科學研究，指出影片適合示範有順序的操作或行為，搭配提問、練習與回饋才有效；另以一份 2013 年 GSK 舉報不當行為 SOP 測試各工具的預設流程。 **What** 預設的 Docs-to-video 流程產出的影片出現規則被改寫的問題，例如把「any executive directly senior」改為「any senior executive you trust」，並加入原文沒有的「zero tolerance」一句。

### 重點
- 原文引用數據：傳統企業影片每完成分鐘約 $1,000 至 $10,000（Clutch, 2025），AI 平台列價約 $1 至 $3；Synthesia 報告製作時間平均由 13 天降至 5 天（-62%）。
- 原文引用研究：影片加入主動教學時 g=0.80，取代主動教學時 g=0.28（Noetel et al., 2021）；純概念類內容的動畫優勢僅 g≈0.23（Berney & Bétrancourt, 2016）。
- 原文作者的主要建議：影片用於有順序的操作或行為示範，並搭配提問與練習，而非只是把文件轉成旁白摘要。

Hello folks &#128075; In 2026, around half of L&amp;D teams are using AI to make video (52%; Synthesia, AI in L&amp;D Report 2026 ). The reason is clear: 88% say the main value of AI is time saved on content creation. The time and cost savings are real and substantial. Before AI, a training video was usually a commissioned job: a production company, a crew, a presenter or actors, and an edit. Agency pricing puts corporate and training video at $1,000 to $10,000 per finished minute (Clutch, 2025). On the AI platforms&#8217; own list prices, a finished minute now costs roughly $1 to $3. The reduction is easy to trace. The crew is now a monthly platform subscription of about $29. The presenter is AI-generated, and the edit is a prompt. Practitioners report that AI has cut the average production time for a training video by 62%, from 13 days to 5 (Synthesia). In HeyGen&#8217;s survey, nearly a third of L&amp;D teams now make more than 21 training videos a month. At pre-AI speeds, that output would have taken around 270 production days: more than a year of one producer&#8217;s working time. And there are signs this is only the start. The tools are multiplying: one directory now tracks 123 AI video generation tools, 108 of them added this year, nearly three-quarters with a free tier (Toolradar, August 2026). They&#8217;re diversifying too, from avatar explainers into generated film-style scenes, prompt-built animation, interactive video and AI role-play simulations. And last week Google made its scene generator, Gemini Omni in Google Vids, free to anyone with a Google account. TL;DR: video production in L&amp;D has been dramatically commoditised, and it&#8217;s only getting started. The one question we haven&#8217;t stopped to ask is: what&#8217;s the impact on learners and learning? Are we helping people learn, or are we just making more video? In this week&#8217;s post, I ask exactly this and share my tips on how to avoid the &#8220;magpie effect&#8221; and ensure that AI video generation drives learning as well efficiency . Let&#8217;s dive in! The Current State of AI Video Tools (2026) As we head towards the end of 2026, AI video tools are multiplying at a rate of knots. One directory now tracks 123 AI video generation tools, 108 of which were added this year (Toolradar, August 2026). The tools are also diversifying. Two years ago there were two kinds of AI video generation product: a narrated explainer fronted by an avatar (Synthesia, HeyGen, Colossyan) and narrated stock footage (InVideo, Pictory). Now there are seven. The originals are still here, joined by five new flavours: Generated footage : film-style scenes with spoken dialogue, built from a description rather than a stock library (Google Vids, Veo, Runway, Kling) Conversational avatars : avatars that listen and reply to the viewer in real time, in a live two-way conversation. HeyGen LiveAvatar does this, and so do Synthesia&#8217;s Video Agents, which Synthesia describes as its first step towards two-way video. Interactive video : branching and quizzes built into the player (Colossyan, Synthesia, HeyGen) Generated animation : animated video from a document or a single prompt (Vyond Go, Runway, Pika) Simulations : an AI counterpart for learners to practise conversations and decisions with (Second Nature, Yoodli, Synthesia Roleplay Sessions, Copilot&#8217;s Learning Agent) The tooling menu has never been longer, or cheaper. Last week Google added a free tier to Vids , meaning anyone with a Google account can now generate six AI clips a month, about a minute of footage, without paying anything. This move is part of a broader trend, rather an outlier; nearly three-quarters of the AI video tools now have a generous free tier and low-cost entry point (Toolradar, August 2026). So generating video for learning content is a bottleneck. But knowing how to use these tools to actually help people learn is. Every tool on that list can support learning in the hands of someone who knows how to use it. But every one of them defaults to video that does little for it: upload a document, get a narrated summary, publish. That&#8217;s information transmission, the job the research says video does least well. Even the simulation tools, which are built for practice, default to the vendor&#8217;s stock scenarios and an AI score nobody has validated. The tools have changed. The defaults haven&#8217;t. To understand why the defaults matter, and what to do instead, we need to look at what the research says video actually does for a learner, and what it doesn&#8217;t. The Science of Video-Based Learning 101 L&amp;D loves video. We tend to assume it&#8217;s the premium format: more engaging than text, more dynamic than slides, more likely to be opened than a PDF. It&#8217;s easy to see why. Video looks like effort and investment, so stakeholders read it as quality. It gives us metrics that look like success, such as views, completion and watch time. And it feels easier to learn from. Research shows that learners rate video as less demanding than text and come away feeling they&#8217;ve learned more (Salomon, 1984; Paik &amp; Schraw, 2013). That last point is also the trickest. Feeling that you&#8217;ve learned isn&#8217;t the same as having actually learned. So if the goal is learner satisfaction, video wins. But if your goal is measurable and substantive learning, the picture is very different. Over forty years of research confirmed that video-based learning has one pedagogical benefit that text on a page can&#8217;t give a learner: a model of how something unfolds in time. To do something yourself, whether it&#8217;s a procedure or a conversation, you need more than the information as a rule; you need a picture of what doing it looks like: the order, the timing, what changes and when. Learners build that picture by watching someone do it and rehearsing it in their heads (Bandura&#8217;s observational learning). A page gives you the rule, but it can&#8217;t give you the picture in the way that a video can. So video helps most when the learner has a performance to reproduce. Here are three related use cases for video-based learning and the data on their impact: Completing a task in a fixed order. Watching a task being done from the operator&#8217;s viewpoint lets the learner rehearse the movement before doing it (Fiorella et al., 2017). Dynamic visuals beat static ones by d = 1.06 for this kind of content (H&#246;ffler &amp; Leutner, 2007). Developing a behaviour in a live interaction. To have any hope of changing their behaviour, a learner has to see the behaviour in action, not read about it on the page. Behaviour-modelling training shows the behaviour done badly and well, spells out the rules for great, then adds practice and feedback. This method is backed by ~120 studies which demonstrate real, measurable effect on learner behaviour and performance over time (Taylor, Russ-Eft &amp; Chan, 2005). Identifying the difference between right and nearly-right. What differentiates great from not great is most often an action, not a word. Comparing near-identical cases by watching the differences in action trains learners to notice the feature that matters better than explanations (Schwartz &amp; Bransford, 1998; Kellman et al., 2010). So the TL;DR is that video can be a very effective mode of delivery for learning, but only when it&#8217;s used for the right kind of goal and in the right way. Correspondingly, there are also ways we can use video which either have no impact on learning or - worst case scenario - negatively impact learner engagement and learning outcomes. Here are two common examples of the latter: Example 1: The wrong kind of goal: information the learner needs to keep. When the content is a concept, a rule or a definition, nothing unfolds, so there&#8217;s nothing to model. Worse, the learner loses what a page gives them for free: the ability to go back. Once the clip moves on, it&#8217;s gone (Leahy &amp; Sweller, 2011). Across 61 studies comparing animated with static graphics, the advantage was small (g &#8776; 0.23; Berney &amp; B&#233;trancourt, 2016). This is where the satisfaction trap bites hardest: the explainer feels easy, so learners think they&#8217;ve got it. Example 2: The wrong way: watching without doing. Even for the right kind of goal, a video only becomes learning when the learner does something with it. Across 105 randomised trials in higher education, most of them in health sciences, video added to active teaching gave g = 0.80; video swapped in for active teaching gave g = 0.28 (Noetel et al., 2021). Asking questions between video segments took final test scores from 68% to 90% (Szpunar, Khan &amp; Schacter, 2013). Reviewing a recording of your own performance against a standard improved professionals&#8217; interaction skills by 0.40 SD (Fukkink et al., 2011). The pattern is consistent: the video content is never the intervention. The question, the practice and the feedback are. How AI Video Tools Work Now, let&#8217;s compare the science of video-based learning to what the AI video tools are built to do. Open almost any of them and the first thing on offer is a document to upload or a script to fill in. The output is a narrated summary: information the learner can&#8217;t go back to, with no performance to model and nothing to do. In other words, the wrong goal, used the wrong way. What AI generation tools do by default versus what they need to do to support learning (October, 2026) What&#8217;s noticeable is that every AI video tool now offers the same default: upload a document, get a narrated video. This isn&#8217;t the only thing that these tools do, but it&#8217;s the first, default option on the screen when you open them, and the fastest route to something that looks finished. To see what it produces, I ran that default workflow on a real document: a publicly available 2013 GSK procedure for reporting misconduct . It&#8217;s exactly the kind of policy L&amp;D teams turn into compliance training every week, and exactly the sort of doc they&#8217;d upload to generate a video. The Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) I used as an input for the most used AI-generated video platforms among L&amp;D. I ran the doc two ways: First, I let the tool decide. I uploaded the procedure, chose Docs to video, and pressed Create. The only input was the document. The tool chose what to keep, what to cut, who the film was for and what it should say. Then I decided. Before opening the tool, I made the decisions the default had made for me: Who is the learner? The manager receiving a report, not the employee making one. What should they be able to do? Respond to a report in line with the procedure: stop, listen, and take it forward rather than dismissing it. What would doing it badly look like? Specific, visible behaviours a learner could point at and name. The default outputs failed in five ways, each of which maps onto a well-documented way that learning goes wrong: 1.
