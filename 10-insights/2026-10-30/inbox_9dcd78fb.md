## The Railway Came Before the Train

早期鐵路早於火車兩百年，瓶頸常在軌道而非引擎。

**Why** 馬車在泥濘路上拖重貨效率低，木製軌道可大幅降低拉力，煤礦因此需要鐵路把煤運到水路。

**How** 木軌、鐵軌與 1709 年 Coalbrookdale 以焦炭取代木炭煉鐵形成互相依賴的循環：煤煉出焦炭，焦炭煉出鐵，鐵強化軌道，運煤成本再下降。

**What** 1804 年南威爾斯蒸汽機車拖運 10 噸鐵，但過重而壓壞鑄鐵軌，軌道改良後才可實用；作者以此類比今日電網，並引用 IEA《Electricity 2026》指出全球超過 2,500 GW 的再生能源、儲能與大用電專案卡在併網排隊。

### 重點
- 1604 年英格蘭已有近諾丁漢的木製 wagonway，據 Richard Rhodes 引述，軌道拖拉所需力氣約為一般泥路的六分之一
- 1709 年 Coalbrookdale 的實驗確立以焦炭（coke）取代木炭煉鐵的做法，讓鐵的產量擴張不必同步擴張林地
- 1804 年蒸汽機車拖運成功但壓壞鑄鐵軌，作者以此類比今日發電建設快於電網建設的併網瓶頸

Do you know, England already had a railway by 1604 only. Just that, there were no locomotives, no railway stations, platforms, and thus no passengers buying tickets. It was just the wooden tracks, heavy wagons loaded with coal, and horses pulling them. The tracks had a simple purpose. Moving a loaded wagon over an ordinary dirt road required considerable effort, especially during Britain’s wet winters. Wheels sank into mud, roads became impassable, and horses could move only so much. Place those wheels on wooden rails, however, and the same animal could pull a much heavier load. Richard Rhodes, in Energy: A Human History , notes that moving a cart on rails required roughly one-sixth the effort of hauling it over an ordinary dirt path. For mine owners, that was reason enough to build a railway. The first known English wagonway appeared near Nottingham in 1604, roughly two centuries before the first successful steam locomotive journey. Which raises a rather curious question. If the railway already existed, why did the train take another 200 years? Coal had a transport problem In our previous story, When Wood Became Too Expensive , we saw how Britain’s growing demand for fuel pushed people towards coal. But coal presented a geographical difficulty. The coal was underground, often far from the towns and industries that needed it. Digging it out was expensive enough. Getting it to customers could be worse. Britain’s rivers and coastal waterways offered the cheapest way to move heavy cargo. The real headache was getting coal from the mine to the nearest navigable water. As nearby seams were exhausted, mines opened farther inland. Every additional mile meant more horses, carts, men, maintenance and payments to landowners whose property the coal had to cross. Wooden wagonways helped solve this problem. Horses pulled coal wagons along parallel timber rails towards rivers, where the coal could be transferred to boats. On downhill stretches, gravity sometimes did the work. In one particularly delightful arrangement, the horse itself rode downhill on a platform attached to the wagon, saving its energy for the return journey. The wagon was now carrying the horse. These early railways had no connection to passenger transport. They existed because coal was valuable enough to justify building a dedicated road for it. There was, however, a catch. Wooden rails wore out quickly under heavy loads. Some lasted only a year or two. The obvious improvement was iron. Unfortunately, iron was expensive. And here the story takes an interesting turn. Coal needed iron. Iron needed coal. For centuries, producing iron had depended heavily on charcoal made from wood. Ironmakers could burn ordinary coal, but impurities, particularly sulphur, damaged the quality of the iron. Charcoal produced better results, and Britain had relied on it for generations. Only now wood was becoming increasingly costly, precisely the problem that had encouraged people to dig more coal in the first place. In 1709, experiments at Coalbrookdale helped establish a workable alternative. By heating coal with very little oxygen, ironmakers could turn it into coke, driving off volatile compounds and some troublesome impurities. Coke could then be used in blast furnaces instead of charcoal. The process needed years of refinement, and adoption was gradual. But the economics were changing. Coal was plentiful, while charcoal was becoming more expensive. Iron production could expand without requiring a matching expansion of woodland. More iron also meant new possibilities for transport. Iron wheels appeared on wagonways. Wooden tracks were reinforced with iron plates. Eventually, cast-iron rails began replacing timber altogether. A curious relationship had developed. Coal helped produce cheaper iron. That iron made stronger rails. Stronger rails allowed larger quantities of coal to reach customers at lower transport costs. Growing demand for coal encouraged still more mining and iron production. Each improvement helped make the next one worthwhile. By the late eighteenth century, Britain had an expanding network of mineral railways, worked almost entirely by horses. The train was still missing. Then someone put an engine on wheels Steam engines had already proved their value in coal mines by pumping out water. Subsequent improvements made them more economical, and experiments with high-pressure steam offered a way to make them smaller. Meanwhile, horses were becoming expensive. Britain’s wars with Napoleon increased demand for military horses, and the cost of fodder rose. Mine owners had another reason to seek mechanical alternatives. In February 1804, a steam locomotive finally hauled ten tons of iron along a railway in South Wales. Some seventy spectators climbed onto the wagons for the journey. It worked. The engine moved its load, and on clear stretches reached nearly five miles per hour. There was one serious inconvenience. The locomotive was so heavy that it repeatedly broke the cast-iron tracks beneath it. Britain had spent centuries improving the movement of heavy goods, only to discover that its newest machine was too much for the railway. The tracks had to improve again. Stronger, more malleable iron rails and better foundations eventually made locomotives practical. Once they could reliably carry the engines, railway networks began expanding for freight and passengers. The railway had been waiting for the train. The train, when it finally arrived, had to wait for a better railway. The tracks we’re still building There is something familiar about this sequence. Today, solar panels can be manufactured and installed far more quickly than many new transmission lines can be planned, approved and built. A solar farm may have land, equipment, investors and customers, yet remain unable to deliver its electricity because the local grid lacks capacity. The International Energy Agency’s Electricity 2026 report estimates that more than 2,500 GW of renewable generation, storage and large electricity-consuming projects are stalled in grid connection queues worldwide. Some can be connected by using existing networks more intelligently. Others will require new substations, transformers, cables and transmission lines. In a sense, we are once again developing the engine faster than the tracks. The comparison has limits, of course. Electricity networks are far more complicated than nineteenth-century wagonways, and their problems include balancing supply and demand in real time. Still, the old coal story offers an interesting way to look at technological progress. We tend to remember the visible inventions: the steam engine, the locomotive, the solar panel. Yet much of their usefulness depends on improvements elsewhere, often in equipment that attracts little public attention. When the first steam locomotive successfully pulled a load in 1804, the railway was already about two hundred years old. So, the core learning from this story is that the diffusion of many innocations has taken, a way longer than the innvoator has wished for. Let’s see how will AI era will shape up, this time.
