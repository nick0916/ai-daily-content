This Drop starts from a strange wave of clips that flooded my timeline at the end of September: next-level game mods, like Minecraft's characters dropped into the world of Elden Ring, or skateboards in Modern Warfare 2. People have decompiled or cloned entire AAA games using AI agents? Then came an open-source Photoshop clone, then music software, in a snowball that shows no sign of stopping. The results are still unstable (hence the name slopensource ), but it doesn’t matter: a deeper shift is happening in software (and hardware) production. I sketch the figure of the Agentic Bricoleur (via Lévi-Strauss) as a new actor in this emerging middle tier of the economy, and imagine its organisational consequences. Maybe, as the TikTok user portou put it: “Companies used AI to replace workers, we use AI to replace companies.”

It started, as many stories do these days, with a tweet. In the clip, a soldier from Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 2 rides a skateboard across a rooftop and lands a kickflip. The caption: “AI HACKERS HAVE PUT SKATEBOARDING IN CALL OF DUTY MODERN WARFARE 2!”

Two days later, another tweet surfaces on my timeline: in the clip, Steve, the blocky hero of Minecraft, flies in diamond armour over the royal capital of Elden Ring, the FromSoftware masterpiece. The incursions have started.

My first reaction was to dismiss these clips as yet another kind of slop, aimed at teenage or nostalgic millennial gamers: franchises glued together for a few seconds of engagement. If this is slop, though, it is slop of a higher order: most of these clips show real mods, semi-working software that was vibe-coded into existence.

In some corners of TikTok, this mashup genre is named game incursions - a term coming from the Marvel multiverse, where an incursion is the moment one universe breaks into another.

The term was popularised by the modder chasm , author of the sensationalist tweet above, who also shows how a game incursion works with his SkyCraft . The mod brings the Minecraft interface and character into the world of The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim, allowing the player to break blocks in Skyrim’s world, fight its NPCs with Minecraft weapons, and invite friends into the same mirrored world.

Other examples went viral soon after: Spider-Man swinging through the Gotham of Batman: Arkham , Mario from Super Mario 64 jumping among Elden Ring ‘s monsters, Call of Duty soldiers in Minecraft ‘s pixelated world, and Minecraft itself dropped into Pokémon Ruby .

As with many other waves this year, it was a new LLM release by Anthropic, Claude Opus 5.5 (and its more advanced version Claude Fable 5.5), that catalyzed this wave. Expectedly, tweets about games incursions are imbued with the mellifluous tone of AI hype, and there are plenty of evangelists who are reposting these clips saying that they are realized with “one prompt”. The same rhetoric is detectable in the ambitious toolkit named Universal Modder , launched with the promise “Every game is moddable now!” and a promotional video that, as an X Community Note soon pointed out, was stitched together from other people’s mods. One can’t help noticing that its author works for fal.ai , a company that sells access to generative image, video, and audio models, and whose paid service the toolkit can use to generate art and sound.

But how is AI actually used in these mods? VGTimes helps us by distinguishing three methods, which differ in how deeply they enter the original game:

The first, and most common, leaves both games intact and builds a bridge between them. Two programs run side by side on the same PC and constantly exchange data (e.g. camera position, terrain, image depth, damage), so that Steve can walk on the asphalt of Los Santos. This is how Minecraft entered GTA 5 and Skyrim , and how Spider-Man reached Gotham. A single unexpected state is enough to break the exchange, so these mods frequently crash.

The second opens the game’s code through decompilation. Game studios keep their source code proprietary, but a decompiler can reconstruct a raw approximation of it: functions without names, variables without meaning. AI Agents excel at turning it into legible code and at matching it with the original. A Nintendo 64 game estimated at 596 days of manual work has been fully decompiled this way in 84.

The third abandons the original code altogether and rebuilds the game from scratch, with the agent doing much of the writing. The skateboarding clip comes from a complete rewrite of the 2009 Modern Warfare 2 , and hobbyists rewrote the entire World of Warcraft client to add skateboarding there too.

Each of these processes used to require months of work. Former Epic Games employee Aakash Gupta noted that a studio team usually spends several quarters on a traversal system like the MW2 skate mod, while the project counted only 31 commits when he posted. The debate on the actual speed of change is interesting, but it does not alter the nature of the phenomenon. Whether or not the one-prompt propaganda holds, and allowing for a good amount of manual coding (the modder chasm says he spent weeks working by hand on the Minecraft code), agentic coding is its main technical enabler.

While I was writing this piece, the same logic left the games realm to spread into the software world at large. On GitHub, we can find an open-source toolkit ambitiously named Reverse Engineer Anything (REA) that hands decompilers to coding agents with a blunt promise: see a feature you like in an app, let the agent take it apart down to the binary, and build your own version. Another viral repository is ArtCraft , which promises to have reimplemented in the programming language Rust the entirety of the Adobe Suite (Photoshop, Lightroom, Premiere Pro, Illustrator, Acrobat).

Online commentators who tried ArtCraft were lukewarm, to say the least, about how well it actually works, and my friend and brilliant interlocutor Simone Robutti soon dropped a portmanteau for it in our group chat: slopensource .

Still, a deeper change is incubating here: what these projects share is a change in who can modify software, and under which conditions. The modders behind the incursions and the ArtCraft’s creators share a peculiar method: they work with materials already at hand, like other people’s games and code, and recombine them into new objects. They point towards a figure I will call, with Claude Lévi-Strauss, the bricoleur . Its emergence reveals a contradiction: the capacity to appropriate, combine and modify software expands at the same moment as dependence on the privately owned infrastructures that make it possible.

It also raises an economic question. If a modder with an agent can rebuild a program in days, the scarcity of skilled labour that sustained the software industry’s margins disappears, and the value it captured has to move elsewhere.

The French anthropologist Claude Lévi-Strauss introduces his famous figure of the bricoleur in the first chapter of La pensée sauvage (1962), while discussing the technical inventiveness of non-Western peoples. From there, the anthropologist builds his famous contrast between two ways of making, engineering and bricolage. Interestingly, Lévi-Strauss traces the etymology of the latter term from the French verb bricoler, which originally applied to ball games, billiards, hunting and riding, always to describe an incidental movement, like a ball that rebounds, or the horse that swerves from its course.

If the bricoleur is, at least semantically, a figure of serendipity, the engineer starts from a project: he defines one, then procures the materials and tools it requires to execute it. On the other hand, the bricoleur works “with the means at hand” (l es moyens du bord ): a finite, heterogeneous stock of objects, saved because they might come in handy one day, each already shaped by earlier uses. Before starting, the bricoleur questions his stock, asking each piece what it could become. The result is therefore always a compromise between the project and the materials: the bricoleur starts from existing objects and bends them to purposes they were not designed for.

The Brazilian anthropologist and intellectual Eduardo Viveiros de Castro continues Lévi-Strauss’ work in his paper “On Models and Examples”, reading this dichotomy as two poles of a continuum: every engineer becomes something of a bricoleur when his plans meet the real world, and every bricoleur is also, always, calculating and anticipating their next moves. What really separates them, he argues, is where they look for guidance. The engineer follows a model while the bricoleur is inspired by examples .

A model, in Viveiros de Castro’s sense, is a general plan conceived in advance and applied to particular cases, which are expected to conform to it. It moves from the universal to the particular, in a top-down descent and it thus creates a hierarchy between those who design it (the planner) and those who must follow it (the planned).

An example is instead a particular case that inspires another particular case. It demonstrates that an operation can be performed and invites a variation on it. Examples circulate horizontally, from person to person, transforming at each passage, with no original to return to.

Models give orders and enforce order; examples give cues.

The distinction is political as much as epistemological: examples are produced by people who are forced to live inside someone else’s model. The inhabitants of engineered landscapes and imposed institutions are never fully compliant: they bend and adapt elements of the model to their advantage, opening small spaces of autonomy from within. Today some of the most pervasive models we live inside are technological. As platform brutality settles in as the ambient condition, tiny examples of agency emerge from the bricoleurs online.

I first started conceptualising the bricoleur posture while preparing a lecture on cyberdecks: handmade portable computers built by non-technical people using a Raspberry Pi, the most interchangeable component in contemporary electronics, and housed in found cases, such as a vintage make-up kit or a Polly Pocket toy. When Amni Raihan asked “why are the girls building cyberdecks?” , she pointed to one of the main signals of the trend’s relevance: its virality, and the popularity it reached among young women and so-called “e-girls”, who build and document their machines on TikTok and Instagram.

Before embracing the bricoleur as a figure of agency, we must admit that, for decades, this kind of technical subjectivity belonged to a familiar type: the white, male nerd, devoted to his tools and often resentful toward women and outsiders.
