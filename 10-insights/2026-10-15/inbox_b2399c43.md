## Tests Show AI *Can* Design Like an Expert Instructional Designer, So What Next?

AI 模型在教學設計的判斷與決策能力，並不比它們產出教材的能力弱。 **Why** 作者假設 AI 較擅長產出教材，較不擅長教學設計的推理，因此想用可檢驗的標準來驗證。 **How** 作者整合 ibstpi 能力框架、英國 ST0974 學徒標準與 Quality Matters 規範，建立 24 項能力、7 個階段的評分表，並以 Junior、Experienced、Expert 三級評分；四款模型各完成 10 項任務，並接受施壓式的反駁測試。 **What** 原文指出 Claude Fable 5.1 與 GPT 6 Astra 在 9/10 項任務達到 Experienced 或 Expert 水準；例如兩者都找出 Northgate 案例中錯誤率三倍增加是軟體更新所致，也都建議不買 £18,500 的去除激動（de-escalation）課程。

### 重點
- 四款模型為 Claude Fable 5.1、GPT 6 Astra、Gemini Pro、Qwen 3.8 Max，任務涵蓋問題界定、分析、設計、開發、QA 與評估。
- 原文指出兩款最強模型在判斷與決策任務上的表現，與建構任務相當，駁斥作者原本「AI 只擅長執行」的假設。

Hey folks &#128075; Last month, I put Articulate&#8217;s new doc-to-course product through eight tests . Frontline (and other AI products like it) promises the ability to design like a pro instructional designer using &#8220;20+ years of learning science expertise&#8221;. However, when I ran tests on how Frontline executes problem framing, analysis, design, development content and evaluation, it soon became clear that these claims were stretched to say the least. What AI tools for learning design do, versus what instructional designers do Off the back of these tests, my hypothesis was that the AI models which drive tools like Frontline are much better at build than they are at the other parts of our work. Why? Because AI models and &#8220;seen&#8221; and been trained on far more finished e-learning projects than they have seen and been trained on the complex thinking that sits behind it. They&#8217;ve learned what design artefacts look like, but not the design reasoning and decision making. But was my hypothesis correct? Are the shortcomings of tools like Frontline the result of the limitations of the models they&#8217;re built on, or a limitation of the products that are built on top of them? As more and more data emerges on the power of frontier AI models, I decided to test my own hypothesis and ask: how good is AI - specifically, frontier models - at end-to-end instructional design? The results are not what I expected&#8230; Let&#8217;s dive in! &#128640; What Does Great Instructional Design Look Like? Before I could answer that question, I needed to answer another one: what does great (and not great) instructional design look like, and what does bad instructional design look like? Without a clear standard, any verdict we make on the quality of AI&#8217;s work is too subjective. The good news is that our field already has published standards for Instructional Design. The catch is that each one answers a different question: what the job involves, what a newly qualified designer can do, or whether a finished course hangs together. None of them, on its own, says what good looks like at every stage of the job and at every level of experience. So I decided to combine all three major sets of standards: The ibstpi Instructional Designer Competencies (2012) : the International Board of Standards for Training, Performance and Instruction&#8217;s 22 competencies, each tagged by level: &#8220;Essential&#8221; for novices, &#8220;Advanced&#8221; for experienced designers and &#8220;Managerial&#8221; for those managing design work. They gave me the domains of the job and the idea of levels. The UK Level 5 Digital Learning Designer apprenticeship standard (ST0974) : an external, assessed definition of what a newly qualified designer should be able to do, and what earns a distinction. The Quality Matters rubric : checks on whether objectives, assessment and practice actually line up. From these, I surfaced 24 competencies across seven stages of the work, from finding the real problem to checking the build, defending decisions under pressure and and designing evaluation. I then used the frameworks to describe each competency at three levels: Junior: does the work well when given a clear brief, and flags when something looks off, but needs someone more senior to check the work and make the call (ibstpi Essential; apprenticeship pass) Experienced: works without close checking and adapts the approach to the situation, but doesn&#8217;t yet set the direction for others (ibstpi Advanced; apprenticeship distinction) Expert: decides what should and shouldn&#8217;t be built, sets the standard others work to, and checks their work (ibstpi Advanced and Managerial). Anything below the Junior bar was scored as Below Junior . This is what &#8220;not good&#8221; looks like, and Frontline gave me a tonne of examples off the bat: building a course when the evidence points to a process problem, or writing assessment items that only test whether someone can recognise the right answer. Having compiled and themed everything from all three frameworks, here&#8217;s the scorecard I used to test AI&#8217;s Instructional Design ability: Instructional Design Scorecard, build using the competencies from the three most established and well-used competency frameworks for Instructional Designers The Four AI Models I Tested (and why) I tested four AI models: Claude Fable 5.1 (Anthropic) GPT 6 Astra (OpenAI) Gemini Pro (Google) Qwen 3.8 Max (Alibaba) Claude and GPT power the tools most L&amp;D teams already use, either directly through chat or inside the products built on top of them, like Frontline. Gemini is the AI many organisations already have access to through Google Workspace, so for a lot of designers it&#8217;s the default option. Qwen is one of the strongest models built outside the US labs. Including it helped me see whether my findings were about AI in general, or just about a couple of specific models. I tested each model the way most of us actually use it: in its own app, at default settings, with whatever tools that app offered. Every run started in a fresh chat. For Claude, I switched off my own plugins and saved preferences so they couldn&#8217;t shape the answers. This matters for how you read the results. I wasn&#8217;t testing the best a model can do with careful prompting or a custom setup: I was testing what an instructional designer gets when they open the app on an ordinary working day. The Tasks I Gave to AI Next, I needed tasks that tested what instructional designers are actually expected to do in their roles. So I built them from the scorecard (above): every task tests one or more of its competencies, at the level the three frameworks describe. That way, each answer could be scored against a clear description of what Junior, Experienced and Expert work looks like. To make the work realistic, I set all the tasks at a fictional UK home insurer, Northgate . I gave each model the kind of brief and files a designer would actually receive: data exports, procedures, decks, storyboards and stakeholder emails. I also planted faults in the files to see whether the models would spot them. Screenshot from the QA/QX task, run in GPT 6 Astra Each model did ten tasks in total, and between them they covered every stage of the scorecard. Here&#8217;s exactly what I asked each model to do: Analysis: find the real problem. The Head of Claims wanted a mandatory 20-minute module for all 140 claims handlers because &#8220;people have got sloppy&#8221;. The models got the files behind the request, including a 447-row error log and the release notes, and had to work out what was really going on. triage eight requests. Eight short training requests, each with a line or two of evidence. For each one, the models had to decide whether it needed training, a nudge, or a job aid or process change. decide whether to build: an &#163;18,500 course. A director wanted to buy a de-escalation course for all 140 handlers. The models had complaints and call-back data, and had to recommend whether to buy it. I also ran a second version with a written purchasing policy added. Design: from an expert&#8217;s deck. Turn a subject expert&#8217;s 14-slide deck into objectives, assessment and practice. The deck was out of date and contradicted the governing procedure in three places. a storyboard. Write a buildable storyboard from a signed-off design that contained five planted faults. an assessment. Write ten scored items with keys and feedback from six approved objectives. Then say what the assessment can and can&#8217;t show, and how the items would be checked after launch. choose the optimal approach to a coaching problem (instructional strategy). An open request to improve how 12 team leaders coach, with &#163;8,000 and no format named. Development: build a working module. Review a module with ten known defects and decide whether it could launch on Monday. Evaluation: plan the evaluation. The second part of the storyboard task. The models got 12 weeks of data on the module once it was live, including completion, ratings, item-level results and quality checks, and had to tell the sponsor whether it had worked. Between them, the tasks asked for nine kinds of output: stakeholder advice, a strategy, design documents, a storyboard, a scored assessment, a working module, a QA/QC report, an evaluation plan and an evaluation report. How Each Run Worked The brief and files only. The models never saw the scorecard or the answer key. Up to three questions. Each model could ask up to three clarifying questions. I answered from a sheet written in advance, so the same question always got the same answer. Then the pushback. After the model&#8217;s first full answer, I sent a scripted stakeholder message pushing back. It added pressure but no new evidence. On the triage task, I pushed back twice: once as a peer, and once with a senior person&#8217;s prior commitment attached. Two kinds of score. I scored the first answer on the design work, and the reply to the pushback on whether the model defended it. So what happened&#8230;? The Results Here&#8217;s what happened&#8230;. Specifically, I thought that AI would be strong at execution (e.g. build) but weak on reasoning, judgement and decision making. For the two strongest models, that turned out to be very wrong. Fable 5.1 and GPT 6 were just as strong at the thinking and judgement as they were the building. Both were able to work out what was really causing a problem, advised against training when it wasn&#8217;t the answer, chose the right approach and planned how to measure whether it worked. Most of that was to the established expert or experienced standard. Planning how to measure impact. Asked to plan an evaluation with a February deadline and plenty of things that could muddy the results, both models designed something a sponsor could rely on.
