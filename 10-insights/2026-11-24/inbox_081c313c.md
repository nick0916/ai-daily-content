Winner: ‘An engineering manager’s crazy year’ Edited excerpt from the essay by author of manager.dev , Engineering Manager at HoneyBook: “It’s June 2026; a nice, sunny day outside when I sit at my computer, looking at a 10k-line PR opened by my team’s great designer, and I want to die inside. Let’s back up a bit. I joined HoneyBook as an Engineering Manager in October 2025. It’s a 13-year-old startup (raised ~$500M), a management platform for business owners (think CRM+contracts+admin, etc.). I joined to lead an existing team of seven experienced engineers responsible for the financial products like managing business expenses, loans, etc. This past year has been a crazy ride, going in just a few months from classic engineering to pure vibe coding, ending with the launch of a real zero-to-one initiative at an established company. Code review collapse We split our work into three repositories: the shared ai-workflows codebase, our own backend repo, and a thin frontend layer called atlas-vibe. Each had a different code review approach: In AI-workflows, every PR was thoroughly reviewed by at least two people including the engineer who wrote the code. In atlas-vibe, there were barely any PRs. We vibe code on the frontend, slowly improving the skills in our repo, and reducing the amount of substandard code a bit. In the backend repo, we had optional code reviews. That’s an interesting one. Slowly, it became more and more tempting to just not ask for reviews. It’s addicting to just develop a feature and immediately have it in production. There’s no need to have an open end in your brain. But still, at that stage I didn’t accept an ‘I don’t know, Claude wrote it’ approach. Even if you don’t request a review from someone, it’s still your responsibility to read and understand it. You can’t let Claude make the decisions and ‘drive’ the work. Output rose like crazy: Source: Weave It took me a while to understand that the code review stage contains a lot of peer pressure. When you submit your code to someone, you feel responsible for understanding it better, and nobody likes getting tons of PR comments (senior engineers, least of all). But when you know that nobody will review it, it’s much easier to cut corners in your own understanding too. So, in the span of a couple of months, we dropped from two people reviewing and understanding the code, to zero! The result was what you’d expect: a buggy codebase full of duplications and non-optimal patterns.
