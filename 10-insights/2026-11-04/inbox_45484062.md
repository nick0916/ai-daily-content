## The bleeding edge of L&D in late 2026

多數 L&D 只是多了「用 AI 快做課」的要求，前沿角色則轉向定義品質與成效。

**Why** AI 讓產出變快，但檢查品質沒有成為工作的一部分，速度因此壓過品質。

**How** 作者分析 2026 年 8 月英美 158 則 L&D 職缺，並檢視 10 家前 1% 雇主的職缺與公開說明。

**What** 前 1% 角色約佔 1%，工作重心轉為定義品質標準、撰寫 AI 必須遵守的規則、抽查與衡量成效；美國前 1% 職缺薪資中位數約 $159K，標準「ADDIE + AI」角色約 $82K（作者自述為小樣本）。

### 重點
- 2026 年 8 月分析 158 則英美 L&D 職缺：約 81% 以製作內容為主，約 18% 混合製作與審查，約 1% 描述人與 AI 的新分工。
- 多數團隊用 AI 但少有深度整合：Synthesia 調查 421 人中 87% 使用、僅 6% 完全整合；職缺中僅約 3% 把「檢查 AI 產出」列為工作內容。
- 作者引用 Faros AI 2025 報告：重度用 AI 的軟體團隊產出多 98%，但審查時間增加 91%，整體交付未改善。

Hey folks 👋 Something I get asked a lot at the moment is: what’s happening at the bleeding edge of L&D? What are the orgs and teams which are really innovating doing differently, and how? So, in this week’s post I thought I’d share what I know about what the most pioneering L&D teams are doing with AI right now, and how this has changed L&D roles, skills & workflows. First, I’d like to make one thing very clear: you are most likely not behind the curve. Across the global L&D industry, a whopping 91% of the open L&D job adverts I analysed still ask for someone to design and build courses, using the workflows we have always used. The role and responsibilities are the same as before, just with an added expectation that we now AI tools like LLMs, video-generation tools and AI authoring tools to do the work faster (and cheaper). An open role at a safety-inspection firm , for example, is currently looking for an instructional designer who can “apply AI tools to accelerate content development, including text, visual, video, avatar, and voice-based assets”. A typical “use AI to make more content” L&D role (November, 2026) TLDR: In the vast majority of L&D teams, the job is the same, but there’s a new expectation that we can use AI to build courses (specifically async eLearning courses) faster. The most innovative L&D roles look very different. In these roles (which so far make up only ~1% of all open L&D roles), L&D professionals have moved from producers of content to directors of AI: Their responsibility has shifted from making content to defining and ensuring quality and results: deciding what’s worth building by analysing data, defining quality standards and measuring impact. Their workflow has flipped: AI drafts and quality-checks its own output, while L&D folks focus on writing the rules it must follow and spot-checking whether it’s following them. The skills that matter have shifted from authoring-tool expertise to deep expertise in problem definition, solution design and impact monitoring. As a result, the top 1% are paid more: a LOT more. The top-1% roles I analysed in the US advertise a median of around $159K per annum, compared with a median of $82K for more standard “ADDIE + AI” roles. That’s almost double (based on a small, hand-picked sample). In this week’s post, I’ll break down how the top 1% work, show how some roles are taking small steps in the same direction, and share three things you can try this week to start preparing for a move from producer to director. Let’s dive in 👇 Where These Findings Come From I see some of this up close in my advisory work. But I wanted to check it wasn’t just my own bubble, so in August I analysed 158 open L&D job adverts from across the UK and US, to see what employers are asking for across the industry. The picture was stark. About 81% of roles are written mainly around making content. About 18% mix making with some discovery and review. Just 1% describe a new split of work between people and AI. I then took a closer look at ten employers whose adverts fit that top-1% profile. Why adverts? Because what an employer asks someone to do (and pays them for) is hard evidence of how work is changing. The top 1% sit mostly in organisations whose product is learning or AI, not in corporate L&D. I’ve kept the ten employers anonymous in the text (their adverts are linked at the end), and I’ll use these short labels throughout: Five learning-product companies: the apprenticeship provider (UK tech apprenticeships), the AI-skills start-up, the curriculum publisher (US schools), the language app and the course platform. One enterprise AI company educating its own customers: the AI software company. Three specialist teams inside very large employers: the search company (sales enablement), the online retailer (vendor education) and the enterprise software company (talent development). One corporate L&D team: the financial services firm. These are hand-picked signals from the frontier, not a picture of the whole market. Some of the adverts have since closed, but they still show what each employer asked for. If you work in corporate L&D, you might be tempted to stop reading here. Please don’t! The fact that most of these teams sit outside corporate L&D is exactly why this matters. These practices are arriving through learning and AI companies first, and teams that don’t adopt them may well end up buying them from vendors instead. Most Teams Use AI, but Few Have Changed How They Work Interestingly, the adverts match what L&D teams tell us about themselves: AI use is now close to universal, but deep change is still rare. In the survey I ran with Synthesia, 87% of 421 L&D professionals said they use AI, but only 9% have begun to scale it across the organisation and 6% describe it as fully integrated ( Synthesia, 2026 ). LearnUpon found 89% use AI and 11% consistently embed it across end-to-end workflows ( LearnUpon, 2026 ). Hiring shows the same gap. In my study of 158 UK and US L&D adverts from August 2026, ~90% mentioned AI, but only 3% made checking AI output part of the job, and 10% named any measure of success. ~90% of L&D job adverts mention AI, but only 3% make checking the quality of AI’s work part of the job. This gap matters. It tells us that most of us are still under pressure to produce, not to check what’s been produced. AI has made making faster, but checking hasn’t become part of the job, so speed keeps winning over quality. I see this frustration again and again on my bootcamp: people who feel under pressure to produce more, faster, and who know that quality is paying the price. It’s also a trap. Software has already hit this wall: teams using AI heavily shipped 98% more work, but review time rose 91% and overall delivery didn’t improve ( Faros AI, 2025 ). L&D is heading the same way: more content, more to check, and almost no one whose job it is to check it. The top 1% are the teams finding a way round it. Here’s a quick snapshot of how they compare with everyone else: How the division of labour between human and AI varies across typical and “top 1%” L&D roles What Sorts of Learning Experiences the Top 1% Design & Build Another question that I get asked more and more is: what sort of learning experiences are innovative L&D teams actually building with AI? The short answer: not courses. Or least, not so many courses. Most of us still build courses, specificially async eLearning. Nine in ten adverts (91%) name a course, module or programme as the thing the person will build. About a third (50 of 158) ask for Storyline, Rise or Captivate, the standard eLearning tools. Where AI appears, it makes the same things faster: scripts, voiceover and avatar videos. Only 6% of adverts ask anyone to design learning that AI delivers. Some of us build a broader range of learning experiences, but the course is still the main unit of output.* These adverts list a wider mix: eLearning, live sessions in person and online, short videos, microlearning and branching scenarios. One asks for “experience with ILT, VILT, WBT, microlearning and social learning”. Another wants “AI-supported scenarios, simulations or branching learning experiences” built in Storyline or Captivate. The choice of formats is wider, but most of it still adds up to a course. The top 1% produce far fewer courses, more help on the job, and in-person learning for high-stakes moments. Five kinds of experience come up in the adverts I analysed: Help in the flow of work. Guidance built into the tools staff already use, and adaptive paths instead of one-size-fits-all courses. Short courses that stay current. Built in weeks, not months, and auto-updated as the product or policy changes. AI practice and coaching. The apprenticeship provider runs an always-on AI coach alongside its human coaches. The search company builds “AI-driven practice... and simulation tools” into its curriculum. The enterprise software company wants someone to build AI coaching tools. Live learning where a person matters. The apprenticeship provider’s programmes are “built around human coaching”, and its designers build the “high-touch... high-stakes onboarding moments” by hand. The financial services firm’s designers run workshops in person and online, using role plays and practice scenarios. Long programmes. The top 1% haven’t dropped depth: the apprenticeship provider still runs two-year apprenticeships. * My inference from example adverts. But what the top 1% design and build is only half the story. How the Top 1% Work The bigger change is in how the top 1% of L&D roles work: what L&D people are responsible for, how the work flows between people and AI, and the knowledge and skills this requires. Let’s take each shift in turn, starting with responsibility. Shift #1: From Making Content to Ensuring Quality & Results What does this new responsibility look like in practice? In the top 1%, it shows up as five practices. Most of the ten employers do the first; fewer do the rest, and none (yet) does all five. Practice #1: Designers write the rules AI must follow This is the most common practice, and it marks the biggest change of all: who owns quality. The apprenticeship provider, for example, describes its technical designer as “the human judgement layer sitting on top of our AI-assisted content pipeline”. In other words, the job is to decide what AI got right, fix what it didn’t, and build what AI can’t. The curriculum publisher’s designers help write the rubrics and checklists that guide both freelancers and AI. Meanwhile, the AI-skills start-up’s designer writes quality criteria, then tests and redesigns the prompts and marking criteria AI uses. Practice #2: AI checks the work, and people make sure its checks are right Once the rules are written down, AI can apply them at scale. The apprenticeship provider now has an AI agent review every coaching session against its coaching framework (before, a manager could review one a month). Managers still decide what to act on. A quick note: this comes from the company’s own published account, not an advert. The rarer skill (and, I’d argue, the most important one) is testing whether AI’s checking can be trusted. Only one advert asked for it: the language app’s Assessment Design Lead must be able to read reliability statistics well enough to know whether a scoring system works, not just whether it looks plausible. Practice #3: Designers build reusable pieces, not just courses The search company’s sales enablement team uses AI to automate 60–80% of traditional design work, cutting build times from months to weeks, and builds modular components for reuse worldwide. The online retailer’s vendor education team says plainly that it doesn’t build courses and hope vendors find them; it designs on-the-job guidance and adaptive paths instead. Practice #4: Shorter courses, kept up to date Some top-1% teams still build courses; they’ve just made them shorter and keep them current. The AI software company’s designer ships 15–45-minute courses in sprints of three weeks or less, and watches product changes and customer signals to spot new needs before anyone asks. Practice #5: Designers measure whether it worked About three of the ten build measurement into the job, so this is still a minority practice, even at the frontier. The AI-skills start-up uses completion and drop-off data to judge whether an assignment works, with AI doing much of the analysis. The financial services firm puts learner research and validation sessions into a Level I design role.
