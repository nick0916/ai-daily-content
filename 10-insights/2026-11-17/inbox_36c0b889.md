## How to Become an AI PM in 2027: The Complete Guide From Cursor's First PM

AI PM 的價值在於先找對問題，再判斷 AI 是不是對的解法，而不是會用 AI。

**Why** 市場上許多人把「用 AI 的 PM」和「做 AI 產品的 PM」混為一談，也盲目複製前沿實驗室 PM 的職涯路徑。

**How** 用一個客服文件問答 copilot 的案例，逐步講解八項技能；已涵蓋的有產品感與 AI UX、模型直覺，以及 prompting、context engineering 與 RAG，並用 retrieval、context、generation 三種失敗原因診斷錯誤答案。

**What** 作者邀請 Cursor 首位 PM Rohan Varma（曾任 OpenAI Codex 產品負責人），提出八項技能堆疊、三個作品集專案、面試與求職策略，以及 90 天計畫。（內文被截斷，後半部技能與計畫未涵蓋）

### 重點
- 客服 copilot 案例：把「全自動聊天機器人」改成「找政策、附引用、由客服審核後送出」，並設定可量測目標，例如政策問答時間 4 小時/天降到 2 小時/天（作者標註為示意數字）
- 同一個錯誤答案（把 30 天退貨說成 60 天）可能源自 retrieval、context 或 generation 三種不同失敗，各自需要不同修法
- 挑模型時保留實際失敗案例，比單一準確率數字更能判斷適不適合任務

You don’t need: An engineering background. To train a foundation model. All you need is to understand how AI products work, where they fail, and how to make decisions about quality, cost, and reliability. But it’s easier said than done, especially when nearly all the AI PMs I know came from traditional PM backgrounds. Let’s address one of the top misconceptions among AI PM aspirants: Yes, your job as an AI PM requires you to build, ship, and evaluate AI products. But all of this only matters when: You build an AI product that should be built. You don’t build an AI product to solve a problem that could easily be solved manually. Problems come first. AI solutions later. This is product decision-making in a nutshell, and this is what AI PMs get hired for. You’re not paid to use AI. You’re paid to solve a problem that can be effectively and efficiently solved with an AI product that offers a strategic business advantage. The eight skills, in the order this guide teaches them To understand all 8 skills in depth, we first need a problem that we’ll solve using each skill. This will also show you why each skill is a non-negotiable part of an AI PM’s toolkit. The problem: A customer support team spends hours answering questions using company documentation. The goal: An AI product that helps the team respond faster. 1.1 Product sense and AI UX One morning at OpenAI I showed up to work, and one of the engineers on the Codex team said, “Look, I built the browser in this. What do you think?” He’d built it because frontend iteration was really annoying. I said, “That’s awesome. We should definitely figure out how to ship this.” His build became our starting point: an actual branch in the product that we could develop off of. We could build things almost instantly, so a lot of our time went into looking at solutions and deciding whether we actually wanted to ship them. It’s literally not a question of what is possible. Everything is possible. It’s just: what should we do? Having something working just lets that conversation happen with evidence on the screen. One of the biggest mistakes aspiring AI product managers make is skipping that question and jumping straight into solutions. They hear about a customer problem and immediately start thinking about which AI model, agent, or framework to use. But great AI PMs start with a different question: What problem are we actually trying to solve, and what would success look like? This requires two critical skills: Product Sense: Understanding your users, identifying their real problems, and deciding what is worth building. AI UX (User Experience): Designing how people interact with AI, verify its outputs, handle mistakes, and stay in control. Let’s look at an example. The problem: Your customer support agents spend four hours every day answering questions about refunds, shipping, and product policies. An inexperienced AI PM might immediately propose an AI chatbot to automate customer responses. A strong AI PM would first talk to support agents and discover that their biggest bottleneck isn’t writing responses. It’s finding the correct policies scattered across internal documents. That insight changes the solution. Instead of a fully autonomous chatbot, you build an AI support copilot that: Finds the relevant policy in company documentation. Drafts a response with citations. Lets agents verify, edit, and approve the answer before sending it. But building the product isn’t enough. You also need to define what success looks like. Here are four measurable targets you might set: Time spent answering policy questions: 4 hours/day → 2 hours/day Average resolution time: 8 minutes → 4 minutes Responses with material policy errors: 5% → Below 1% Responses sent without substantial edits: To be measured → 80% Illustrative targets; actual baselines and targets should be validated with the support team. Your goal: Reduce the time agents spend answering policy questions by 50% without compromising accuracy. 📌 Don’t start with what AI can build. Start with the customer problem, design the right experience, and define measurable success. Only then should you decide which AI model can deliver it. One habit that helps me build it: I keep my own Codex setup pretty light on custom skills (the reusable instruction files Codex can load). We built a ton of interesting plugins internally at OpenAI, but I find it really useful to see where Codex falls over when it doesn’t have anything specific. Test your models and watch how they behave on five things: Tokens: The units the model processes: words, parts of words, and punctuation. Token usage drives cost and how much information the model can handle. Context window: How much information the model can process in one request: the user’s query, instructions, retrieved documents, and sometimes the conversation history. A large context window helps with information-heavy tasks, but it doesn’t mean the model will use all of that information correctly. Tool calling: The model’s ability to call external actions or sources, such as searching a knowledge base or looking up an order. The application runs the tool and returns the result to the model. Latency: How long the system takes to respond. A more accurate model can take longer to answer. Inference cost: What it costs to run the model. It varies with the model, the number of tokens processed, and how many times the model is called. More terms: the AI Product Manager Glossary . For our support copilot, say you have three models to test with 50 customer questions: Model 1 is quick and cheap to run, but it frequently misses key information. Model 2 is more accurate, but slower and more expensive. Model 3 is good at simple questions, but struggles when it has to combine information across multiple documents. Now you have to decide: Do you use the most accurate model for every question? Do you use the cheaper model for simple questions? Is the extra quality worth the cost and response time? This is a product trade-off! For your three-model comparison, keep the actual failures alongside the scores. Was a policy exception missed? Did the model combine two documents incorrectly? Did it use a tool when it should have asked a clarifying question? Those examples will tell you more about which model fits the task than a single overall accuracy number. You can’t make that decision well without understanding prompting, context, and retrieval. 1.3 Prompting, context engineering, and RAG A great AI product manager understands where an AI answer comes from, why it might be wrong, and how to fix it. Three skills matter here: Prompting: Giving the model clear instructions about what to do, how to respond, and what constraints to follow. Context engineering : Ensuring the model has the right information, instructions, and tools available when completing a task. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) : Retrieving relevant information from external sources, such as company documents, and providing it to the model as context. Let’s return to our support copilot. A customer asks: “Can I return a product after 30 days?” Your copilot searches the company’s documentation, retrieves the return policy, and generates an answer. But there’s a problem. The company allows returns within 30 days, yet the copilot confidently tells the customer they have 60 days. An inexperienced AI PM might simply tell the engineering team to improve the prompt. A strong AI PM investigates where the system failed. Each cause needs a different fix There are three possibilities: Retrieval failure: Did the system find the wrong information? Perhaps it retrieved an outdated policy that allowed returns within 60 days. The fix might involve improving document indexing, retrieval ranking, or filtering outdated policies. Context failure: Did the correct information reach the model? Perhaps the system retrieved the right document but excluded the paragraph specifying the 30-day limit. The fix might involve changing how documents are divided into chunks or how retrieved information is assembled into the model’s context. Generation failure: Did the model misinterpret the correct information? Perhaps the model received the correct 30-day policy but still generated an incorrect answer. Now you might investigate the prompt, conflicting instructions, or the model’s ability to follow the policy. The same incorrect answer can have three completely different root causes. And each requires a different solution. That’s why learning RAG means understanding the entire information flow well enough to diagnose failures systematically. When Codex doesn’t do something correctly, it’s easy to just say, “Great, I’ll do it myself.” I try to ask what was missing instead. Probably I had some context or information that Codex didn’t have. Sometimes the missing piece is a document. Sometimes it’s access to a tool. Sometimes it’s a constraint I never explained because it felt obvious to me. Then the job is figuring out how to give it that context, so it has it moving forward.
