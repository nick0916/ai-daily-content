I am starting up a new project this year. To continue my work on digital wellness, I have committed to reflecting more about my personal experiences working in the AI Education space for the past four years. This new project, The Historical Present, invites readers to compose their own reflections and share them on my Substack. See submission guidelines here. Use this interest form to contact me. I will get back to you shortly after I receive your form. Last week I sat down to write about digital wellness and ended up writing a chapter in a much larger history. The writing process carried me back to the spring of 2023. AI hysteria, AI bans, AI detectors, student plagiarism and exploration. I honestly didn&#8217;t know what to think at the time. But after a summer of reflection and writing, I decided to lean into the change in search of educational practices that could hold up in the face of AI disruption. By the time I finished my draft, I had sketched out something resembling the arc of my experience over the past four years, a narrative that a number of my readers found extremely valuable. This reader-response prompted me to think more deeply about just how many stories are out there waiting to be shared. Consider this article an invitation to share your story on my Substack. Subscribe now My friend and colleague, Terry Underwood, remarked to me a few years ago that the record of personal experience becomes increasingly important in the time of propulsive, unyielding change. And if we are being honest, the rapid advancement of technology and attendant narratives have a tendency to erase or elide individual experiences. What use is personal narrative when technology is attempting to redistribute the boundaries between the individual and the collective, the human and the machine? But for me, questions like these underline the necessity of personal narratives of change. In the face of rising homogenization, the loss of individual voice and agency, and increasing panic and uncertainty about the future, we need personal narrative to scale down the hype, to contextualize the impact, and to create a history of the technology as we experience it. Long-time readers know that my PhD focused on rhetoric, narrative, and ethics. My dissertation explored how contemporary authors used modernist and postmodernist literacy techniques to explore ethical complexities that resist easy solutions. In other words, these authors, representative of a broader movement in contemporary literature, used narrative to advance a deliberately contextual and pluralistic vision of ethical engagement with the world. I am certain that if we work together in light of some simple guidelines, we can advance an equally diverse, compelling, and pluralistic vision of what it means to live, teach, and learn in the era of gen AI. The Historical Present I am calling this project The Historical Present and understand it (at least in this moment) as a serial account of generative AI in schools told by the people who lived through it, beginning in the fall of 2022. As an undergraduate, I studied the classics. I loved the nuanced differentiation between tenses in Latin and ancient Greek: the imperfect, the perfect, the aorist, the pluperfect, the future perfect. Inspired by the linguistics of &#201;mile Benveniste and the short fiction of Jorge Luis Borges, I wondered to what extent the structures of language shape our cognition and experience of the world. For instance, would Anglophone culture and history look different if English had a fully developed middle voice and verb system? This fascination carried over into my graduate work, where I examined a parallel process at the level of narrative and rhetoric. I ultimately concluded that there is an intrinsic porousness to experience, a quality that resists social, linguistic, and narrative structures. Even so, these structures exert great influence over our lived experience. As I studied literature, I gravitated toward narratives that used tense, voice, and different modes of narration to create distinctive experiences of the narrative world. I became fascinated by second-person singular and plural narratives, iterative narration, shifts between the narrating and the narrated I, and strategic uses of the historical present. For those unfamiliar with the historical present, it is a storyteller&#8217;s tense that punctuates past action with the present tense. Caesar and Livy used it to pull their readers into the past action. We use it at dinner without thinking: So I&#8217;m reading the third paper, and I recognize the sentence. The linguists Nessa Wolfson and Deborah Schiffrin, studying everyday American storytellers, found that speakers slip into the present to provide emphasis. More intriguingly, the historical present allows speakers and listeners to occupy past action with limited foresight. Hilary Mantel wrote Wolf Hall in this tense so readers would stand behind Thomas Cromwell&#8217;s eyes. &#8220;He can&#8217;t have hindsight,&#8221; she told NPR. Quite literally, it is the tense in which a speaker or writer evokes the precarity of a lived experience that cannot know what is about to happen. I use the historical present to describe this project as an invitation to immerse ourselves in our past experiences, to value their contingency, and to resist the tendency to overwrite the complexity of the past in terms of a driving telos and in response to a particular outcome. Writers do not need to literally use the historical present in their submissions. Here, as in Benveniste and Borges, the historical present is a concept, a worldview, a mode of engagement with our past and, indirectly, our present. What I Am Looking For Current discourse about AI in education, on Substack and in other media outlets, is dominated by two questions: What do we do now? What is coming next? Like all attempts at prophecy, the answers rarely match the full complexity of the moment, a complexity I have tried to preserve in countless posts of the past several years. The larger context enacts a kind of never-ending competition to get things right about what is coming next and to influence what is coming next with the most persuasive narrative about what should happen. Know that I am not giving up on this vital work of looking forward. Even as I write about my past, I am unavoidably thinking about how that record might be useful for teachers and students on the ground. And yet, the intensity of antagonism and emotional panic in current discourse strikes me as a call to engage in a different kind of intellectual and emotional labor, work that can run parallel to more practical explorations of AI tool implementation, classroom management, and policy development. To return to Terry&#8217;s recommendation, if we don&#8217;t write this history as it is unfolding, someone else will, with very different motives. I see this process already playing out in popular publishing, where authors&#8217; personal vendettas against certain corporations, or a priori commitments about what technology is and does, erase and elide the individual experiences that would complicate the simplistic narrative arcs of enthusiast and doomist histories of AI. But together we can braid tougher a different story, one at once plural and particular, told from classrooms and in the strange middle voice of reflective engagement. As my friend and colleague Lincoln Smith recently remarked to me: no single account of AI education will settle the matter of past significance, present orientation, and future direction.. Braided together, dozens of accounts can hold together the contradictions that a single author with a thesis to defend is tempted to smooth away. The teacher who banned ChatGPT and the teacher who built a course around it may turn out to be the same person, and both stories need to become part of our recollection and record. So here is what I am looking for. Look back across the arc of the past few years and find the moments where something changed dramatically: the week you gave up or gave in to gen AI, the policy you wrote and later put on the shelf, the conversation with a student or a colleague shifted the ground beneath your feet. Tell me what you believed at the time and what the experience taught you, and let the larger impact emerge gradually through the narrative. Step back into the moment, in the historical present if you like, and write from inside the uncertainty that shrouded the future. A little vulnerability goes a long way. Admitting what went wrong is often the hardest but most freeing things to write down. On a side note, It is also the one your reader will remember the most. I will read every submission myself and work through the edit with each contributor. Pieces should run between 1,000 and 1,200 words, and the full submission guidelines are here . Submit an idea for a submission using this Google form . I will get back to your shortly after you fill out the form. I am excited to hear from you. If you know of someone who is already engaged in this work and is looking for an outlet, share this post and the submission guidelines. I am excited to see where this project goes. In this context, not knowing what will happen next is a real source of inspiration and excitement.
