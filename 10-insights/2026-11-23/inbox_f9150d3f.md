## Stop asking users what they want

訪談使用者時不要問他們想要什麼，而要問他們實際做過什麼、為什麼。

**Why** 使用者只會說出自以為想要的東西，不一定知道真正的需求；在 AI 寫大部分程式的時代，產品工程師更該花時間理解真實問題。

**How** 以真實行為切入（「上次使用是什麼時候？」）、追問情緒字眼與模糊詞、把功能請求還原成問題、用排序與價格階梯取代「你想要嗎？」。

**What** PostHog 根據數千小時的真實使用者訪談整理出問題清單，分為設定脈絡、了解實際行為、釐清真意、了解真正需求、評估重要性與收尾。範例包括：問「你說的 some 大概是百分之幾？」（得到 5–10%）、PMF 問題「若不能再用會有何感受」、價格梯度問題（太貴／值得討論／划算／便宜到可疑），以及結尾的「還有什麼我該問的？」。

### 重點
- 避免問「你覺得這產品如何？」「你想要什麼功能？」「你會想要這個嗎？」，改問上次使用經過、此功能解決什麼問題、對營收或留存的影響
- 遇到模糊詞要量化（「some」「most」→「10% 還是 90%？」），遇到 hate、love、frustrating 等強烈情緒字眼一定追問
- 用結構化價格階梯與同一措辭的 1–10 評分，讓不同受訪者的回答可比較

If you ask users what they want, they will tell you what they think they want. They don’t know what they actually need because they haven’t spent as much time thinking about it. That’s your job. In an age when AI is writing most of the code , product engineers should spend more time talking to users to understand their real problems, and how to solve them. We’ve researched thousands of hours of real interviews with PostHog users to find the questions that get real, useful intel on what to build. Note: Examples are based on real interviews. We’ve removed all identifiable info, and modified details that could be used to identify participants. Setting the context I noticed you started doing X more recently, what drove that change? Ask specific questions about their account, like unusual growth trends or churn signals. A user is more likely to engage if it’s obvious you’ve arrived prepared, too. Interviewer: I noticed your error log volume dropped from about 500k to about 100k. I’m just curious, what drove that change? Can you tell me more about your role at the company / your background? The answer can completely change what you ask and how you interpret their answers. The below example indicates the user is a generalist with a broad brief. Interviewer: In your org, is there a data team or is it just you? User: I’m the whole data team here. Even though it’s, like, 100 people, I’m doing everything. What does a typical day look like for you? This helps you get a sense of their routine and where your product fits into it before you narrow in on your topic. You can follow-up with questions like “When do you use our product?” What do they actually do? Can you tell me about the last time you used this? This gets to the truth. Otherwise, they'll give you a quick, general answer that feels true but doesn't match what actually happened. User: Just 2 hours ago, we got this ticket saying, “I can’t even log in,” which gave me a heart attack. It turned out to be a problem with their computer, not our product. What do you typically do right before and after using X? Walking through the sequence often reveals what you should build next. User: I was trying to understand how many people are visiting the site, and from where. We do a lot of grassroots posting on Reddit, a Twitter presence, bit of TikTok, but we don’t know which one’s actually working. DON’T ASK... "What do you think about this product?" It invites vague answers. Even though this response was positive, it didn't tell us anything new or useful: User: I really like PostHog. The engineer who works with me uses it on his product and his side projects. We've both been very impressed with it. Ask them to think out loud while they try it out instead. You'll get specific reactions as they use it in real time. If you were to use X again, what would you do differently? This will reveal exactly where the experience went wrong and what else they've tried. User: My workaround would be to just throw it into the notebook and watch it from there, though I tried that and it makes the video smaller. What do they really mean? Can you tell me more about that? You'll probably ask a version of this 20 times in a single call. As the below example shows, it helps extract more meaning and context about why they want to do things. User: I wanna define the personas. Interviewer: Could you say more about that? User: Looking for our service is something that hits you like a bus, so you immediately need to act. Or, you’re like, a very concerned thinker and planning way ahead. We have internal terms for these two personas that I'd like to classify our data with and generate reports. Am I understanding this correctly? If they use vague words, repeat what you heard so they can correct, confirm, or clarify. Interviewer: So your team assumes something is wrong based on the spike in number of occurrences, is that right? User: It’s the spike in occurrences, but also which group of users are being affected. Could you give a rough estimate? You'd be surprised at what someone really means when they say words like "some" and "most." User: I’ve seen a lot of times that the recordings aren’t available. Interviewer: What percentage would you say that happens? Is it around 10, or more like 90? User: It’s much closer to around 5 or 10%. What made you say you hate that? Always follow up on strong emotional phrases like hate, love, annoyed, or frustrating. Non-verbal cues like a laugh or sigh are also worth asking about. User: It’s expensive without a good underlying reason, which is what really pisses people off. They quoted me $2,000/month for essentially what I had in Google Sheets. Like, this is my data. What's the reason for doing it this way, instead of another way? Offering a counterpoint helps diagnose the true reason behind their process. Interviewer: Is there a reason why you look at your dashboard versus just asking, "Agents, tell me how my conversion is doing today"? User: That way I don’t have to worry about an agent generating the chart differently each time. What do they actually want? DON’T ASK... "What would you change about this?" or "What features would you like to see?" Most users aren’t trained in product development. Don’t take feature requests at face value; look for the problems and reasons behind them. What problem would that solve for you? If a user does suggest a feature, ask them why they want it instead. This will reveal the underlying desire, so you can consider how to solve it. User: I'd want precise notification analysis, like how often each one gets clicked; what's the impact; charts and visualizers for that, and so on. Interviewer: What kinds of questions would that answer for you? User: Basically we want to see which day or time period would be best to send a notification to get users to visit our app more often. What impact would this feature have on your business? Check if the request is serious enough to impact their business, or just a nice-to-have. Would it improve some important metric, like revenue or retention, for them? Interviewer: Do you expect to see improvements on retention from doing that? User: Yeah. I mean, our retention's a little bit different than a typical SaaS product because there's a limited seasonal window when they use it. How much does this matter? DON’T ASK... "Would you want this?" or "Is this good?" Users will say yes to be nice, especially since there's nothing at stake for them. Which of those problems is the most important to you? Ask them to rank problems or ideas instead. They'll be more honest about what actually matters, and you can see how they compare. User: This one’s probably not that important, to be honest ... But there's another one that I didn’t mention yet that's probably just as important as the other two. How would you feel if you could no longer use this product? The good ol' product-market fit (PMF) question . Most people use this in quantitative surveys, but in an interview, focus on why they'd be disappointed, or not. Who on your team would most likely use this? Ideally, people describe themselves when answering this variant of the PMF question. Product Tours beta testers often described non-technical teammates outside our ICP, which helped us decide to sunset the product. User: It's mostly non-engineering people that are building our onboarding, so there needs to be a browser extension ... An easy way to do things on the fly with a UI. Use a structured pricing ladder question to understand perceived value. Here's what we say to get real answers without pressure: Interviewer: Nothing you say here would change your bill, this is really just about gut numbers. At what price is it so expensive you wouldn’t even consider it? ... expensive, but you would debate it with your co-founder? ... A good deal? ... So cheap that it’s suspicious? User: $500 is too much ... $200 is debatable ... $29 or 39 would be fine ... $19 is kind of a bargain, I'd think it's a scam. On a scale of 1–10, how easy was it to find what you needed? Two people can have different ideas of what "very difficult" means. Asking rating questions with the same wording each time helps you compare answers more objectively. Wrapping up Is there anything else I should have asked? What people bring up unprompted is usually an honest signal of what matters most to them. Interviewer: Is there anything else you wanted to bring up that's been on your mind? User: No, I talked quite a bit already ... Actually, I wanna emphasize again, it would be nice to have some agent feature where they sample 100 chats per day and give me a static report of insights. Is there anyone else you think I should talk to? This can get you warm intros for more interviews. To reach someone with more decision-making power, try "Who owns the budget for this?" or "Where does the money come from?" Can I follow up with you once we've built something? People are often happier to chat again than you'd expect. One of our engineers got a reply within three minutes after sending follow-up mockups over Slack.
