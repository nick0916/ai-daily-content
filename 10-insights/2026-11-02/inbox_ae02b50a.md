## Analytics has changed. Here's what to do about it

AI 時代的數據分析要讓 AI 主動找問題、把產品與營收數據接在一起、把 agent 當使用者，並先定好統一定義。

**Why** 產品資料量變大、成本變高，人看不完（中位數公司每月錄 9 小時 session replay，99.8% 沒被打開）；AI 又有實質邊際成本，且人人可自助提問，答案卻不一定對（Anthropic 內部 1/20 的分析查詢有誤）。

**How** 給 AI agent 明確的 skill 與輸出格式而非放任；把營收、訂閱、AI 觀測資料與產品資料放同一處；用 source 屬性、MCP tool call 事件與 log 區分 agent 與人的行為；用 semantic layer 與 skill 固定事件定義與排除規則。

**What** PostHog 作者 Ian Vanagas 列出四點做法。其 Scouts 之一的 404 fixer 兩個月改了 377 個檔案，每週省下 3000+ 次壞連結請求；其用戶中約 50% 的 dashboard、18% 的 experiment 由 AI 建立，1/5 新 org 經由 agent 註冊。三個 AI 工具回答「上個月 MRR」得到三種查詢與答案，顯示共享且經驗證的上下文有其必要。

### 重點
- Scouts 主動式分析 agent 必須搭配 skill 與固定輸出格式；404 fixer 兩個月改 377 檔、每週少 3000+ 次壞連結請求；Dashboard maintainer 發現 3,401 筆未被任何 insight 涵蓋的 skipped evaluations
- 產品數據與營收數據合併才看得到：Experiments 留存 86.6% vs 僅用 flags 78.4%；250 人以上 org 只有 15% 付費；Replay Vision 營收 15% 來自不為 Replays 付費的 org
- agent 分析易出錯：Claude、Cursor、PostHog AI 對同一 MRR 問題給出三種查詢；用錯欄位（status_code vs proxy_status_code）導致 0% vs 1% 錯誤率；解法是 semantic layer 加 skill 固定定義、排除規則與改名事件

In the last few years, analytics has changed as much as coding has, but with nearly none of the fanfare. Products now take in more data, scale faster, and have higher costs. Doing analytics the old way will leave you overwhelmed and underinformed. To help you update your knowledge, here are the things you need to know about how analytics has changed, and the best practices for doing analytics now. 1. Let AI make your analytics proactive Humans can’t handle all the analytics data coming their way. For example, the median company records 9 hours of session replays per month , which leads to 99.8% of session recordings going unopened. AI and automations might do a worse job than humans, but they can do analytics more proactively. This can squeeze more value out of your analytics data that currently goes unused and wasted. Getting AI to do proactive analytics requires giving it direction . It’s not enough to just let AI loose on your analytics data and say “find some fixes.” When given free rein over all your data, agents often get lost or confused. Our proactive analytics agents (we call them Scouts ) always have skills to guide them and a clear output format. Examples include: 404 fixer. The skill tells it to “keep posthog.com free of dead URLs. Each run: find 404s that real people actually hit, work out why the URL is dead, and file a report carrying the exact fix so a draft PR opens against posthog.com .” In the 2 months it has run, it has changed 377 files and saved 3000+ broken link requests per week. Customers struggle scanner. Turns “the last 24 hours of observations from one Replay Vision scanner into one Slack message for a customer success manager. The scanner judges one recording at a time. Your job is the cross-session work: refresh the scanner’s account scope, group its findings by account, and write the digest.” Has found query failures as well as customers “repeatedly clicking the recording summary control with no visible output, then abandoning” that led to outreach to those specific customers. Dashboard maintainer. This has “four jobs: surface interesting changes, close what’s missing, maintain the dashboards, and discover dashboards to watch.” One finding was noticing 3,401 skipped evaluations that no insight covered. It built a new chart breaking down the reason that was then added to their main dashboard. 2. Connect your product and business analytics Pre-AI, you could get away with focusing on product analytics . More users and usage was good because it didn’t cost you anything (or cost very little). Just making your product good was good enough, worrying about the business could come later. This isn’t the case anymore. The primary reason for this is that AI costs a lot of money. Devs pay hundreds of dollars for coding agent subscriptions. AI-powered features can cost a lot. Building AI into your product means it’s no longer possible to scale to hundreds of thousands of users with your only expenses being a $15/m server and Stripe fees. Software doesn’t have zero marginal costs anymore. You’ll actually need to figure out what customers will pay for and how to charge them from the start. You can vibe code an app, but you can’t vibe code a business. AI helps here (again) by: Reducing silos. In the past, all your data would be in isolated platforms. Using either PostHog or a bunch of MCP servers enables you to have it all in one place. Reducing complexity. Querying and combining multiple different sources require complex joins and identification. AI can handle them for you. Anyone can do complicated, cross-source investigations now. With data like revenue , subscriptions, account status, ad spend , and AI observability in one place, you can figure out things like: Which features paying customers need, not just free users. Only 15% of orgs over 250 people using PostHog pay for it, a lower rate than with 100-249 people. Improving that rate would increase ARR by many millions. We only know this because usage, company size, and billing are joined. Which features bring in new customers. 15% of Replay Vision revenue is from orgs that don’t pay anything for Replays themselves. Which features retain customers. Experiments retain customers better than flags alone (86.6% vs 78.4%). The difference between an AI-powered feature costs and what you sell it for. Thousands of real AI sessions with cost inform our AI pricing, not just usage estimates. These are all things that product engineers at PostHog look at when deciding what to build, and it’s only possible because the business analytics data is connected to the product data. 3. Treat agents like a new set of users Analytics has not only expanded into new data sources, but whole new types of users: agents. Just like humans, being able to understand what agents are doing is critical to providing them a good experience. Agent analytics is a nascent field, but here’s some tools at your disposal: Tagging key events with an agent or human source. At PostHog, backend events like dashboard created or experiment created get a source property with values like web , mcp , slack , and cli . This is how we know 50% of dashboards and 18% of experiments are created using AI. MCP Analytics . Our SDK captures every tool call as an $mcp_tool_call . It provides clients, error rates, latency, and intent. Through this we’ve found many missing capabilities as well as broken ones, like agents telling us they couldn’t resolve or archive error tracking issues. Tracking AI crawlers and assistants through logs as they don’t run JavaScript. We forward logs from Vercel as $http_log events, and then classify them. This lets us see the requests AI crawlers, assistants, and search are making on our site. This matters because agent usage is different from humans and is growing. A third of organizations that query PostHog do it through agents. Agents create about half of objects in PostHog like insights, dashboards, and feature flags. 1 in 5 new orgs signs up are through an agent. It’s not implausible to think that agents might be the primary user of PostHog in the near future. The same might be true for you. 4. Create shared, verified context (before it’s made up) Analytics is mostly self-serve now. Anyone can ask anything and get plausible and confident answers. The problem with these answers is that they’re not always correct. Even Anthropic found that 1/20 internal analytics queries are wrong. This is because agents often... Use the wrong definition for a term like “sign ups” or “MRR.” We asked Claude, Cursor, and PostHog AI “what’s our MRR last month?” and got three different queries and answers. Do their own math and mess up the calculation. An MCP-heavy user told us experiment numbers didn’t match the UI because the agent was writing its own query. Use the wrong column or property. A question about AI crawler errors returned an 0% error rate because it used status_code rather than proxy_status_code (which shows a 1% error rate). You, your agents, your colleagues, and their agents will assume they are right and there’s no gatekeepers (like a data team) to say they’re wrong. This is why shared, verified context matters. It’s how you prevent these inaccuracies from happening. What does this look like? What are the events we actually care about? We have collected 14 events and 72 sign up related actions, but only two are “approved” through our semantic layer . Are they accurate? Replay activation is an event we care about, and we saw it falling over the last four months, when we realized it didn’t account for MCP users, so it needed rewriting. What does good look like? A scorecard says gross revenue retention above 80% is healthy for mature products and monthly logo churn below 2.5% is green. Codifying definitions and gotchas. Skills and our semantic layer is how we do this. For example, our revenue analysis skill mentions that “upcoming” invoices are full-period projections, not settled revenue and credited startups read $0 until credits run out. What to exclude. Almost every internal query excludes PostHog’s own project and events carry a was_impersonated property so support sessions don’t count as customer usage. Renamed or retired events. mcp_tool_call was replaced by $mcp_tool_call and tool_name became $mcp_tool_name in June. A lot of this isn’t new. Data teams have always maintained definitions. It’s just more important now that anyone can “do analytics” and that leads them to do it more. AI might make analytics easier, but there’s still work to do to set context so you, your agents, your colleagues, and their agents can “do analytics” well in ways that are specific to your team, needs, and goals.
