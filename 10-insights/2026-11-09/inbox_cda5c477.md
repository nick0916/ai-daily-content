## The Beer That Built Modern Medicine

把啤酒酵母純化成單一菌株，間接促成了現代胰島素與 GLP-1 藥物。

**Why** 發酵早於人類理解微生物，菌株混雜導致批次品質不穩，需要可預測的生產菌。

**How** Carlsberg 的 Emil Christian Hansen 於 1883 年以稀釋分離法培養單一酵母細胞，並將純種培養法免費分享給其他酒廠。

**What** Carlsberg 的 S.P.L. Sørensen 於 1909 年提出 pH 量表；Novo 於 1987 年以基因工程酵母生產人類胰島素；Novo 用 Saccharomyces cerevisiae 生產 semaglutide 前驅物，該藥於 2017 年以 Ozempic 獲美國核准，2021 年推出 Wegovy。

### 重點
- 1883 年 Hansen 以稀釋分離法得到純種拉格酵母，Carlsberg 同年開始用純種釀造並公開方法
- 1987 年 Novo 以基因工程 Saccharomyces cerevisiae 推出人類胰島素
- 2017 年 semaglutide 獲批為 Ozempic，2021 年 Wegovy 上市，生產仍依賴酵母發酵

Once you realize that the world we live in, has been ( often ) shaped by a simple object that has been around for more than 2000 years, you can’t help but marvel at it. One of my favourite author Steve Johnson in his book “How We Got To Now, Six Innovations That Made The Modern World, call this as “ The Hummingbird Effect”. This is another example of such effect, from beer to GLP. In 2018, archaeologists studying three ancient stone mortars from Raqefet Cave, near Mount Carmel (present-day Israel), they reported an unusual discovery. The mortars had the evidence of beer brewing, dating back nearly 13,000 years. The people who lived there (called as Natufians), had been collecting wild cereals, storing malted grains and producing a thick, porridge-like fermented drink, possibly for funeral feasts. All this happened before domesticated cereal farming appeared in the region. There is an old debate among archaeologists about whether humans began cultivating grain partly because they wanted a more dependable supply for brewing beer. The evidence remains inconclusive, although the Raqefet discovery lends the argument some weight. Whatever be the motivation, humans had discovered fermentation long before they understood the microorganisms responsible for it. Getting those organisms to behave predictably would occupy brewers for thousands of years, eventually drawing them into chemistry, microbiology and medicine. Beer before farming The earliest brewing methods were surprisingly sophisticated for something developed without scientific knowledge. Grains were soaked until they began to germinate, then crushed and mixed with water. Natural enzymes broke down starch into fermentable sugars, which wild yeasts converted into alcohol. However, different microorganisms produced different results, so a good batch could be followed by one that tasted unpleasant or spoiled entirely, even when the brewer followed the same process. Over time, brewers learnt to preserve a portion of a successful batch and add it to the next one. This practice, known as backslopping , helped maintain a relatively consistent population of microorganisms. Anyone who has made sourdough bread using an existing starter will recognise the method. It improved reliability, although brewers still had little understanding of what they were cultivating. By the nineteenth century, when European breweries were expanding into large commercial operations, such uncertainty had become increasingly expensive. In 1847, J.C. Jacobsen established the Carlsberg brewery in Copenhagen, having spent time in Bavaria studying its increasingly popular lager beers. He admired their clean taste and consistent quality and wanted to reproduce them in Denmark. By 1875, he started a research laboratory within Carlsberg, where scientists were encouraged to investigate fermentation, chemistry and whatever else might improve brewing. The following year, Jacobsen established the Carlsberg Foundation with an unusual provision: important discoveries from the laboratory were to be made public. Even competing breweries could benefit from research paid for by Carlsberg. The foundation would eventually support scientists working in fields far removed from brewing, including Niels Bohr, whose rather unusual relationship with the brewery we explored in The Quantum Pint . Carlsberg Laboratory, 1883 Louis Pasteur had already established that fermentation was caused by living microorganisms, but breweries continued to struggle with spoiled batches. Emil Christian Hansen , a scientist at Carlsberg, suspected that the yeast cultures used by brewers contained several different strains, some responsible for undesirable flavours and spoilage. He began diluting yeast suspensions until individual cells could be isolated, then cultivated new populations from those cells. By testing the resulting cultures, he could identify which yeasts behaved reliably. In November 1883, Carlsberg began brewing with a pure culture of lager yeast. Brewers could now begin each batch with a selected microorganism and expect much greater consistency. Jacobsen chose to distribute the culture and its cultivation methods freely to other breweries. Within a few years, pure-culture techniques had spread across European brewing, eventually reaching major American manufacturers. There is an interesting parallel with the history of glass, which we followed in The Material That Taught Us to See . Venetian glassmakers had spent generations learning to remove impurities so their glass could become clear enough for precise lenses. Hansen was dealing with a similar problem, except that his impurities were living organisms capable of ruining an entire batch of beer. In both cases, improving the material opened possibilities well beyond its original use. The Carlsberg laboratory continued to attract scientists working on problems unrelated to brewing. In 1909, S.P.L. Sørensen, who headed its chemistry department, introduced the pH scale while studying the behaviour of proteins. The familiar measurement of acidity, now used in medical testing, agriculture and industrial chemistry, emerged from research funded by a brewery. Carlsberg also became an important centre for studying enzymes and yeast physiology, developing expertise that travelled with researchers into other Danish industries. Insulin and the Danish connection In 1922, Danish physiologist August Krogh travelled to North America, where he learnt about the recently discovered treatment for diabetes using insulin. His wife, Marie, was a physician who had diabetes herself, and the couple recognised the importance of making the treatment available in Europe. Krogh secured permission to manufacture insulin in Scandinavia, and in 1923 helped establish Nordisk Insulinlaboratorium in Copenhagen. Early insulin manufacturing required extracting the hormone from the pancreases of slaughtered cattle and pigs. In 1925, two former Nordisk employees established a competing company called Novo, beginning a rivalry that would last until the businesses merged in 1989 to form Novo Nordisk. Novo discovered that insulin extraction also yielded useful enzymes. Trypsin, recovered from pancreatic material, could be sold to leather manufacturers for softening animal hides, while amylase found applications in textiles. When wartime shortages disrupted animal supplies, Novo began developing fermentation-based methods for producing enzymes using microorganisms. By 1952, the company was selling fermentation-produced amylase. Its industrial enzyme business subsequently expanded into detergents, where enzymes helped break down protein stains and improved washing performance at lower temperatures. During the 1970s, genetic engineering introduced another possibility. Scientists developed methods for inserting foreign genes into microorganisms, allowing them to manufacture proteins they would never naturally produce. In 1978, Genentech produced recombinant human insulin using genetically modified bacteria, and Eli Lilly introduced the resulting medicine commercially in 1982. Novo eventually pursued a different approach, using genetically engineered Saccharomyces cerevisiae , the yeast commonly associated with bread and ale. In 1987, it introduced human insulin manufactured using this method. The strain was different from Carlsberg’s original lager yeast, but Denmark’s accumulated expertise in yeast physiology and industrial fermentation had proved valuable. Novo recruited scientists with experience at Carlsberg, drawing on knowledge that had originally developed around brewing. Meanwhile, diabetes researchers had been investigating another biological process. They discovered that the gut released hormones that influenced insulin secretion after food was consumed. One of these, GLP-1, appeared especially promising because it helped regulate blood sugar and appetite. Unfortunately, natural GLP-1 survived in the bloodstream for only a few minutes, making it difficult to use as a practical medicine. During the 1990s, researchers at Novo Nordisk began modifying GLP-1 to extend its activity. They experimented with attaching fatty-acid chains that would help the molecule bind to albumin, a protein circulating in the bloodstream, slowing its removal. After years of work, they developed semaglutide, a molecule that remained active long enough for once-weekly injections. In 2017, semaglutide received US approval as Ozempic for type 2 diabetes. A higher-dose formulation, Wegovy, followed in 2021 for weight management. Both medicines emerged from decades of research into hormones and molecular biology, combined with manufacturing techniques refined through industrial biotechnology. The production process still involves yeast. Novo Nordisk uses genetically modified Saccharomyces cerevisiae to manufacture a semaglutide precursor inside large fermentation vessels. The precursor is recovered, purified, chemically modified and purified again before it becomes the finished medicine. A modern pharmaceutical fermenter is a long way from the stone mortars of Raqefet Cave, or even Hansen’s laboratory in Copenhagen. Yet the work begins with a familiar requirement: cultivating the right microorganism, under the right conditions, to produce a reliable result. The patient never encounters the yeast. By the time the medicine is injected, it has been removed, leaving behind the molecule it was taught to make.
