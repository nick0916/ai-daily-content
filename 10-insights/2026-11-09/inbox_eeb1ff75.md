## Fable 5.2 and Jagged AGI

作者實測 Claude Fable 5.2，認為它已「幾乎」達到 AGI，但仍有難以察覺的鋸齒狀弱點。

**Why** 作者想檢驗最新模型是否跨過 AGI 門檻；他的定義是「在電腦上能做的大多數智力任務上，和多數人類專家一樣好」，並明確排除實體世界、與人互動及完整工作。

**How** 在公開上線前，用單一提示詞給它不同領域的高難度任務：駭入硬體做成遊戲機、規劃創業、重建 Auden 的課程網站、做原創學術研究、建立 3D 傳送門世界。

**What** Fable 5.2 接在 Opus 5.5 之後；它讀程式碼為 Codex Micro 鍵盤寫出六款遊戲，並產出創業方案 Prebid Brief（AI 自估成功率 20%–40%）。它也做出 Auden 課程網站、182 頁的 LLC 規費研究（註冊量約 ±17%，但尚不可發表），以及超過 450 個世界的網站（約 25,000 次 agent 執行）。弱點在品味與判斷：第一版網站「準確卻無趣」，且它自己沒發現問題；結果也出現冗長與 Claudespeak 文風。

### 重點
- 作者將 Fable 5.2 稱為 Jagged AGI：不再是簡單錯誤或幻覺，而是品味、判斷、是否自我察覺問題的落差
- 原創研究：Colorado LLC 規費由 $50 降到 $1，新註冊約增 17%；英國由 £12 升到 £50，約減 17%；論文 182 頁，且沒有真的預先註冊

Before getting started, I wanted to thank my readers for making my new book, Co-Existence, a New York Times bestseller two weeks in a row. If you haven’t read it, you can get a copy here . I have had the chance to use the new Claude Fable 5.2 quite a bit before its public launch 1 , and it is very impressive, following on the already excellent Opus 5.5. AI has gotten so good, in fact, that it is worth asking if Anthropic’s newest models (and the inevitable competing models OpenAI and Google will soon release) cross the boundary into being an Artificial General Intelligence? AGI is a much-debated term with a lot of potential definitions. For this post, I’ll go with: “an AI as good as most human experts at most intellectual tasks you can do with a computer.” This explicitly excludes a lot of human work, including interacting with the physical world and other people, as well as most actual jobs, where “using a computer” is just one task among many. Still, even with these caveats, building a limited form of AGI has long been the goal of AI developers. So as not to keep you in suspense, I think the answer is that this is “almost” AGI, or what I have called Jagged AGI. Even within my limited definition, there are still important areas in many tasks where AI is jagged enough to fall well behind human capability. But the jaggedness is no longer about simple errors or hallucinations, which makes it harder to describe. So I want to try to do that in this post by showing you what Fable can do and where the gaps are. To get started I asked the new model: “I write OneUsefulThing among other stuff, I need you to create the most impressive thing I can share with people when you launch. It needs to be embeddable in a post and i can preface it however you like. it needs to have a visceral impact on people to show what you can do and the range of capabilities. It should grab them quickly. Be amazingly clever about this.” Fable asked me not to reveal anything until you had watched its video to the end. This was in one shot, with no feedback from me beyond the prompt. There is creativity here (including in the twist ending) and judgment and playfulness and cleverness. This is, I think, at the level of a human expert trying to make a movie with code alone, the sort of performance I would once have associated with AGI. The “G” in AGI Code and video creation have been areas where AI development has focused, so is Fable 5.2 really a General Intelligence? To test that, I gave the AI a variety of hard challenges in different domains: Hack some hardware from a single prompt: I have a tiny USB keyboard with a few light-up keys on my desk called a Codex Micro (ironically built to work with ChatGPT), and I asked Fable to turn it into a game console. There are no public instructions for this (as far as I can tell), so it read the code of the two apps that control the keyboard, worked out how to set the color of each key and read the knob and joystick, and wrote six games, including Simon and a safecracking game you play with the knob. Fable instantly understood what I wanted, designed fun and playable games that fit the very limited capabilities of the keyboard, and figured out how to hack it together without any help on the very first try. Launch a business : Now we move past domains like coding to the messier world of business. I told Fable to “launch a business to make me money, I want at least $20,000 in a year. it should take no more than 20 minutes a month from me, you do all the rest.” In response, Fable screened 22 ideas and came up with Prebid Brief, a service that would tell equipment sales reps which park and water projects were being funded or designed, even before they went up to bid. It read public agendas from 201 city and county governments to produce its first issues. It also built files of what cities already own, how old it is, and what they paid. You can see what it came up with here , including its marketing plan and proposed pricing. Fable started to set up scheduled tasks to build the business and asked if it could start sending public-records requests to local governments. At that point, I asked it to just put together a demo, rather than launching the idea. I am an entrepreneurship professor, so I evaluate business plans all the time. I would have been impressed if this came from a team of my MBA students at the end of a semester. It is plausible and well thought out and could work, though it is in no way a guaranteed money-maker (the AI’s own guess was a 20%-40% chance of success). I stopped the experiment before finding out whether it could actually run the business, because I worried that it would take money and fail to provide something of quality in return. The ongoing infrastructure it seemed to assume would be easy to create was not yet in place, and I don’t know how well it would handle the many moving pieces required for sales and marketing. However, I suspect that, with a few hours of human work a week to fill the gap, I could make a real attempt at this business. So if AGI means an AI consistently running a business independently, we are likely not there yet. Then again, I would have some questions before trusting a human who promised to launch a business and make me money while requiring twenty minutes a month of my attention. Reconstruct the hardest humanities class in history: In 1941, the poet W. H. Auden taught a course at the University of Michigan whose syllabus the internet has since called one of the hardest ever. When he announced the final exam, a committee of his students declared it “impossible and inhumane .” Fate and the Individual in European Literature” had 6,000 pages of reading, punishing assignments, and a requirement to memorize long poems and translate others. Much of what we know about the class is scattered among surviving student notes and Auden’s letters. I asked Claude to recreate it in a fun and engaging way that teaches you about poetry rather than punishing you. It made Fate and the Individual . The site has a piece for each of the 35 major works on Auden’s list, as well as what he wrote on the blackboard, the records he played, and the work he asked for. It also read through all of Auden’s published scholarship and annotated the poems with what he had written about them in other sources. Claude did the research, design, writing and code, had critic agents go over every word and fact-checkers compare the claims with the sources, and put the open source code on Github here (and also made this teaser video). The results are really interesting if you love literature, and I think they would have taken months of human work to put together, given the mix of scholarship, web design, and literary analysis. Cross-checking myself and using other advanced AIs found no major errors in its work. But there were still elements of jaggedness around taste and judgment. Fable’s first version was accurate and joyless: a fifteen-week course website with tests. I told it it created “a brutal slog through the most boring webpages” and told it to make something beautiful. Its first try at that created a kiddie game where you had to ring bells, so I had to give more feedback. When I asked it to explain why a poem’s rhythm or word choice mattered, it wrote confident readings without considering what Auden said about them. In each case the fix took the AI minutes, but it did not notice any of these problems itself. So is this a difference in taste or a failure from me to specify what I want or a sign of AI weakness? Sometimes it is hard to tell. Do original academic research: I asked Fable to find an unsolved empirical problem in entrepreneurship research, find the data, preregister its hypothesis and methods, write the paper, and have other agents critique it. Soon, I had “The One-Dollar Company,” which you can read here . The analysis estimated that when Colorado cut its LLC filing fee from $50 to $1, new company registrations rose about 17 percent, and when the UK raised its fee from £12 to £50, they fell about 17 percent. Those changes in registrations were not matched by comparable changes in several later records of business activity, suggesting the new businesses were not high quality. Its a neat result, and Fable picked the question, found the public data and natural experiments, and wrote the analysis plan and code. It did not actually preregister the study: the plans stayed inside the project, and some were written after earlier results. Unlike many of the AI papers I have tried, this tackles an important topic with a clever research design. On a first read, it is ahead of what I would expect from second-year PhD students. It is also not publishable as it is. It runs 182 pages, because every critique got a new analysis and nothing was ever cut (this is a theme we will come back to soon). I also had GPT-6 Pro (the best model for one-shot reviews of complex academic work) act as a peer reviewer, and it twice found issues that needed to be resolved. Those revisions led to more convoluted writing and “Claudespeak” being inserted into the text, which required yet more rounds of changes. As it stands, I suspect the current paper is close to a real contribution to the literature. This would have taken months of human work, but I spent less than an hour of my time to get it to this stage. However, that leaves out a substantial part of the work of research: no human has fully checked the results, including me. AI can now produce research faster than anyone can read it, creating a crisis in a lot of academia. A second project went more smoothly. I asked Fable to replicate research claiming that stories follow a few basic emotional arcs, and then to find out whether anything about plot is universal. It read 1,186 stories from around the world and built a lovely site on the nature of stories concluding that they “share no one shape or plot, but most are built from the same few parts, and the most typical story is a Hawaiian folk tale about a wizard with a magic banana.” The main problem I encountered was the same Claudespeak, which was fixed with a couple of reminders from me. Through the Portal In one of my favorite experiments, I gave Claude a one-paragraph description of an endless, hands-off journey through 3D worlds linked by portals, and told it to “do this and make it awe inspiring.” Twenty hours later there were 204 worlds on a live website . There are now over 450, including a Hall of a Million Mirrors , a series from Calvino’s Invisible Cities , the Wonders of the Ancient World and a scene 40 seconds before the asteroid that killed the dinosaurs hits . There are worlds modeled after M.C. Escher , cave paintings , Hokusai’s Great Wave , the Bayeux Tapestry , a pop-up book and a child’s crayon drawing . Every one is drawn live by code, with no pre-made 3D models or image files behind it. My prompt asked for teams of artist agents, coding agents and evaluators. By the tenth day there was a lead AI agent and 28 teams of other agents building in parallel. Claude estimated that the project had involved 25,000 agent runs, though the vast majority did a single task like judge an image (I am still glad I wasn’t being billed for token use during my early trial). I came up with a list of places (wilderness and oceans, cities and interiors, books, painters, AI in-jokes) and some feature requests (a mode to reduce motion for people who felt seasick, a gallery of images to select). But everything else was Claude’s, including the creative work of the worlds. And they were really creative. In The Valley Unzips , the hills are sewn from denim and a zipper a mile long is coming undone. Bouncy Castle Town is made of nylon houses that stand only while their fans keep running, and Keep Refrigerated is the inside of a refrigerator as a fruit fly sees it when the door opens.
