Hey folks 👋 This week, Anthropic launched its new frontier model, Fable 5.2 , which it described as capable of tackling the most daunting of challenges. Fable 5.2 is the latest in a growing line of frontier models, the most capable tier of AI model. Others include OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra, Google’s Gemini 4 Argon and the brand-new Gemini 4 Carbon, and xAI’s Grok 4.7. A question I keep getting asked more and more by L&D folks is this: which tasks should I use Frontier models for? What people are really asking here is: when are frontier models worth the cash, and when will smaller, cheaper models do instead? I’ve attempted to answer this question by running some rapid tests of Fable 5.2 (the frontier model) and the cheaper Sonnet 5.5. I tested both on tasks across our end-to-end workflow, from “find the real problem” to “did the training work?”, using the same files and the same prompts. I then had raters blind score the outputs against a 24-competency instructional design standard ( more on the standard in this post ) without knowing which model wrote what. In this post, I share what I learned so far. Spoiler : I found five L&D tasks where a frontier model is worth the credits, and many many more which just aren’t. Let’s dive in! Frontier Models 101 First, a quick spin through what a frontier model is, and how it differs from other AI models. Two things matter most here: 1. Frontier models are the most powerful. They’re built to work through long, complicated problems step by step, hold a lot of detail at once, and make fewer mistakes along the way. Below them sit the mid-tier models, like Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol: faster, far cheaper, and good enough for most everyday work. Below those are the small models, like Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6 Luna: very fast, very cheap, and best at simple, repetitive jobs. 2. Frontier models are also the priciest. AI companies charge by how much text a model reads and writes. A typical task in my tests meant giving the model a brief and a handful of files, about the length of a short report, and getting back a few pages of analysis. On Fable 5.2, that task cost about 25p (30 cents). On a mid-tier model like Claude Sonnet 5.5, it cost about 6p (8 cents). On a small model like Claude Haiku 5.5, it cost less than half a penny. That sounds like loose change, but it adds up fast. A hundred tasks a week comes to about £25 on Fable 5.2, £6 on Sonnet and 30p on Haiku. And if you’re on a subscription, you won’t see a bill at all. You’ll just hit your usage limit sooner. So one of the most useful AI skills right now isn’t writing a better prompt. It’s knowing which model to use for which task, and when a cheaper one will do just as well. To find out where that line sits in L&D, I put Fable 5.2 and a mid-tier model through the same tasks across our workflow. Here’s what I found. Five Tasks Where Fable 5.2 Is Worth the Investment 1. Diagnosing a Problem from Messy Evidence Someone asks for training, and you have to work out whether the problem they describe is really a skills gap, usually from a pile of logs, reports and comments nobody has had time to read. AI can read all of it in minutes and test each explanation against the evidence, which is the slow part of the job. Do this: give the model the request and the raw evidence behind it (the error log, the release notes, the survey comments) and keep your own diagnosis to yourself. In my test, a Head of Claims asked for a mandatory module for all 140 handlers because “people have got sloppy”. Both models traced the errors to a software release, not to the staff. Fable 5.2 scored 24 out of 25. Sonnet 5.5 scored 20. The four points were in the detail. There was a smaller, real training gap in the data, and it sat with new starters. Sonnet spotted the gap but never said who had it, so the training it proposed had no named audience. Try this: “Here is a request from a stakeholder and the evidence behind it. Before recommending anything, tell me what the evidence says is causing the problem, who it affects, and what a fix would still leave unsolved. Don’t build anything yet.” What I uploaded: six files from my fictional insurer. The Head of Claims’ email asking for the module, three months of the quality team’s error log (447 rows), six months of monthly error summaries, the release notes for an update to the claims system, staff survey comments and a team information sheet. Fable 5.2 performs better than non-frontier models on root cause analysis and problem definition. Why it’s worth the credits: both models found the cause, but only Fable 5.2 worked out who it affected. That’s the difference between a diagnosis and a plan: Sonnet proposed training with no named audience, while Fable pointed it at the new starters, where the real gap was. It also got every figure right. Compared with a person, it reads all 447 rows and checks each explanation against them in minutes. Most of us, on a deadline, read the summary and go with the stakeholder’s story. Everything you build rests on the diagnosis, so this is where accuracy matters most. 2. Deciding Whether Training Is the Answer Before anything gets designed, someone has to decide whether training is the right fix at all, or whether the real problem is a process, a tool or a manager. A model can weigh the request against the data and your own funding policy and set out the case either way. Do this: attach your policy on when you fund training, and ask the model to quote the clause. A director wanted to buy a de-escalation course for all 140 handlers. The data said 40% of complaints were about late call-backs, 13% were about attitude, and five handlers accounted for more than half of those. Fable’s first answer treated these as separate problems needing separate fixes. Sonnet’s first answer set out a plan to put all 140 through the course by 18 December. Then I added one file: a written policy on learning investment. Both models refused the purchase, and both held when the director pushed. Try this: “A manager wants to buy a training course. Using the attached data and our policy on when we fund training, tell me: (1) what is actually causing the problem; (2) whether this course would fix it, yes or no, and why; (3) whether the request meets our policy, quoting the clause. Then give your recommendation in one paragraph.” What I uploaded: seven files. The director’s email asking for the course, the quarter’s complaints, call-quality scores for every handler, the vendor’s one-page pitch, customer satisfaction comments, an information sheet on the claims team, and our policy on when we fund training. Why it’s worth the credits: Fable 5.2 didn’t take the request at face value. With no policy to guide it, it split the complaints by cause and saw that late call-backs, attitude and five handlers were three different problems with three different fixes. Sonnet planned to send all 140 people on the course. Separating causes like this is exactly what gets skipped when a director wants an answer by Friday. Once I attached our policy, the cheaper model got there too. So if you have a written policy, Sonnet can do this. If you don’t, pay for Fable. A course for 140 people that fixes 13% of the problem costs far more than the credits. 3. Checking Content Against Its Source More and more of the content we review wasn’t written by a person. It was drafted by AI, or by a subject expert working from slides that have drifted from the official procedure. Either way, the course has to follow the source: the procedure, the policy, the approved storyboard. Checking the two line by line is hours of work for a person and minutes for a model. Do this: attach the content and the source document that governs it, and ask where they disagree. Spend frontier credits here when the content is legal or safety-related. Try this: “Attached are a draft and the source document it should follow. List every place the draft differs from the source: anything changed, added or left out. Then tell me which version a course should follow, and anything wrong with the source itself.” Test 1: an expert’s deck against the procedure. I gave both models a subject expert’s 14-slide induction deck on supporting vulnerable customers, with speaker notes, and the procedure that governs the same topic. The deck was written before the latest version of the procedure, and I’d planted three contradictions between them. Fable 5.2 performs substantially better than smaller models when comparing documents and spotting where content has been dropped or changed. On points this was a tie: 26 out of 28 each, and both models refused to build from the outdated deck when the expert pushed. But Sonnet missed one of my three contradictions and made three small errors, including a feedback line that gave the wrong reason. Fable 5.2 made none. My checking tests showed the same pattern: the higher-tier model caught a condition that had been dropped from a rule, and the cheaper one didn’t. Test 2: an AI-built module against its storyboard. The module in my pre-release test was built by a different AI model, and I’d seeded it with ten defects. Fable 5.2 found the ones that mattered, tied each to the storyboard and the build standard, and said “do not release”. The cheaper model caught the main defects too, so for straightforward build checks you can save your credits. Why it’s worth the credits: Fable 5.2 found all three contradictions I’d planted and made no errors of its own. Sonnet missed one and made three. In my checking tests, the higher-tier model also caught a condition that had been dropped from a rule. That’s the hardest kind of error to spot, because nothing on the page looks wrong. A person checking line by line tires by slide 10. The model checks slide 14 as carefully as slide 1. When the content is legal or safety-related, one missed contradiction gets taught to every learner. 4. Writing the Questions for Your Subject Matter Expert Your subject expert’s review time is short, so the questions you bring to it decide what gets caught. The model reads the draft as a sceptical practitioner would and turns every doubtful passage into a specific question, so the meeting goes on what only the expert can answer. Do this: before the review meeting, have the model read the design as a sceptical practitioner and turn every doubtful passage into a question only the expert can answer. On the same data-privacy storyboard, Opus 5.5 wrote 29 questions. Sonnet 5.5 wrote 22. Same storyboard, same prompt, seven more questions. Try this: “Before I send this design to our subject expert, help me prepare the questions. Read the attached as an experienced practitioner who doubts everything, and: (1) find every statement that could be wrong, out of date, incomplete or unsafe; (2) turn each one into a short yes/no or one-word question only the expert can answer; (3) put legal and safety questions first. Don’t judge whether the content is right yourself. Instead, write questions which will gather the critical information I need to design this learning experience. Give me a table with two columns: questions to ask; why to ask them (value for the design).” What I uploaded: one file. The approved storyboard for a module on supporting vulnerable customers, before it went to the subject expert for review. Why it’s worth the credits: when it came to identifying “need to know” information required to design well, the higher-tier model performed like a lead designer, turning each gap into a question only the expert can answer. It asked seven more questions than the mid-tier model on the same storyboard, and more of the legal ones. It’s hard to doubt a design you helped write, and the model has no stake in it.
