On 8 November 1779, Benjamin Tallmadge wrote to a merchant in British-occupied New York, asking him to put a distinguishing mark on one particular sheet of paper. The sheet would otherwise look like any other in a bundle of writing paper. It contained a message for George Washington, written in invisible ink. Tallmadge was worried he might forward the wrong sheet, which would be rather embarrassing considering the trouble everyone had taken to conceal the correct one. The letter was addressed to Robert Townsend, who sold dry goods in Manhattan and moved easily among British officers and merchants. To his customers, he was another businessman trying to get through a war. To Washington, he was Culper Junior, part of a network created in 1778 by Tallmadge to collect intelligence from New York, the British military’s principal American base. Townsend’s reports travelled through couriers to Long Island, crossed the Sound by boat, and eventually reached Washington’s headquarters. The agents used false names, numerical codes and invisible ink. Washington himself did not know all their identities. In 2020, the Long Island Museum discovered Tallmadge’s undelivered letter among its holdings. The document had been sitting in the museum’s collection since 1951. The curious detail about the marked sheet survived, although its intended recipient apparently never received the request. Almost 250 years after the network was formed, a young American investor borrowed its name. Christian Lamarco had watched Turn: Washington’s Spies while growing up and liked the comparison between gathering information under people’s noses and investigating public companies. In 2019, after working at a Chicago investment firm, he set up Culper Research. He eventually moved to New York, working with two analysts from a Brooklyn apartment. Its business was activist short selling: find a company whose public story might be wrong, publish the evidence and profit if the share price falls. The company nobody wanted to short On 13 May 2026, Culper released a 40-page report called NVIDIA: The China Problem . Nvidia was then the most valuable company in the world and central to the artificial-intelligence boom. Its chips had become the hardware on which companies were spending enormous sums to train and run AI models. Betting against Nvidia was an uncomfortable proposition, even for somebody accustomed to taking unpopular positions. Its shares reached a new high the following day. Culper was investigating a question created by American export restrictions. After tighter controls were imposed on advanced AI chips in April 2025, Nvidia had said its affected China data-centre business had effectively fallen to zero. The report argued that Chinese demand had not disappeared; it had travelled through buyers and data centres in Southeast Asia. Culper estimated that more than 20% of Nvidia’s fiscal 2026 compute revenues remained tied to China. This was the firm’s allegation, and it has not been independently established. One company drew particular attention. Megaspeed, a Singapore-based buyer of Nvidia equipment, had grown remarkably quickly. Culper pointed to its filings, which it said showed its balance sheet expanding from $33 million at the end of 2023 to $3 billion at the end of 2024. Much of the increase came from refundable deposits. The report traced suspected financing links through Singapore entities to Alibaba and examined trade records involving servers assembled by other suppliers. The idea was to follow the money and the physical equipment, rather than assume that the address on an invoice revealed where a chip would ultimately be used. There was also a passage about the people involved. Culper included photographs of Nvidia’s chief executive, Jensen Huang, at events with Alice Huang, an executive associated with Megaspeed. The two share a surname but are not related. It described their relationship as “uncomfortably close” and questioned whether Nvidia had examined Megaspeed with sufficient independence. It did not claim to have discovered an affair or a Chinese intelligence operation. Lamarco’s team had worked through corporate filings in Singapore and Malaysia, spoken to industry sources and studied public footage, including video taken outside a Taiwanese restaurant. Some of the investigation began with earlier reporting by established newspapers. In his later account to The Wall Street Journal , Lamarco recalled abandoning the inquiry for a time because it seemed improbable that a tiny firm would find something important about the world’s most closely watched chipmaker. A short seller has an obvious incentive to make a worrying case sound persuasive. Culper disclosed that it was betting on Nvidia’s shares falling, and its findings deserved scrutiny on that basis. Nvidia defended its business with Megaspeed and said it had checked the company’s chip deployments. An unusual balance-sheet item or a photograph of executives together is reason to ask questions; neither proves illegal exports. Initially, the market showed little enthusiasm for Culper’s interpretation. Six months later On 29 October, The New York Times reported that American intelligence officials suspected Alice Huang of working on Beijing’s behalf. Nvidia initially maintained that Jensen Huang’s dealings with Megaspeed were ordinary customer interactions. In early November, Huang acknowledged marital infidelity, while Nvidia’s board opened an independent investigation into whether he had been targeted by a foreign intelligence operation. On 12 November, Nvidia announced that Huang had resigned as chief executive and director. The company appointed its long-serving finance chief, Colette Kress, as interim CEO. Its shares had already suffered a sharp fall amid the controversy and other worries about AI valuations. There is an important difference between Culper’s May report and what emerged later. The firm’s questions about the relationship attracted renewed attention, but intelligence allegations came from subsequent reporting, not Culper’s own investigation. Nor has the episode settled its larger claim that more than a fifth of Nvidia’s compute revenue was effectively driven by Chinese demand. Singaporean authorities, for their part, said they found insufficient evidence to charge Alice Huang over suspected false declarations involving server shipments; their inquiry was not an espionage case. The report circulated widely in November, having received relatively little attention in May. Whether Culper made money from the trade remains unknown: Lamarco has declined to disclose his returns or whether he still holds the short position. The original Culper agents had to hide a written message among ordinary sheets of paper to prevent an enemy from reading it. Their namesakes put forty pages on the internet and waited six months for the crowd to look again.
