## Scaling Slack and GitHub with Maude Lemaire

前 Slack、GitHub 工程師談大型產品擴展時真正遇到的效能與資料庫問題。

**Why** 產品規模到 Slack、GitHub 等級後，看似無害的設計（自訂表情符號、Active Record 抽象層）會在基礎設施層造成嚴重壓力。

**How** Maude Lemaire 以實例說明：限制自訂表情數量避免 thundering herd；用 Go 寫精簡版 Slack client 做負載測試；指出 Active Record 讓開發者看不到資料庫負載。

**What** Pragmatic Engineer 播客，來賓 Maude Lemaire（曾任 Slack 後端效能基礎設施團隊主管、GitHub、Cursor）。重點數據：Slack 限制自訂表情為 23 個；用 headless Chrome 對 50 萬 WebSocket 連線做負載測試，每次約 15 萬美元，因此她做了 Koi Pond；GitHub 首頁曾觸發約 100 次 SQL 查詢跨三個資料庫叢集，scatter query 使其無法超過 16 個 shard。她也提出平台與產品工程師輪調，並對平台等基礎工作親自驗證 LLM 輸出。

### 重點
- Thundering herd：10 萬人頻道每個自訂表情要扇出給 10 萬人，100 個表情即 1000 萬次 fan-out，Slack 因此限制自訂表情為 23 個
- Koi Pond：用 Go 寫精簡版 Slack client 建立 WebSocket 連線，取代每個 headless Chrome 實例 5 小時 0.37 美元、單次負載測試約 15 萬美元的作法
- GitHub 的 Active Record 讓首頁載入觸發約 100 次 SQL、scatter query 使 shard 數無法超過 16；Maude 主張平台與產品工程師定期輪調，並對基礎性工作親自驗證 LLM 輸出

We get an insider’s view on this from Maude Lemaire , a principal engineer and author of ‘ Refactoring at Scale: Regaining Control of Your Codebase .’ She spent nearly eight years at Slack, where she led the backend performance infrastructure team, before joining GitHub to tackle database scaling challenges. Most recently, she worked on improving performance at Cursor – including time to first token. In this episode of The Pragmatic Engineer , Maude and I dig into the challenges of scaling some of the world’s largest software products. We explore how Slack handled growing numbers of enterprise customers, how it evolved its approach to load testing, and the lessons Maude learned during nearly eight years of scaling Slack’s infrastructure. She also tells us what it was like joining GitHub in 2024 when Copilot was taking off, the challenges of working with GitHub’s gigantic Rails monolith, and how Maude uses AI in her work – while keeping judgment calls and decision-making firmly in her own hands. Takeaways from the conversation with Maude Here are eight interesting points from our discussion: 1. Performance should matter even if there’s no competition. In 2015, the homepage of Rent the Runway took up to 10 seconds to fully load due to a high-resolution hero image on the site. Maude was told it wasn’t an issue because there were no competitors to worry about. She thought differently: “The best competition is competition with yourself, knowing that you can do better, knowing that you can drive a better experience, ” she said. Maude spent some free time on speeding up the website without asking for permission to do so because it was the right call. 2. Slack limits custom emojis to 23 to avoid thundering herds. Here’s an example of a thundering herd : the CEO of a 100,000-member Slack channel posts “everyone gets Friday off!” In response, people start posting custom emojis – hundreds of them. The problem is that every single custom emoji has to fan out to all 100,000 users. With 10 custom emojis, that’s 1,000,000 fan-outs, with 100 it’s 10,000,000; a lot of network load! Slack limits custom emojis to 23 to avoid this problem. In reality, the absent 24th custom emoji is not missed. 3. Event floods can cripple the machines of Slack’s power users. Event floods are the cousin of thundering herds: they occur when a single client is overwhelmed by events coming through its own WebSocket. For example, when an IT worker is logged into lots of busy channels and they all receive the “everyone gets Friday off!” message – along with all the associated custom emojis and messages. This “power user” gets a sudden influx of messages with no deduplication and they struggle. 4. Load testing 500,000 WebSocket connections the simple way would have cost $150,000 per run. A new customer with 500,000 users was about to sign up to Slack, and Maude needed to run a load test to confirm the service could handle the load. Initially, she planned to spin up headless Chrome instances – each instance running a Slack session, and that would have cost $0.37 per five-hour session, per instance. The total price would’ve been crazily expensive. Instead, Maude built a tool called Koi Pond that spins up slimmed-down versions of Slack (‘Koi’), written in Go to establish the WebSocket connections. 5. Active Record downside: too much database load! GitHub used to be a Ruby on Rails monolith, which used Active Record as a database abstraction layer. This caused unexpected load: GitHub’s home page fetched live data from the database for most reloads, and loading the home page regularly triggered 100 SQL queries across three database clusters. This revealed a downside of abstractions like Active Record to Maude: when devs used it, they didn’t know or care about the additional load they unwittingly placed on the database while fetching data for whatever they were working on. 6. GitHub could not add more than 16 database shards because of scatter query problems. A scatter query is one without a primary key (a sharding key), and it needs to be run across all shard instances . But more shards make such queries slower. Back in the day at GitHub, lots of queries made through Active Record (many on the homepage) were scatter queries. This limitation prevented GitHub from adding more than 16 shards. I find this detail fascinating: it shows why a developer-friendly abstraction (Active Record) can actually cause severe pain at the infra level! 7. For platform work, Maude personally verifies everything she gets from an LLM. When doing foundational work, Maude pays close attention to verifying the output of LLMs because she believes that ambitious structures can only be built on stable foundations. It’s an interesting approach to separate “foundational” and “non-foundational” work in order to give more attention to the former, most critical, type. 8. Platform and product engineers should rotate more. The best platform engineers Maude has worked with are product engineers who reimplemented similar features a few times, grew frustrated with the repetitive toil, and so built tools to remove it once and for all. But an issue arises when platform engineers become detached from product development and drift away from engineers’ experience of using the product. To remedy this, Maude suggests rotation cycles where a platform engineer joins a product team to build a feature, and a product engineer joins the platform team.
