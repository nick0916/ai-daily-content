## Why Most of What You Build Creates No Value

AI 讓寫程式變便宜，但多數做出來的東西依然沒價值，上線前仍需要證據驗證。

**Why** 依 Itamar Gilad 的說法，A/B 測試、功能使用冪次分布與回顧 roadmap 都顯示至多約三分之一的點子有可量測效果，原因是使用者不易改變行為，且多數團隊不量測上線成效。

**How** 他用 Confidence Meter 依證據強度給點子信心分數：個人意見最多約 0.1/10、紙上審查約 0.5/10、蒐集資料最多 3/10，真正的測試與上線後數據才能拿更高分；並以 GIST（Goals、Ideas、Steps、Tasks）框架組織目標、評估點子、分步驗證與任務管理。

**What** Techworld with Milan 訪談前 YouTube／Gmail 產品負責人、《Evidence-Guided》作者 Itamar Gilad。他舉自己在 Gmail 的案例：fake-door 與可用性測試都通過，全部做完後 A/B 測試結果很差，因此未上線，這促成了 Confidence Meter。他認為 AI 反而讓驗證程式碼更貴，且 launch-and-iterate 難以隔離效果；工程師常見三個錯誤是不肯偷工、忽略 distribution、為技術而技術。

With AI agents we produce a massive amount of software today, faster than ever before. Now you can do something in a weekend that would have taken months before. Yet, we tend just to build, spend tokens and ship, even though no one has validated the idea first. My guest today disagrees with that approach. Itamar Gilad worked at YouTube and led parts of Gmail at Google, and before that he worked at Microsoft. He is one of the go-to people for me when I want to learn more about building products. Today his focus is coaching product teams, and he is the author of the book Evidence-Guided, in which he describes his GIST framework and the Confidence Meter, which was a revelation for me. At the core of his work is that at most one in three ideas we produce today has any measurable effect. Most teams never find out which ones, because they don’t measure launch outcomes. And this is interesting for my readers, who are mostly engineers, building those ideas. In this issue, we cover: Who is Itamar. From engineer to Gmail product lead at Google, and what pushed him to write Evidence-Guided. Where the one-in-three number comes from. A/B test data, feature usage power laws, and a backward look at your own roadmaps all point the same way. The cause is the same in every case: users don’t change their behavior easily. The Gmail idea that evidence killed. Fake-door and usability tests said yes. The A/B test said no, after the whole thing was built. That failure became the Confidence Meter. Why cheap AI coding makes validation matter more. Validation was never about saving coding time. Shipping bad ideas bloats the product with features, and launch-and-iterate is an expensive way to learn. Three mistakes engineers make with their own products. You can’t run this alone. Does AI research count as evidence. AI can analyze data, run web research, design experiments, and summarize interviews. It cannot stand in for users. You say that most of what we build creates little or no value for users or the business. Where does that come from? There are multiple sources that confirm this claim: Analysis of A/B tests across tests show that at most 1 in 3 experiments create the expected effects. Most have no effect, some have no effect at all. In any company where you analyze feature use you find a power law, some features are used broadly, many sporadically, and yet many more are hardly used at all. If you do a backward analysis of your roadmaps over the last few years in terms of outcomes, you’ll notice that almost nothing really moved the needle in a major way. Most of your bets seem to generate minor effects at best. In other words we’re working in high failure mode, although most of it is kept hidden because we don’t measure launch outcomes. The amount of waste generated is staggering. Improving your success ratio is by far the biggest lever product companies have to succeed in the market. The reason is quite simple: it’s really hard to change your users’ and customers’ behavior. They tend to disregard the new and keep using what they already know (which is sometimes the older version of your product). For a feature to be successful it has to traverse multiple hurdles: the user/customer needs to become aware of it, to try it once, to keep using it. They need to be willing to pay the costs of learning a new behavior and taking a risk on something they don’t already know. The odds are stacked against our ideas. 3. Can you tell us about a time evidence killed an idea you personally believed in? I once worked on a big strategic idea at Gmail. We validated it initially using fake-door tests and usability tests, and then made the mistake of implementing the whole thing (which was quite costly for us and for other teams) before finally testing in an A/B test. The results were really bad, and no matter how much we iterated they didn’t improve. The evidence was conclusive - there’s no reason to launch this feature, so we didn’t. The team was really disappointed, and I as the PM felt quite bad. But ultimately that was the right thing to do. The learning for me was that you shouldn’t overly trust early-stage tests like fake-doors and usability tests. They can easily create false signals. That’s a concept I later codified in the Confidence Meter . The Confidence Meter The Confidence Meter is a tool I created to assess the weight of the evidence we collect in support of an idea. Think of it as a thermometer that goes from zero (we have no confidence in this idea) to 10 (sure thing). The upper-right-corner (dark blue) is mostly about opinions of individuals or groups. This type of evidence gives very little confidence - no more than 0.1/10. The bottom right corner is about reviewing the idea with others, and analyzing it on paper. These are somewhat harder tests, but they still won’t give us more than 0.5/10 confidence level. The next classes of evidence are about collecting supporting data — qualitative or quantitative. These data may be anecdotal — a few data points, or Market Data — much larger data sets — with appropriate confidence levels for each, but never more than 3/10. The highest category of evidence comes from testing the idea. There are different tests which give more or less confidence depending on how rigorous they are. The ultimate test is actual post-launch data. We don’t have to find every type of evidence for every idea, and we don’t have to reach a high confidence level every time. “ Good enough ” is determined by the cost of the idea and how risky it is. 4. AI has made the cost of building cheap. Does this make evidence-guided development more important or less? I hear this a lot: “We used to validate ideas because development was expensive. Now it’s cheap, so let’s just build, ship and see what happens.” There are several flaws in this claim: AI accelerated coding, but it made some things like validating the code more expensive . There’s also the overall costs of AI coding on things like maintenance and security in the long run that are not well known. In other words it’s unclear yet how much dev is truly accelerated. The ratio probably varies by product, and company. We didn’t validate ideas just to save on coding time. We did it mostly because launching bad ideas is a deadly antipattern: bloating your products with features that almost no one uses is very costly. There’s also the opportunity cost of missing better ideas. Launch-and-iterate is a very expensive and ineffective way to learn. After we launch a feature it’s much harder to measure whether it’s working or not (many other things are happening in parallel, no way to isolate the effect of the new feature vs. the effect of a marketing campaign running in parallel). It’s also harder to iterate on a live feature and we’re often not motivated to call it a success and move on. It’s true that AI coding can get us quicker to a high-confidence test (see above the explanation about the confidence meter) so we may skip some earlier validation steps. But skipping validation altogether means testing on 100% of the users, which is rarely a good thing. In other words, evidence-guided dev is just as important, if not more, in the age of AI. 5. In your coaching work, what are the most common product mistakes you see engineers and indie builders make? I’m a former engineer myself, so these are things I was guilty of myself: Not willing to cut corners — whether it’s a product that has all the features, or setting the quality bar very high, or just aiming for a product that is super polished and beautiful. These are things you may care about, but the customers care about whether your product does what they need, and what costs it incurs on them. You can build a very successful product that is neither complete nor beautiful, because it addresses the core pain points of the users very well. Think of early Google Docs. Compared to Microsoft Office it was clearly an inferior product with a small subset of the features and a clunky interface. But it nailed some key pain points of collaboration and version sync, and it was much cheaper. That was enough for Google Docs to disrupt a market that was dominated by Office for decades. Disregarding the importance of marketing/distribution — this is especially true for B2C, but is also very relevant to B2B. “If you build it they will come” rarely works. All the successful products you’re using today were successful because they’re very good products and because they figured out distribution, marketing, pricing etc. Technology for technology’s sake — AI is just the latest example, but we had Web3, blockchain, VR, AR, assistants, and a long list of other ideas that were mostly a solution looking for problems. It’s always advisable to start with the goals — what is the technology supposed to achieve, and work your way from there. 6. Many of my readers have never worked with a product framework. Can you walk us through GIST (Goals, Ideas, Steps, Tasks) as you’d explain it to an engineer? The core idea of GIST is very simple: to move to evidence-guided development, product orgs need to change the way they work in four key areas: Setting goals — outcomes over outputs, balancing business goals with user-value goals, metrics hierarchies, OKRs... Evaluating ideas (aka prioritization) — Instead of picking ideas based on opinions, consensus or rank, factor in impact, cost, and available evidence (aka Confidence). Validating ideas in Steps - A Step in GIST terminology is any activity or short project that validates the assumptions underlying your ideas. The industry calls them experiments. Project-managing the tasks — We already have Agile Dev and Kanban to do this. The key at this layer is not to invent a new project management system, but to connect the tasks to the Step, Ideas, and Goals. I recommend using a GIST board for this.
