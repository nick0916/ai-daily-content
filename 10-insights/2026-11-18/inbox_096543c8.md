In December 1778, a nutmeg tree in a botanical garden on Mauritius finally bore fruit. The tree had been brought to the island years earlier by Pierre Poivre, a French administrator who had spent much of his career trying to acquire valuable spice plants from Dutch-controlled territories in Asia. For the gardeners, it was a welcome result after years of experiments. However, for the Dutch East India Company , it represented precisely the sort of development it had spent more than a century trying to prevent. Nutmeg had made the Company a considerable amount of money, largely because it was so difficult for anyone else to obtain it at the source. The nutmeg tree produces a yellow fruit which, when ripe, splits open to reveal a dark seed wrapped in a red membrane. The dried seed becomes nutmeg; the covering becomes mace. For centuries, the Banda Islands in eastern Indonesia were the world’s principal source of both. The inhabitants knew how to cultivate the trees and had long traded their harvest with merchants arriving from Java and elsewhere in Asia. The spices travelled through the Indian Ocean, often changing hands several times before reaching Europe. Each merchant added a margin, while distance and the uncertainty of supply did the rest. Spices had a peculiar position in European imagination. Herodotus repeated tales about cinnamon being gathered from enormous birds’ nests. Pliny the Elder complained about Rome’s expenditure on expensive eastern imports, particularly those from India. Yet merchants across the Indian Ocean knew a great deal about these products and had been trading them long before Europeans attempted to reach their sources by sea. Black pepper came from the Malabar Coast; nutmeg and cloves came from islands much farther east. Their geography would eventually help redirect the course of European commerce. A few years ago, in Spices: A Chilli and Pepper Story , I wrote about how the Europeans’ search for pepper, made European to explore different sea routes for India. This, nutmeg’s journey offers another side of the same history: the attempts to control where a plant could be grown, and what happened when those attempts began to fail. A company with its own army When Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498, he arrived in a trading world with established merchants, routes and customs. The Portuguese wanted to deal directly with Asian producers, but found that access to the coast did not guarantee favourable terms. Their response increasingly involved armed ships and fortified ports. The Dutch arrived later and took the arrangement considerably further. In 1602, several Dutch ventures combined to form the Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie , or VOC. Its charter granted exclusive Dutch trading rights across much of Asia, along with powers to negotiate treaties, build forts and wage war. The financial arrangement was unusual too. Instead of raising money for a single voyage and winding up the enterprise when the ships returned, the VOC could retain capital and continue operating. Its initial subscriptions approached 6.5 million guilders. Investors could sell shares to other investors, and trading in those claims became an important part of Amsterdam’s emerging securities market. Long voyages, permanent capital and military protection were now brought together under one institution. The arrangement worked less smoothly in the Banda Islands. A Dutch charter granting exclusive trading rights meant very little to an islander accustomed to selling nutmeg to several visiting merchants. The VOC wanted to buy the entire harvest. The Bandanese had no particular reason to prefer a single buyer, especially when others offered better terms. Agreements were signed, disputed and broken. The Company built forts and gradually replaced commercial bargaining with force. In 1621, Governor-General Jan Pieterszoon Coen launched a brutal campaign against Banda. Much of the indigenous population was killed, enslaved or driven away. The Dutch divided nutmeg-growing land into plantations operated by European planters and worked largely by enslaved labourers. A commercial monopoly had acquired the machinery of territorial rule. Recent research suggests that nutmeg production may have declined after the conquest. Control over supply, however, had an economic value of its own. In the late 1620s and early 1630s, a pound of nutmeg could sell in Amsterdam for around 50 stuivers, while plantation operators received about 0.87 stuivers. Freight, losses and other costs stood between those prices, but the spread helps explain the expense of maintaining the monopoly. The island and the garden The English tried to establish themselves on Run, one of the smallest Banda Islands. Its value lay in its nutmeg trees. After decades of Anglo-Dutch rivalry, the Treaty of Breda in 1667 confirmed English possession of New Netherland, including Manhattan, while the Dutch retained Run and their position in the spice islands. The comparison looks absurd only with hindsight. Manhattan’s future was unknowable; nutmeg already commanded high prices in European markets. The difficulty was that a monopoly over a living plant could not be secured forever. It was possible to guard ports and prevent merchants from buying outside the Company’s network. Preventing a species from taking root elsewhere was a different proposition, particularly once rival colonial powers began collecting and transplanting useful plants. Pierre Poivre’s attempts to grow nutmeg on Mauritius were part of that effort. He obtained spice plants from the Indo-Pacific region and introduced nutmeg to the island in 1770. Even then, success was uncertain. Nutmeg generally has separate male and female trees, and a young tree takes years to mature. A planter could spend that time tending a tree which would never bear fruit. Transporting viable seeds and reproducing the conditions of their native islands presented further problems. Local cultivators, enslaved gardeners and knowledge acquired in the spice islands were important to the experiments, even when their contributions went unrecognised in European accounts. The first fruit appeared in 1778. The VOC did not collapse because of a botanical experiment; its eventual demise in 1799 had several financial and political causes. But nutmeg cultivation gradually spread beyond the Banda Islands, eventually reaching other tropical colonies and parts of southern India. The geographical advantage that had sustained the Dutch monopoly was slowly disappearing. There is a curious circle in this history. European ships sailed east looking for spices, helped carry American chilli into India, and subsequently transported Asian spice plants to other parts of the world. Both chilli and nutmeg are now familiar ingredients in Indian kitchens, their rather extraordinary journeys largely forgotten. The contest over who gets to grow and sell a valuable plant continues today, although the methods have changed considerably. Modern seed companies protect particular varieties and technologies through patents, plant breeders’ rights and licensing agreements. Governments negotiate access to genetic resources, while farmers and companies dispute the rights to reproduce and sell seeds. The commercial value still depends, in part, on who has permission or the practical ability to grow something. The nutmeg trees at the Pamplemousses garden in Mauritius belong to an earlier chapter of this continuing argument over ownership. Four centuries ago, a company needed soldiers and warships to keep a valuable plant within its control. Today, I guess it’s same!
