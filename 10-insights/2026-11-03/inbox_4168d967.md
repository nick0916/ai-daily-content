## The Wednesday Night That Crossed a Border

印度電台排行榜靠零散證據拼出全國榜單，榜單又反過來影響聽眾。

**Why** 沒有串流數據與收聽紀錄時，要衡量全國最受歡迎的歌曲只能靠間接證據。

**How** Binaca Geetmala 向唱片行收集銷量、參考聽眾來信，並納入約 400 個 Shrota Sanghs 俱樂部的每週選曲，每週三公布排名。

**What** 1952 年由 Ameen Sayani 主持，於錫蘭電台播出，首播來信約 9,000 封，據報導一年內每週約 6 萬封。1957 年 All India Radio 推出 Vividh Bharati，Geetmala 於 1989 年移轉、1994 年結束。

### 重點
- 1952 年 Ameen Sayani 主持 Binaca Geetmala，節目從錫蘭電台播出，贊助商為牙膏品牌，每週薪資 ₹25
- 排名依據唱片行銷量（贊助商 CIBA 收集）、聽眾來信與約 400 個 Shrota Sanghs 俱樂部的選曲
- 原文指出排名可能被片商操弄，而排名高低會帶來更多聽眾、銷量與點播，形成回饋迴圈

On 3 December 1952, a 20-year-old radio announcer had a modest assignment. Ameen Sayani was to introduce seven Hindi film songs for Binaca Geetmala . The recordings would travel from Bombay to Radio Ceylon, which would broadcast them back to India. The sponsor was a toothpaste company. Sayani was paid ₹25 a week to select the music, write the script, present the programme and handle listener mail. It was a competition, too. Listeners could send in answers for a chance to win ₹100. The producers expected forty or fifty letters. The first broadcast brought 9,000. Within a year, the weekly mail had reportedly reached 60,000 letters. A small team struggled to keep up. The music that went overseas The newly independent Indian government had strong opinions about what its citizens should hear. B.V. Keskar, the Minister for Information and Broadcasting, was deeply committed to Indian classical music. He considered much of the popular film music of the time vulgar, overly Westernised and unsuitable for the country’s principal broadcaster. Keskar wanted radio to cultivate certain tastes, even as cinema was cultivating quite different ones. All India Radio began severely restricting film songs in the early 1950s. Hindi cinema kept producing music people wanted to hear, but AIR gave it little room. Across the Palk Strait, Radio Ceylon saw an opportunity. It had powerful shortwave transmitters and a commercial broadcasting operation willing to sell advertising to Indian companies. An advertising agency in Bombay could find sponsors, arrange programmes and prepare the recordings locally. So the songs were recorded in Bombay, flown to Colombo and transmitted back to Indian homes. A toothpaste advertisement paid for the journey. Listeners only had to turn their tuning knobs. Too many letters By 1954, Geetmala had become too popular for its original competition format. The enormous volume of mail made the seven-song contest difficult to manage, so the producers changed it into a one-hour countdown of popular film music. Now listeners wanted to know which song had climbed, which had fallen, and what would be number one on Wednesday. But how did you measure a country’s favourite songs without streaming statistics or a record of who was listening? You asked the shops selling records. The sponsor, CIBA , collected sales information from record shops across India. Listener letters provided another indication of demand. Eventually, fan clubs called Shrota Sanghs began sending their own weekly selections. At its peak, the programme had around 400 such clubs. None of these measures could tell the producers exactly how many people had heard a song. They had to work with the evidence people left behind. Someone in Bombay had to make sense of record sales, postcards and club preferences, then announce the results every Wednesday at eight. Who decides number one? There were allegations of film producers and music directors manipulating record purchases. Requests could be organised as well. A good ranking brought more listeners, which could bring more sales and more requests. The chart was helping create the evidence used to compile the next chart. The organisers used listener clubs as a counterweight to sales figures. Film industry figures were later invited to examine the ranking process and its records. Some listeners kept handwritten diaries of every week’s rankings. Others argued over whether their favourite song deserved its position, as though Wednesday evening had given them a small part in deciding the country’s musical pecking order. Jhumri Telaiya, a small mining (mica) town in Jharkhand, became famous partly because its residents sent so many requests to radio programmes. Its name was heard in homes across India, carried there by postcards. It was a urban legend to ask someone, are you from Jhumri Telaiya? The rankings gave listeners in distant towns a way to hear what people elsewhere were choosing. Record shops and fan clubs, meanwhile, kept supplying the evidence for next Wednesday’s countdown. Back across the border All India Radio eventually responded. In October 1957, it launched Vividh Bharati, giving popular film songs more space in its programming. Geetmala stayed with Radio Ceylon for decades. It moved to Vividh Bharati in 1989 and ended its long run in 1994. By then, cheap music cassettes and television had changed how Indians heard songs. People could replay a favourite without waiting for the radio announcer to reach its place in the countdown. Today a song’s play count appears almost instantly on Spotify or YouTube. We can see what’s popular, then choose to hear it because it’s popular. The chart affects the listening that produces the next chart. Geetmala encountered that problem with record-shop registers and handwritten postcards. A purchase could lift a song’s ranking; its higher ranking could send more people to the record shop. In 1952, Bombay’s film songs travelled through Colombo because Delhi had restricted their airtime. By 1989, Delhi was broadcasting the countdown itself.
