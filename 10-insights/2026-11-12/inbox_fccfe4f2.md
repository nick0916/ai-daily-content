## We Promised Three Months. It Took Nine.

軟體工程「定律」互相矛盾時，代表你還沒看清哪股力量在主導，而 AI 只會放大這些定律。

**Why** 作者團隊重寫 12 年歷史的計費系統，承諾 3 個月卻花了 9 個月，過程中 Brooks、Conway、Gall、Hyrum、Hofstadter 等定律輪番應驗。

**How** 每條定律都有適用範圍，先檢查其假設；度量時搭配反向指標；遇到團隊問題用多條定律逐一檢查，先命名主導的力量再處理。

**What** 作者是在 Heapcon 發表此演講。案例包括：兩團隊以 API 整合，一邊送「分」、一邊收「歐元」導致發票金額錯誤，解法是先讓團隊坐在一起談定契約再讓 AI 寫碼；AI 寫碼使 bus factor 趨近 0，建議核心模組至少 2 人理解並把文件放在程式旁；15 人 5 組各自用 agent 設計微服務導致整合失敗，應改用模組化單體；新舊匯出格式（INV-000123;17.10.2026;1.200,00 與 INV-123;2026-10-17;1200.00）雖都符合規格，卻讓最大客戶匯入失敗（Hyrum's Law）。

### 重點
- Brooks's Law（10 人 45 條溝通線、15 人 105 條）與 Linus's Law 不衝突：前者講協調成本，後者講開源除錯；每條定律都有適用範圍，要先檢查假設
- 度量要量成果不是產出，每個指標配一個反向指標；token 排行榜會導致 tokenmaxxing 而非品質提升
- AI 放大定律：Conway（API 契約錯誤，分 vs 歐元）、Bus factor（AI 寫碼降為 0）、Gall（各 agent 自設微服務）、Hyrum（日期/小數格式被依賴）、Ninety-Ninety（AI 只寫完前 90%）

It was a Monday, and our CTO told us we needed to rewrite our 12-year-old billing system. It was a real legacy system, with a lot of technical debt. The CTO said, "I think we could probably rewrite this in three months, but let’s check the feasibility with a PoC first." And one of our senior engineers, Ana, did this in an afternoon. The CTO concluded, "Yes, we can do it, and I will tell the board." Then, when we started the project, we found 140 special cases that failed in the new system, and it was clear it wouldn't be easy. A bit later, our CTO split us into three teams and put up a token board to see whether we were really spending AI tokens as promised. After 6 months, five new engineers and 10 new AI agents joined us, and soon we had 40 microservices implemented. Then, in month nine, we shipped the new system, but our largest customer’s import was broken. This is a Monday I had more than once in my career, and at this year’s Heapcon I gave this talk. During this time, it was obvious that we had different laws pulling in opposite directions. Brooks’s Law says that adding new people makes a late project take longer, while Linus’s Law says that with enough eyeballs, every bug becomes easier to detect. In April, I talked about 20 Software Engineering Laws and said some of them contradict each other, and today we will learn what to do about it. In this issue, we will cover: Laws can contradict each other. Two laws can disagree because they operate in different contexts and are affected by different forces. Every law has an operating range. We need to understand and check each law's assumptions. Measure, then measure the cost. How Goodhart and Gilb laws work together. Laws sometimes work better in groups. Five key questions for a team that has a problem. AI makes the laws more emphasized than before. What will change when agents write most of the code? Name the force before you fix it. How you can use three steps to detect issues and solve problems in your team. So, let’s dive in. 1. A collision between laws is a signal We have many examples of laws that seem to contradict each other. For example, Brooks’s Law and Linus’s Law . They basically explain the same thing: what happens if you add more people to the project. Eric Raymond (who coined Linus’s Law after Linus Torvalds) said in The Cathedral and the Bazaar that if we considered only Brooks’s Law, Linux would not have been possible. And why is this? Because they describe different work. When we talk about Brooks’s Law, it’s mostly about increased coordination costs and more communication lines. With 10 people, there are 45 communication paths, and when you have 15, there are 105. So, if you add one more person, everyone needs to spend time coordinating with that person, onboarding them, etc. Why doesn’t this affect Linus’s Law? Because it describes debugging open-source projects, where more people can help, and you don’t need as much coordination. One law I found outside the software world (actually, it came from coaching) is that the Map is not the territory . Real life is the territory, with different forces pulling in different directions, but the laws are the map that explains them in software projects. Although they may look contradictory, that only means we don’t yet know which force is strongest. The map is not the territory 2. Every law has an operating range When we look at these software laws , note that they depend on your specific situation, and you need to check them in your case. We all know that Knuth said “ Premature optimization is the root of all evil ”, and this is true in most of the cases, but not in all cases. It assumes most code is not on the critical path, so optimization may be premature. But in some cases, e.g., a high-frequency trading system, latency matters a lot. Another example of Postel’s Law says, “ be conservative in what you send, but be liberal in what you accept from others. ” This law helped us to build the Internet as it is today, and it was possible back then when it was much friendlier and smaller. But many of these parsers were overly flexible and introduced security issues, such as HTTP request smuggling, and RFC 9413 in 2023 describes how tolerance makes interoperability basically worse over time. Postel’s Law Also, YAGNI is one of my favorite principles, but it doesn’t hold in all cases, e.g., if I need that feature later and it would take much more to implement (and I’m confident I will want it). 3. Measure, then measure the cost As a CTO, I often ask myself: should we measure developer productivity at all? Gilb’s Law says it’s better to measure anything than nothing, but Goodhart says we should be careful not to make our measures targets. So, they seem contradictory, but from the right angle, they help us measure properly. The right thing to do is measure outcomes, not outputs, and keep a counter-metric in mind for every metric you define. In the recent State of Developer Experience report by Atlassian (from October 2026), we still see that most leaders use wrong metrics that could easily be gamed (mostly measuring outputs). If your company created a leaderboard measuring how many tokens each developer spends, token usage will increase (called tokenmaxxing). But your product quality probably won't. State of Developer Experience Report by Atlassian To address this problem properly, you can add another metric that shows, e.g., which features your users actually use (and are satisfied with), how much it costs you, whether that code is removed or rewritten, etc. What I recommend to leaders when they define any metric: first, ask their team, is this something you can game? If yes, don’t make it a target. Pair every metric with a counter-metric. Even though all of this may seem reasonable, our industry has a long history of measuring the wrong things (see the image below). Only in the last year have we seen many companies use token boards. We tend to measure outputs instead of outcomes, and outputs are easy to game. And I’m sure we will see this in the future. Keep this in mind when you set your team's next targets. Museum of Meaningless Metrics (Author: Itamar Gilad) 4. Laws work better in groups If you have a problem in your project or team, it's hard to explain it with only one law. If your team has issues with productivity or morale, here are a few questions you can ask before you change anything in their work or processes: Conway’s Law: Does your team organization reflect your system architecture, or have you divided responsibilities in a way that's now making communication difficult? Brooks’s Law: Have you added new people to your team recently in order to go faster, and did this slow your team down? The Ringelmann Effect: Has your team grown so large that everyone's individual productivity has dropped? Goodhart’s Law: Do you have a metric that measures the wrong thing? Not the one you really wanted to measure. Broken Windows: Does your project have many small issues that no one wants to fix? It’s piling up your technical debt . Your environment shapes behavior in your project. So if you just replace people, you will not gain anything. Usually, more than one issue happens at the same time. You solve it by debugging to find the main issue and solving that one first. We'll see how exactly in the next sections. 5. AI makes the laws stronger Most of these laws are really old, some over 50 years, and they will stay with us for our lifetime and much more. Why? Because they are bound to the roots of human nature. AI makes them only stronger now. AI amplifies these laws. In our project, after three months, two of our teams didn’t communicate properly as before because AI agents could build their systems faster by integrating via API. And when two services (and two teams) communicate via API, we can see another set of problems. For example, we had a case where one side sends the amount in cents, and the other receives it in euros. So the invoice was charged incorrectly. This is the basis of Conway’s Law . Now we talk more with agents than people, and the system reflects this gap. We solved this by putting these teams in the room so they sit and agree on the contracts first, before AI writes any code. The bug is between the teams. As our project continued, our AI agents generated a lot of code, but we couldn’t review it all. Yet, only one of our engineers knew the Payments module deeply, so no one else dared to touch it. When that engineer took a two-week vacation, no one knew why the module worked the way it did, and we had to wait for them to return. This is one example of a Bus factor , which is the number of people who could be hit by a bus (figuratively speaking) and get our project in trouble. For our payment module, it was one. But when AI writes the code, it’s usually 0, because no one understands how or why it works that way. Bus factor The proper way to solve the bus factor problem is to have at least two people know the core parts (modules) of your system, but also to create proper docs near the code they describe. Why? Because code doesn’t tell the whole truth about why we did it this way, what we also considered as an option, what decisions we made, and more. With these docs, people and agents can better understand the overall system. Then, in month seven, we had 15 people in five teams of three. Each team designed its own microservice system with agents, and soon we got a system with many microservices. Nothing from this worked together. Gall’s Law explains this problem. You cannot build a successful complex system from scratch. You can build it only from a simple system that worked well. In our case, every agent designed its own microservices instead of using a modular monolith approach in production for one country. Then, when one module changes more often, we can extract it as a separate service so we can build and release it independently. Gall’s Law By month nine of our rewrite, we had an issue with our biggest customer. They could no longer import our invoices. Our old export was in this format INV-000123;17.10.2026;1.200,00 , and the new one was like: INV-123;2026-10-17;1200.00 . Both were according to specs: invoice number, date, and the amount, but some small changes broke it, like leading zeros and the decimal separator. Both exports follow the spec. This is exactly what Hyrum's Law defines. With enough users, someone will depend on every observable part of your system. And this includes not only endpoints, field names, and API status codes, but also response times, date formats, and even bugs. Yes, we had a bug our partner depended on. When we fixed it, it failed on the partner’s side. Now it’s clear our three-month plan took nine months in the end, and we ran into Hofstadter’s Law , which basically says, “ it always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter’s Law .” Tom Cargill at Bell Labs explained it as the Ninety-Ninety rule . The first 90% of code takes the first 90% of the time, but the last 10% takes the other 90%. I've seen this many times on projects. We developers are optimistic with estimates, and we often focus on the core part of the job. But the other 10%, which includes activities like integrations, testing, documentation, handover, and deployments, is often overlooked, and it could take the same amount of time as the core part. And with AI, it is not much better: it writes only that first 90%, but the rest is still on us to do. The solution, of course, is to build in a buffer for the other 90%.
