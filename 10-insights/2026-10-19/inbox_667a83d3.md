Share It is spring 2023, and the question hanging over higher education is whether students should be allowed to use generative AI at all. ChatGPT is suddenly in faculty conversations, usually paired with words like cheating, plagiarism, detection, and ban. Jason Lively, Elizabeth Melick, and I are approaching the problem from another direction. In an eight-week web design and user experience course, students are introduced deliberately to text and image generators and asked to try them as part of the design process. None of us can know yet whether these tools will improve the work, hollow it out, or become another novelty students abandon when the assignment ends. What interests me is not merely whether the technology can generate an image or a paragraph; I want to know what happens when students encounter a new form of assistance and must decide where it belongs in their process. Looking back from 2026, I recognize that we were already asking a question larger than the course: when a machine can participate in creative work, what parts of the work do students still understand as theirs? The course gives students repeated opportunities to find out. In week two, they encounter eleven AI applications and begin using AI to make selected design decisions. In subsequent weeks, they experiment during ideation, aesthetic development, copywriting, image generation, and other formative stages. At several points they must explain how they used AI, why they selected particular applications, and where generated material entered the design workflow. By week six, they are presenting a client project and documenting those decisions; in week seven, they discuss AI and its impact on design. Then week eight arrives, and one small change alters the meaning of everything that came before it. Students submit their final projects, but this time they are not required to mention AI at all (Lively et al., 2023). For the first time in the sequence, the scaffolding stops telling them that AI belongs in the assignment. That is the moment I return to now. If students had used the tools only because we required them to, AI might have disappeared as soon as the requirement disappeared. Instead, 87.5% incorporated AI applications into their final projects (Lively et al., 2023). Some used text generation, some used image generation, some combined the two, and others used the tools primarily to find ideas before creating content themselves. The number catches my attention, but not because it proves that students &#8220;liked AI.&#8221; They did not respond uniformly; some disliked the exercises, some remained uncertain, and several did not believe AI improved their final work. What is important is that most had moved from complying with an instructional exercise to making an independent decision about whether a tool belonged in their work. We had introduced the technology, but by the final project the students were beginning to draw the boundaries. Their boundaries were not where I initially expected them to be. In the 2023 survey, 78.13% supported allowing AI during ideation and other formative stages, while only 65.63% supported using AI to complete and submit final work (Lively et al., 2023). I now read that difference as an early expression of something more interesting than acceptance or resistance. Students seemed to understand assistance and substitution as different categories even when faculty policy had not yet developed precise language for the distinction. Generating possibilities, testing a color palette, overcoming a blank page, or exploring versions of copy could feel like support for thinking. Allowing the technology to cross into the finished artifact raised another question: if the system does this part, what exactly am I demonstrating? In 2023, much of the conversation focused on whether AI use counted as cheating. The students were already confronting a harder problem, namely how much cognitive and creative responsibility they were willing to delegate. At the time, our research questions were practical. We wanted to know whether text generators could improve productivity, writing, and coding; whether image generators could support ideation and aesthetic decisions; and whether students would produce stronger projects when these systems entered the workflow. Yet the finding that has stayed with me is less about performance than identity. When Jason and I extended the work longitudinally through spring 2024, student adoption continued to vary according to disciplinary background. Computer science students, for example, showed greater willingness to use AI for color selection, a task outside the center of their disciplinary identity, than for coding, a capability much closer to what they understood themselves to be learning to do (Lively &amp; Hutson, 2024). That pattern clarified what I had not fully seen in the original study. Students were not simply deciding whether AI was useful; they were deciding which forms of expertise they were prepared to share with it. The question of tool adoption had quietly become a question of professional and intellectual identity. I think differently about that spring now because the tools have become more capable, while the educational question has become less simple. In 2023, it was tempting to imagine that institutions could solve generative AI primarily by establishing the correct permission structure: allowed here, prohibited there, disclose this, do not submit that. Those rules still matter, but they do not resolve the underlying issue. A student can use AI extensively and still exercise substantial judgment, criticism, revision, and disciplinary knowledge; another can use much less AI and surrender the thinking an assignment was designed to elicit. The important variable is not only how much of the artifact a machine touched, but where the student&#8217;s intellectual agency remained active. I did not have that language clearly in spring 2023. I had emerging tools, a study, students making choices, and the intuition that keeping AI outside the classroom would teach us little about what students would actually do with it. Their choices began supplying the vocabulary later. Three years later, I am less interested in asking whether students use AI than I was when that first study began. I want to know what they asked it to do, what they rejected, what they changed, what they can explain, and which decisions they insisted on making themselves. That does not mean every use of AI is educationally valuable, nor does it mean every assignment should permit it. It means the most important evidence may reside in the boundary a learner draws between assistance and authorship. In spring 2023, we thought we were studying whether generative AI could improve web design education. I now think we were watching students negotiate, task by task, what it meant to retain ownership of their learning while working beside a machine. The surprising result was not that most students used AI when they were given the choice. It was that choice itself became data.
