## The Memory That Built the Flat Screen

電視變平的技術源頭，來自為省電腦記憶體而做的電漿顯示器。

**Why** 1960 年代 PLATO 教學系統要連接大量終端，但把整個畫面存在電腦記憶體中成本過高。

**How** Bitzer 團隊讓電漿單元自己記住開或關的狀態，電腦只需在畫面變動時送出指令。

**What** Owens-Illinois 1971 年推出 512×512 的 Digivue 電漿顯示器；電漿後因平面特性用於大尺寸電視，Fujitsu 1990 年展示 31 吋彩色電漿，Panasonic 2013 年停產電漿電視，Samsung 與 LG 隨後跟進。

### 重點
- Bitzer 團隊的電漿單元可自行記住像素開/關狀態，PLATO 電腦只需傳送變更指令，不必持續重繪整個畫面
- 1990 年 Fujitsu 展示 31 吋彩色電漿，後推出 42 吋；1990 年代末已有約 60 吋的電漿螢幕
- LCD 面板尺寸持續放大、耗電與發熱較低，2013 年 Panasonic 停產電漿電視

If you walked into a typical living room in 1995, the television would have been the largest piece of furniture in the room. It sat in a wooden cabinet, occupying a substantial corner, and weighed as much as a small refrigerator. But then the modern flat-screen television originated not from entertainment companies, but from computer scientists tackling an economic crisis in the 1960s regarding excessively costly data storage. In the early 1960s, the University of Illinois was working on PLATO, one of the first large computer systems built for teaching. Students sitting at terminals could study mathematics, science, languages and other subjects while a central computer handled the lessons. The ambition was to eventually connect many such terminals, which created an expensive problem. Computer memory at the time was costly, and using large amounts of it simply to keep a picture on a screen was difficult to justify. Donald Bitzer, one of the people behind PLATO, began looking at the problem from the other end. Instead of storing the image inside the computer, perhaps the display itself could hold it. The idea led to experiments with plasma. Scientists had known for a long time that certain gases glow when electricity passes through them. Neon signs worked on the same principle. Bitzer’s team tried turning this effect into a display by trapping gas inside tiny cells between sheets of glass. Each cell could light up independently and become one point in a larger picture. An early version was built in 1962 by graduate student Robert Willson. The principle worked, but constructing a useful display was another matter. The glass had to hold thousands of cells, the gas could leak, and the electrical system required too many components. At one stage, a 500-by-500-pixel screen would have needed around 250,000 individual resistors. During later experiments, a small amount of air accidentally entered the gas mixture. Something useful happened. Once a cell had been switched on, it could remain in that state even after the voltage was reduced. The electrical charge around the cell helped preserve its condition. A pixel could now remember whether it was on or off. The PLATO computer did not have to continuously redraw the whole screen. It only had to send a new instruction when something changed. Part of the job that expensive computer memory would normally perform had moved into the screen itself. The University of Illinois licensed the technology to Owens-Illinois, a company whose main business was glass. In 1971, Owens-Illinois began selling a 512-by-512 plasma display called Digivue, and PLATO became an important early customer. At this point plasma was still largely a computer-display technology. Its second life came from a different feature that had almost nothing to do with computer memory. The problem with a large television Televisions of that period used cathode-ray tubes. They produced excellent pictures, but the tube extended far behind the screen. Making the picture larger meant making a much larger and heavier television. This eventually became ridiculous. Sony produced a 45-inch CRT television that weighed more than 400 pounds. Plasma screens were flat. That property was not the main reason Bitzer’s team had worked on them, but by the 1990s it was becoming far more valuable than their ability to remember pixels. Fujitsu demonstrated a 31-inch colour plasma screen in 1990 and later produced a 42-inch version. By the end of the decade, companies were showing plasma screens around 60 inches across. For anyone who remembers the large wooden television cabinets that occupied a corner of the living room, these screens looked completely different. A television could now be wide without also being deep. It could hang close to a wall. Plasma became one of the defining technologies of the early flat-screen television market. Expensive models appeared in hotels, offices and wealthy homes before gradually reaching a wider market. For some years, if you wanted a very large television, plasma was often the obvious choice. Meanwhile, the problem that had helped create plasma in the first place had quietly disappeared. Semiconductor memory improved rapidly after the development of DRAM around the end of the 1960s. Chips became denser and cheaper. Computers no longer needed a clever screen that could save them from storing the picture themselves. Plasma had survived because it had found a second use. That second use was now being attacked by LCD. Calculators enter the living room Liquid-crystal displays had spent years in smaller devices: calculators, digital watches, laptops and computer monitors. The early panels were too small and too expensive to threaten large televisions. Manufacturing improved, factories became larger, and the size of LCD panels kept increasing. The economics began to turn against plasma. LCD televisions generally consumed less electricity and produced less heat. Manufacturers could push them towards higher resolutions, and enormous investments were being made in LCD production lines. Each new generation of factories made larger panels at lower cost. Plasma still had advantages. Many viewers preferred its black levels, colours and treatment of fast-moving images. For enthusiasts, good plasma televisions remained excellent products. But television markets are rarely decided only by which picture a specialist prefers. They are also decided in factories, shipping containers, electricity bills and retail prices. By the early 2010s, LCD had taken most of the market. Panasonic ended plasma television production in 2013. Samsung and LG followed soon after. A technology that had once looked like the future of television disappeared within a few years. Its history is easy to miss because we remember plasma through the last product it became famous for. The actual chain is stranger. A university wanted to teach students through computers. Computer memory was expensive, so researchers built a screen that could remember its own pixels. The same technology happened to be flat, which became valuable when people wanted much larger televisions. Then computer memory became cheap, LCD factories became enormous, and both reasons for needing plasma gradually disappeared. The television was only one stop in the journey. There are many technologies around us with histories like this. Their most famous use was often not the problem they were invented to solve. They travelled because another industry discovered that some forgotten property had suddenly become useful. A plasma screen hanging on a wall twenty years ago looked like a new kind of television. It had actually begun as an answer to a very old computer problem: memory cost too much, so the screen learned to remember for itself.
