## What on earth is going on with AI pricing and packaging?

AI 產品的定價頁讓人看不懂：只告訴你「多 5 倍」「5 億 token」，卻沒說這些能做多少事。

**Why** AI 產品能做的事幾乎無限、每次成本落差又大，不像傳統 SaaS 能用席位或儲存量收費，導致業界連自己也難以預測用量與成本。

**How** 作者歸納四個同時發生的原因：（1）發明「遊樂場代幣」式的 credits／token 讓錢不像錢；（2）用大數字取代價值；（3）不知道該以什麼為計費單位（訊息、任務、時間、成果？）；（4）連自己的推論成本都難預測，尤其是會規劃、重試、呼叫工具的 agent。

**What** Elena Verna（Lovable 成長負責人）逐一檢視 Grok、Muse、Lovable、Instinct、ElevenLabs、ChatGPT、Anthropic 的方案：Grok 的 Premium 是 Plus 的 4x、Super 是 15x，卻不說 Plus 含多少；Muse 登出狀態無定價頁，App 內為 $20/月、每週 5 億 token；Lovable $25/月給 100 Pro credits；Anthropic Max 為 Pro 的 5x 或 20x。她認為目前產品太新太好所以使用者容忍，但大眾市場未必。

### 重點
- 四大成因：credits 如遊樂場代幣（mental accounting）、大數字取代價值、計費單位不明、AI 自身成本不可預測
- 實例：Grok Premium 4x／Super 15x 卻未揭露 Plus 基準；Muse $20/月每週 5 億 token；Lovable $25/月 100 Pro credits；Anthropic Max 5x/20x
- 有明確單位的產品較好定價（ElevenLabs 以音訊分鐘、Firecrawl 以頁面數），越通用的 agent 越難找到單位

I recently went down a rabbit hole looking at AI pricing pages, trying to answer what I thought was a pretty basic question: If I pay $20 a month for an AI product, what exactly am I getting? How much can I use it? And how do I know if I need the $20 plan, the $100 plan, or the &#8220;contact sales and surrender your firstborn&#8221; plan? I&#8217;ve spent an embarrassing amount of my career obsessing over pricing and packaging and most importantly I work for an AI company, so I assumed I could figure this out. Turns out, I have vastly overestimated my qualifications. Some companies promise 5x more usage without explaining 5x of WHAT. Others give you hundreds of millions of tokens, which sounds generous until you realize you have no idea what a token buys you. Some have decided that having a pricing page is optional. Just imagine interviewing someone who tells you they&#8217;ll cost 500 million tokens a week, or that for twice the salary they&#8217;ll do &#8220;5x more work,&#8221; without telling you how much work they do today. Funny, right? In AI, we call that a pricing page. And here&#8217;s the fascinating part: these companies are WILDLY successful. Millions of users, eye-watering valuations, explosive growth. Apparently, understanding what you&#8217;re paying for is no longer a prerequisite for buying software. Before anyone accuses me of throwing stones from my very glass house, I lead growth at Lovable, and we have the same problem. We sell credits for a product that can build websites, apps, internal tools, presentations, and whatever else someone dreams up tomorrow. Good luck wrapping that into three subscription tiers. But maybe we&#8217;re not all just terrible at pricing. AI products can do almost anything, and the cost of doing it can vary wildly. Unlike traditional SaaS, where we could reasonably charge for seats, storage, or transactions, we&#8217;re now trying to price work that neither the customer nor the company can reliably predict. I think there are four things happening simultaneously, and they&#8217;re making an already complicated problem considerably worse. 1. We&#8217;ve invented arcade money for AI Let&#8217;s start with something we&#8217;ve been doing in pricing for decades: making money feel less like money. There&#8217;s a concept in behavioral economics called mental accounting, which helps explain why people treat different forms of money differently. It&#8217;s why casinos have chips, arcades have tokens, and every other mobile game seems to have its own imaginary currency that somehow requires you to buy 1,200 gems to purchase something that costs 950. AI companies have enthusiastically adopted this approach. Instead of dollars, we give people credits, tokens, compute units, and whatever other imaginary currency our pricing teams come up with. And yes, these currencies can make it harder to understand what you&#8217;re actually spending. When you&#8217;re using 12 credits instead of $3, you might be less inclined to think about the real cost. But here&#8217;s where I have to defend the industry, including Lovable. Credits aren&#8217;t necessarily a sinister monetization scheme. They can solve a real problem. When your product has multiple AI models, tools, and actions that each have different underlying costs, a common currency makes it possible to package them into something resembling a subscription. Otherwise, we&#8217;d essentially be asking customers to manage a miniature AWS bill every time they wanted to accomplish something. The problem isn&#8217;t necessarily that we invented these currencies. It&#8217;s that we&#8217;ve done a bad job explaining what they&#8217;re worth. If I give someone 100 Lovable credits, I should be able to help them understand roughly what they can accomplish with them. Otherwise, I&#8217;m not really selling them 100 units of value. I&#8217;m selling them 100 units of mystery. But this is incredibly hard when you have AI doing something custom for every customer! 2. Maximum messages. Extended access. Nobody wakes up wanting 500 million tokens (sorry Muse). They wake up wanting to get something done. But we have no idea what our products can even do for our customers, since AI leaves it so open ended. So we are stuck once again. 3. You could charge per seat, per transaction, per gigabyte of storage, or per API call. These weren&#8217;t perfect measures of customer value, but they were usually predictable enough that someone could estimate what they needed before buying. AI has made that considerably more complicated. What exactly is the unit of consumption for an agent that can research your competitors, create a marketing campaign, build an application, or handle your customer support? Is it the number of messages you send? The number of tasks it completes? The time it spends working? The number of times it calls another tool? And more importantly, which of those measurements would actually make sense to the customer? Some products have it easier. ElevenLabs can charge by minutes of generated audio or agent calls. Firecrawl can translate credits into pages scraped or searches performed. Those units aren&#8217;t perfect, but at least they&#8217;re connected to something a customer can imagine needing. But the more general-purpose the product becomes, the harder this gets. An agent that can do almost anything doesn&#8217;t have one obvious unit of value. If we charge by the hour, customers might wonder why they&#8217;re paying more for a slow agent. If we charge by the task, how do we distinguish between answering an email and building an entire application? And if we charge for successful outcomes, who decides what success means? The irony is that we&#8217;re building increasingly sophisticated AI systems capable of doing work we couldn&#8217;t have imagined automating a few years ago, and then asking customers to estimate how many tokens they&#8217;ll need to use them.
