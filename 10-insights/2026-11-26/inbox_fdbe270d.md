Hey folks 👋 We all say we want to use AI for more than generating content and summarising documents. I hear it on every bootcamp cohort, and Taylor and Vinauskaitė’s 2026 report shows teams starting to move that way: evaluation and analytics has climbed from eighth to third of ten uses since 2023. But most of us struggle to know where to start. As 2026 starts to come to a close, I asked Claude (the LLM I use most) to go back through all of our chats and projects from this year and tell me how I actually use AI. The results were pretty enlightening. In two years, my use has fundamentally shifted in three ways: From faster to better. I used to use AI mostly to do the same work faster: drafts, scripts, outlines and quiz questions. Now I mostly use it to do the work better, or to do things I couldn’t do at all before: hear from every learner, test a design against the research, give every learner feedback on their own work, and see whether behaviour changed at work. From tasks to workflows. I used to use AI for one task at a time, such as writing an outline or a set of quiz questions, and put my effort into perfecting the prompt. Now I chain tasks into workflows, where each step feeds the next. I research the problem, turn the findings into design options, build a prototype of the one I choose, then test it. Standing set-ups hold my standards, templates and sources at every step. From prompting to product building. I now use the process I teach in my bootcamp to build my own AI tools and products: pick a task, write a spec, build a prototype, test it, then shelve it or scale it. Below are my top five, one for each phase of my workflow, with the tool, inputs, prompt and output for each so you can try them too. Let’s go! 🚀 The Current State of AI Use in L&D (Nov, 2026) AI use across L&D has grown fast: from about 40% of teams in 2024 to 68% in 2026 (Taylor and Vinauskaitė, 2026). Over the same period, the share of teams held back by skills or not knowing where to start fell from 49% to 36%. But what teams use it for has barely moved. Content development is still the top use, and in Synthesia’s 2027 survey, 75% named faster content creation as a gain from AI, against 11% who named better performance on the job. The teams pulling ahead use AI for different work. In Taylor and Vinauskaitė’s data, about 14% of teams say the business now asks them for different things. They are more than twice as likely as other teams to put AI in front of learners for practice, coaching and feedback (38% against 17%). I was interested in how my own use compared, so I asked Claude. Here’s the prompt I used, so you can try it too. You’ll need a chatbot that can search your past chats, such as Claude. Memory on its own doesn’t give a chatbot your full chat history. “Search the past chats you can access from this year. First, tell me what you can and can’t access. Run a separate search for each phase (analysis, design, development, implementation and evaluation) before you summarise. List the ways I use AI in my L&D work, grouped by phase. For each use, name the chat it came from and its date. Then tell me which uses make my work faster, which make it better (more accurate, rigorous or effective), and which let me do something I couldn’t do before. A use can do more than one. Finally, tell me how my use appears to have changed over time, and list the chats you looked at. Base everything only on chats you found. Don’t invent chat titles or guess at work you can’t find.” Claude’s response genuinely surprised me. When you’re deep in the work, project after project, you don’t notice how much your practice is changing. Seeing a year of it side by side, the shift was bigger than I’d realised. An extract from Claude's audit of my AI use: each use mapped to a phase and rated F (faster), B (better) or N (new). At every stage, my use of AI has moved from mainly making things faster to making them better, and sometimes doing things that weren’t possible before: Analysis: I used to ask AI to summarise a few interviews. Now it analyses every learner signal I can get, from interview transcripts and survey comments to help-desk tickets, and backs every finding with a quote. Design: I used to ask AI to draft a design. Now it builds me an evidence base in under an hour, and I test my design against it before I commit. Development: I used to ask AI to build content. Now I also build synthetic learners, with the misconceptions my real learners have, and test the content on them before anyone else sees it. Implementation: I used to ask AI to write facilitator guides. Now I build AI feedback bots that give every learner feedback on their own work, against my standards, whenever they need it. Evaluation: I used to ask AI to summarise happy-sheet comments. Now it shows me whether people actually do things differently at work, with every instance quoted. Here are the five main AI use cases which emerged from my debrief with Claude, plus prompts so you can try them too 👇 Analysis: From summarising a few interviews faster, to analysing every learner signal Most needs analysis rests on a handful of interviews and a skim of the survey comments. The loudest voices set the agenda, and the one person who spotted the real problem gets lost. Now I give AI every learner signal I can get: interview transcripts, survey comments, help-desk tickets and messages in help channels. It sorts every problem into my categories, counts them, and backs each one with quotes I can check. Why it matters: you can analyse far more of the data than you could by hand, so minority views and disagreements come to the surface. And because the categories are yours and every quote can be checked against its source, you can defend what you found. Tool: a chatbot your organisation has approved for this data, such as Microsoft 365 Copilot. Work in batches small enough to paste in full, such as 20–30 comments or three to five transcripts at a time. You’ll need: your transcripts, comments or messages, each labelled with an anonymised person ID, plus a list of categories with one line defining each. Try this: “Here are [transcripts / comments / messages], each labelled with an anonymised person ID: [attach]. Sort every problem people describe using only these categories: [list, with definitions]. A comment can belong to more than one category. For each category, give the number of different people who mention it (count each person once per category), their IDs, and up to two quotes copied word for word, with their source. Put anything that doesn’t fit under ‘Other’. List separately any point only one person made, and any points where people disagree, including positive comments that contradict a reported problem. If there are none, say so. Tell me how many records you analysed, and flag any you couldn’t read or classify. Don’t invent quotes or counts.” What you get: a table of problems by category, with counts and checked quotes, plus a list of one-off points and disagreements. ✔️ Check: in a new chat, attach the sources again, paste in the quotes and ask: “Find each quote in these sources and mark it VERIFIED, PARAPHRASED or NOT FOUND.” AI can invent quotes that look real. Then read the one-person list: that’s often where the insight is. Design: From drafting designs faster, to rapid evidence-based design Most designs start from a template, last year’s course or what the stakeholder likes. A proper evidence review took weeks, so it rarely happened. Now, before I build anything, a deep research tool gives me an evidence base in a couple of minutes: a verified summary of what it takes to produce the change I want. Then I score my own design against it. Why it matters: you find the weakest assumption in your design before you’ve spent anything on it. Your design choices are examined against the available evidence, not habit. Tool: a deep research tool: the Researcher agent in Microsoft 365 Copilot, or deep research in Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini. You’ll need: the behaviour you want and where learners will use it. Keep your draft design back for the second step. Try this: “Our goal: [learners] can [behaviour] in [work context]. Using peer-reviewed research, prioritising systematic reviews and studies of adults at work, list the design features the evidence suggests would support this change. For each, give the finding, how strong the evidence is and why, how well it fits our learners and context, and a link to the study. Only cite studies you can find and check, and say if you could only read the abstract. Also list barriers outside the learning, such as workload, incentives or manager support, that could stop the behaviour. Where the evidence is thin, indirect or mixed, say so.” What you get: a checklist of evidence-based design features, plus the barriers outside the learning. Then, in a new message, paste your draft design and ask: “Mark our design against each feature as met, partly met, not met or not enough information, with a reason. For anything partly met, say what would make it fully met. Then name the assumption in our design with the weakest evidence behind it, and suggest a quick way to test it.” Researching before the AI sees your design stops it hunting for studies that back what you’ve already planned. ✔️ Check: open the top three studies. Were they about adults at work, or students in a lab? 💡 Tip: go one step further and give the AI your real constraints. Then ask for two designs side by side, so you can see what each compromise costs: “Using the evidence above, and these constraints: [platform, budget, deadline, learner time], give me two designs. A: the optimal design the evidence supports. B: a practical design that fits our constraints. For each, say what learners do, how they practise and the feedback they get. Then list what B gives up compared with A, and the likely cost to learning of each trade-off.” Taking both to your sponsor turns “what can we afford?” into a decision they make with the trade-offs in front of them. Development: From building content to building synthetic learners Most development time goes on building content. An SME checks it for accuracy, but nobody tests it on learners until it’s live. By then it’s too late to fix the question that everyone got right for the wrong reason. Now I build synthetic learners: a novice, someone with some experience and an expert, each with the knowledge and misconceptions my real learners have. Before any real learner sees a quiz, they answer every question and explain their reasoning. In research on school maths, AI predictions like these correlated with real question difficulty at 0.75 to 0.82 (Acquaye et al., 2026). Why it matters: you spot questions that may be too easy, too hard or unclear before any real learner sits them. Most teams never check the assessment itself, so this is rigour you didn’t have before. Tool: a chatbot (Copilot, Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini). You’ll need: five to ten draft questions at a time, with their answer options, plus one or two misconceptions your learners really hold. Keep the answer key to yourself until the second step: if the AI sees it first, its “learners” will know the answers. Try this: “Here are my draft questions: [paste five to ten questions and their answer options, without the answer key]. For each question, predict how three learners would answer: a novice who [what they know now, and one or two misconceptions they hold] a learner with some experience who can [what they can already do] an expert who can [what they can do] For each learner, give the answer they would most likely choose, their confidence (low, medium or high) and a short reason. Say whether that answer comes from knowledge, a misconception, guessing or clues in the wording. If a question has no answer options, describe the likely answer in a sentence. Flag any question where you can’t tell the learners apart. Treat all of this as prediction, not measured performance.” What you get:
