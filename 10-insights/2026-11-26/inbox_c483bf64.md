## How Clay Builds GTM Agents — Jeff Barg, Head of AI

Clay 把 GTM 工具做成任何 agent 都能用的基礎設施，並分享打造 agent 的實戰取捨。

**Why** 客戶不想自己挑模型、也不想被綁在單一介面；Clay 希望無論用自家 agent 或 Claude Code，結果都差不多。

**How** 把 Sculptor 從「工具迴圈」重建成跑在 Pi harness 上的 coding agent，在雲端 sandbox 執行 Clay CLI，並用 Audiences 資料層串接 CRM 與 Gong；後端自建 BullMQ 而非 Temporal 或 LangGraph，並用 LangSmith 觀測、自製限流與暫停/續跑機制。

**What** Head of AI Jeff Barg 表示使用量已接近 50/50 分給 Clay agent 與外部 harness。以約 1,000 token 的 LinkedIn 頁面加 500 token 的 ICP 估算，用 TypeSafe 的 Jev 分類器掃完 4,000 萬家公司約 $1,500；他認為年度推論花費約 $1,000 萬–1 億美元之間才值得 post-train；Clay 9 月以 71 億美元估值募得 1.15 億美元，團隊約 600 人，並即將推出自動模型 router。

### 重點
- 以 Jev 分類器掃描 4,000 萬家公司約 $1,500（每頁約 1,000 token + ICP 約 500 token）；理想 enrichment 預算每合格 lead $1–10、每場約成 call $10–100
- Post-train 門檻：年推論花費約 $1,000 萬–1 億美元；常見錯誤是想打敗前沿模型，或花費未達門檻就開始
- Sculptor 舊版（工具迴圈、Sonnet 3.5 時代）因工具受限且看不到 CRM 資料而失敗，改為 Pi harness 上的 coding agent + Audiences 資料層；自建 BullMQ 堆疊取代 Temporal/LangGraph

Open video on YouTube ↗ Clay wants you to get about the same results whether you use its own agent or point Claude Code at Clay. Usage is already split close to 50/50 between Clay’s agent and outside harnesses. In this episode, we chat with Jeff Barg, Head of AI at Clay, about what it takes to build go-to-market infrastructure that any agent can use. Jeff joined Clay when it acquired Avenue , the ops alerting startup he cofounded. At the time, Clay had about 50 people, and Avenue had five. AI was already a big part of how Clay thought about its product, but nobody there worked on AI. Since then, the business has grown roughly 10x, and the team is up to around 600 people. In September, Clay raised $115 million at a $7.1 billion valuation . By Jeff’s napkin math, it would cost about $1,500 to check all 40 million companies in Clay’s database against your ideal customer profile with a classifier like TypeSafe’s Jev. Models have been acing Clay’s lead-qualification evals for about a year now. Jeff sees more value in using frontier models for orchestration and deep research. We also talk about why customers hate picking a model, how Clay plans to pick one for them, and why the team still runs its agents on a system it built with BullMQ, without Temporal or LangGraph. Jeff tells us what happened when OpenAI called off Clay’s ChatGPT apps launch with two days to go. We get into why customers hated Workflows at first, how agents came to build most workflows, and when post-training pays off. We also talk about why agents still can’t debug other agents, even as Clay’s agent can count its YC customers in about a minute. Sculptor started as a tool loop. Now it's a coding agent. When Jeff took over as Head of AI, one engineer was doing all of Clay’s agent work. He soon left to start his own company. Claude Code changed what Jeff wanted to build. His first launch was Sculptor, which shipped in September 2025 on models from the Sonnet 3.5 era. It ran a set of tools in a loop with a system prompt the team had spent a lot of time tuning. It was great at adding columns but terrible at booking ten qualified meetings. The evals didn’t catch much. Clay used an LLM to judge which tools Sculptor called. That missed things people actually did with it, like asking it to analyze a table or giving it an enterprise table with millions of rows. Jeff took away three lessons. Coding agents are really good at Bash and Python. Sculptor’s tools were too limited to do much by stringing them together. And the agent couldn’t see CRM data or campaign results. So Clay rebuilt Sculptor as a coding agent on the Pi harness. It runs the Clay CLI in a cloud sandbox. A new data layer called Audiences matches records across the CRM and Gong so the agent can see them together. Jump to the Sculptor discussion → Most companies post-train too early For Jeff’s first eighteen months at Clay, the team talked about training its own models. It only started to look viable in the past couple of months. Jeff sees two ways to bring costs down. First, swap in an off-the-shelf open-weight model, which is already much cheaper than Opus. Then you can post-train a smaller model on your own tools and queries until it does as well as Opus on your tasks. Jeff sees companies make two mistakes here. One is trying to beat the frontier models, which is still really hard even in a single domain. The other is starting before you spend enough on inference for the savings to matter. Jeff puts that threshold somewhere between $10 million and $100 million. Jump to the post-training discussion → $1,500 to score every company in the world Clay hasn’t put TypeSafe’s Jev into production yet, but Jeff has run the numbers. Clay’s database has about 40 million companies. A LinkedIn page is roughly a thousand tokens, and an ideal customer profile adds about 500 more. Ask a classifier whether each company is a good fit, and the bill for all 40 million comes to around $1,500. Today, most customers qualify a list of maybe 10,000 companies. At these prices, you can start with the entire market. Jeff puts a good enrichment budget at $1 to $10 per qualified lead and $10 to $100 per booked call. He thinks qualification itself is close to solved. Models have been scoring about as high as they can on Clay’s evals for roughly a year. Just about any model from the past year can pull a company’s latest funding round. Frontier models still matter for deep prospecting research and for orchestration, where Jeff says the best plays can be ten to a hundred times as effective as an average one. Jump to the Jev discussion → Customers hate the model selector Customers complain when they see Clay’s model selector. They can’t check the evals for their use case every time a new model ships, and they shouldn’t have to. Clay is about to launch an automatic router that picks a model for each task. There’s no fancy branding. Finding Apple’s CEO is a very different job from finding the CEO of an obscure local company. The selector isn’t going away. Agency owners and power users still want control. Everyone else just wants to get on with their work. Clay has to earn their trust before they’ll leave it on auto. Jump to the model routing discussion → No Temporal, no LangGraph Clay built most of its agent stack itself. It uses BullMQ to keep agent jobs running, Postgres and ClickHouse for product data, and Snowflake for analytics. Jeff isn’t sure how BullMQ will hold up if usage grows another tenfold, but it’s held up so far. Clay recently shipped 25 to 30 new integrations. Contracts and testing took longer than the engineering work. Where Clay did run into trouble was scale. People run an agent per row, so if someone starts a run on a table of 50,000 companies, inference demand spikes all at once. Clay brought in LangSmith to see what was happening. The team also built a way to throttle requests and to pause and resume work when a provider goes down or starts rate-limiting. The harder part is keeping things fair. Someone running a workflow for ten people still needs it to run when an enterprise customer is enriching a million rows. Jump to the infrastructure discussion → Built for every agent, not just Clay's Clay plans to ship a sandboxed agent that’s free to use any of Clay’s tools and building blocks. Jeff wants someone using Clay from Claude Code to get about the same results. People should be able to use Clay wherever they already work. Usage is already much closer to a 50/50 split between Clay’s agent and outside harnesses than Jeff expected. The push came from inside Clay. Its own go-to-market team lived in Cursor and wouldn’t leave it, so they asked for a CLI. The MCP team switched over to build it, and Clay’s sales team now does deal prep in Codex. So why build a first-party agent at all? Plenty of customers live in the Clay UI and want an agent right there. Jump to the agent platform discussion → Keep building when the plan blows up Jeff thinks it takes more courage to change plans at 600 people than it did at 50. Clay was a launch partner for ChatGPT apps. In the middle of the quarter, Clay put about a quarter of its engineers on the integration for a month. Two days before launch, OpenAI postponed it indefinitely. Clay kept building on the assumption that OpenAI would change its mind. Three or four days later, it did. When Clay first showed customers Workflows, they were confused. Some were upset. They wanted their spreadsheet. Now agency owners like it, and a growing share of the work that used to run in tables runs in Workflows. Most workflows are built with the CLI or the agent. CEO Kareem Amin spent five years building Clay when almost nothing was working. Jeff still sees that persistence in the company today. Jump to the ChatGPT apps discussion → Agents still can't debug agents What Jeff wants most is a model that can read a long agent trace and tell him where the run went wrong. Today’s models tend to flag whichever step took a long time, even when the mistake was somewhere else. Clay would like to pull traces from bad runs, have an agent review them and use what it finds to improve the agent automatically. That still doesn’t work, and Clay writes a lot of its skills by hand. Models still make basic mistakes, like retrying the same CLI command with the same inputs over and over. But when Jeff wanted to know how many YC companies use Clay, he asked the agent and had the answer in about a minute. A year ago, he would’ve had to scrape the batch, get access to Snowflake and cross-reference the two by hand.
