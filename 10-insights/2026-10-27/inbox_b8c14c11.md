## AI SRE: what is it, how could it change on-call – and does it work?

用 AI 在警報響起後先幫值班工程師查原因，而不是讓 AI 自己改 production。

**Why** AI agent 讓部署與 PR 頻率大致翻倍，依 incident rate = change rate × 失敗機率的推算，事故與 page 隨之增加；Rootly 資料顯示非上班時段的 page 幾乎翻倍。

**How** 主 agent 啟動調查並分派給多個 subagent，讀取 alert、dashboard、runbook、log 等資料，工程師在 Slack 的事故頻道內下指令引導；調查先行，關鍵決策仍由人做。

**What** 文章訪談 Rootly、incident.io、ResolveAI（作者是 incident.io 的投資人），並提到 OpenAI 的 Sevbot、ING 自建的 AI SRE。修復面各家做法不同：incident.io 把脈絡交給客戶既有的 coding agent，ResolveAI 簡單 PR 自己寫、大改動交出去，Rootly 的目標是 agent 起草 PR、另一個 agent 判斷後自動合併。文章也提到信任與資料存取使不少中大型公司選擇自建。

### 重點
- 可靠性簡式：incident rate = 變更頻率 C × 單次變更失敗機率 P；AI 拉高 C，所以要靠部署前驗證或從事故學習來降低 P
- 「AI SRE」多半只指加速事故調查：值班者進頻道時就拿到 AI 調查摘要，省去開八個分頁的「shovel work」
- AI SRE 需要存取 telemetry、log、版本控制等敏感資料，信任問題是中大型公司自建的主因；作者認為專責廠商長期可能比內部副專案更強

RSVP here In May 2025 at Microsoft’s annual BUILD conference, the tech giant introduced “Azure SRE Agent” with an onstage demo in which an alert fired and the AI SRE Agent went to work on identifying the root cause, and then shipped a fix, all without having to wake up a human dev oncall. Watching this demo, the whole thing felt artificial to me, and I failed to imagine any engineering team at Microsoft – or anywhere else! – letting an AI make production decisions with no human oversight that could potentially cause a worse outage. And sure enough, after last year’s BUILD I heard no more about “SRE agents” at Microsoft or elsewhere – until recently, that is. Now, I do hear it being mentioned: OpenAI built an AI SRE bot called Sevbot, and last month, an engineer at ING bank told me they’re working on their own, in-house AI SRE. Just as visibly, at tech conferences I’ve attended, AI SRE vendors’ stands have been popping up in numbers. To find out what’s going on, we reached out with Jessica to three of the most popular AI SRE startups/AI incident platforms to hear directly from them: Rootly , incident.io , and ResolveAI (note: I’m an investor in incident.io ). Thanks for all the input! Today, we cover: Why is AI SRE “hot”? Incidents and pages are trending upward, thanks to AI agents speeding up at shipping code to production. It only makes sense to invest in better oncall tooling. What does “AI SRE” mean? It’s mostly about doing incident investigation much faster, and inside of Slack. Whether “AI SREs” should make code changes is debated. Building an investigation agent. Fanning out multiple agents is common, as is the practise of engineers steering them in Slack or Teams. A “context layer” with a knowledge graph and memory is used for better investigation outcomes. Several tech companies built their own investigation agents instead of relying on vendors. Making agents accurate. Helpful approaches include verifying incident hypotheses with adversarial agents and a mix of models, as well as running evals and backtesting. However, measuring “accuracy” of AI SREs is tricky and highly specific to each company, and even engineering team. Making agents fast & affordable. Everyone wants fast, cheap, and accurate AI SREs, but there are tradeoffs. Model routing, lean prompts, and tracking cache hit rate are ways to engineer a system with the right tradeoffs for your constraints. How long until autonomous agents? The fully autonomous agents demoed on conference stages are still not a reality, but AI agents triaging pager calls – and silencing unimportant ones – is becoming more common. Interestingly, there’s not been much change in this area from how Meta operated its own systems with AI two years ago. What’s changing for on-call engineers? A new category of AI-related outages is appearing, and engineers should eventually get hit with fewer pages, but the incidents that do reach engineers will be hard. There’s a deskilling risk, and it could be smart to train oncall with incident response simulations like those from Uptime Labs . As usual, I have no affiliation with vendors mentioned in this article except for my investment in incident.io – mentioned above. I have not been paid to mention any company. 1. Why is AI SRE “hot”? A year back, there was little to no buzz about AI SREs, but that’s all been changing. The main driver seems to be that there’s a lot more code deploys. Almost all tech companies have seen their PR frequency double in a year due to AI agents. Let’s do some basic “reliability math”: i = c x p: I ncident rate = [ C hange rate] x [ P robability] C = number of changes per unit time P = probability a single change introduces failure This says that if we have few deployments (low change rate) and high confidence that each deploy works (low probability of failure), then that is a pretty stable system. But with AI agents, deploy frequency is up and the incident rate is likewise increasing at most companies. We know that C (the number of changes in a given timeframe) is up twofold or more on last year at many companies, and will probably climb higher. Data from Rootly shows that pages are also up from a year ago, and outside-business-hours pages have nearly doubled: Pages up: Higher deploy frequency responsible. What does “AI SRE” mean? “AI SRE” looks on its way to becoming the agreed term for AI-augmented oncall tooling, even though we’ve not spoken to a single person who likes it – and we agree. AI SRE vendors themselves don’t like it. When we talked with incident.io, Resolve, and Rootly for this article, the reply was that, unfortunately, they’ve not come up with anything better and so this name has come to stick instead. Alternative names proved less sticky even though they’re less inaccurate, like “autonomous reliability agents”, “automated remediation agents”, and “automated incident investigation agents.” A reason “AI SRE” is less than ideal is as simple as the fact that a site reliability engineer (SRE) does a lot more than just deal with oncall alerts! They work on making a system more reliable in its entirety and a good chunk of that work happens before and after outages. Despite all that, this article is going with the flow and using “AI SRE” because there’s not much choice, while covering oncall and incident handling tools that are AI-augmented/AI-powered. We cover more on the SRE profession and reliability engineering in a deepdive with Dave Connor, who worked at Google when the term ‘SRE’ was coined. AI SRE tools mostly help with the investigation phase of incidents. Tools like Rootly, incident.io, and ResolveAI are most helpful after an alert has fired and an engineer starts investigating. Pre-AI, this investigation phase involved: Alert details: inspect the alert and try to figure out what it means Dashboards : check custom dashboards to confirm whether there’s an alert, which systems are impacted, and where the root cause investigation should start, if so Runbooks : consult runbooks and follow the steps to remedy the issue, if it’s a common one Logs/observability : look through the logs of observability systems like Datadog, Sentry, Honeycomb, etc These days, AI tools have gotten pretty good at all these tasks if they have sufficient data access. Of the three vendors we talked with, all do their investigations with AI. When building in-house AI SRE tools, starting at the investigation phase is a no-brainer: Investigations first: engineers make the key decisions & the tool just supplies context faster Most AI SRE tools speed up the investigation of an incident. incident.io’s agent orchestrator does something similar to when an oncall engineer opens eight tabs to look through what systems might have issues: Orchestration: the main agent decides to kick off an investigation Subagents: it fans out investigation work to subagents which report on progress Heuristics: the orchestrating agent can make speculative calls, then use heuristics to decide where to go deeper There’s a lot of value in having an oncall engineer join the incident channel roughly five minutes after an alert fires, and to have a summary of investigations by AI agents. It gives the oncall dev a sense of what might be happening, instead of having to do all the “shovel work.” AI SREs typically interact with devs in Slack. At startups that use Slack for comms, Slack is also the place to coordinate incidents; even pre-AI, it was common to open a dedicated Slack channel for an incident. Most AI SRE tools have a Slack bot in the incident channel; incident.io’s bot sits in the incident channel the whole time and takes steering instructions from engineers (look at the status of a specific system, ask it to dig up specific logs, etc.) AI SRE tools require a high level of trust, which is a reason to build in-house. For these agents to be effective, they need access to telemetry, observability, logs, even the version control system because the more data they can access, the more effective they are. As Sam Newman writes in his book, ‘Building Resilient Distributed Systems’: “For tools like incident.io’s AI SRE to work, they need access to information. This means that they will need broad access to a lot of valuable data if you want them to be effective. If the agent can’t see your telemetry data, then it can’t slice and dice that data for you. If it can’t look at your source code repository, then it can’t spot recent commits that may have caused the issue. This does mean that you need a high degree of trust in the vendor creating and running the agent.” But how much do you trust a third-party vendor with all your data; potentially even a readonly version of your sanitized production database, which could come in handy for some investigations? The larger the company, the less likely that access to highly sensitive data will be granted. The trust issue is a major driver in why many mid-size-and-above companies build their own AI SRE tools. Even if a vendor offers zero data retention (ZDR), there’s still a risk that its system could be compromised and leak information. Also, a vendor offering ZDR could be at a disadvantage when improving the system, compared to an in-house AI SRE tool that it’s possible to train on the data. Our take is that AI SRE vendors are likely to become more capable than home-grown solutions because AI SRE startups obsess about better tooling fulltime; success depends on it. At larger companies, an AI SRE tool is more of a novel side project; engineers initially get excited but then move on to newer projects. Should AI SREs only mitigate, or also remediate? Once the root cause analysis of an outage is done, and the AI SRE agent has helped to mitigate it, is the work complete? On one hand, the AI SRE tool typically also has access to the codebase, so should be able to come up with code-fix suggestions. On the other hand, pre-AI, you hardly ever saw an SRE team raise pull requests to fix the root causes of outages because they didn’t have the context or expertise for systems someone else built. But with AI, context can be passed on and expertise in writing code is pretty common for AI agents. We asked the three AI SRE startups how they approach writing code to fix root causes, post-incident. They have different ways: Handing off PRs to coding agents. incident.io is focused on passing incident context to engineers’ existing coding agents. The company built and demoed autonomous PR creation at its annual conference last year, but cofounder Chris Evans told us that customers generally prefer to use their own coding setups. The focus is therefore on giving agents the context they need to help engineers implement a fix. A hybrid approach at ResolveAI. The tool writes simple PRs itself and hands over larger changes to the customer’s coding agents. Intend for agents to autonomously merge PRs at Rootly. Their vision is to get to a point where their agent drafts a PR, and another agent judges whether it’s safe to merge, and when deemed safe, the merge happens automatically.
