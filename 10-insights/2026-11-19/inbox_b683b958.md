## The 4 highest-impact things L&D can build with AI right now

AI 能直接生成可互動網頁，L&D 應依學習研究挑互動類型，而非多做點擊式測驗。

**Why** 多數 L&D 仍用 AI 加速既有格式；原文引調查，超過半數人希望 AI 改善學習者體驗，實際做到的只有 17%。

**How** 作者依教育研究挑出 simulation、self-explanation、case comparison、worked examples 四類，並用兩段 prompt：先列出文件事實，再據此生成單一 HTML 檔。

**What** 測試 Claude Opus 5.5、GPT-6.2 Astra、Gemini 4 Argon 共 8 次；邏輯與規則表現佳，但多數無法做到頁面內呼叫 AI 批改自由作答；四類效果量分別為 g=0.85、0.55、d=0.50、0.48，測驗為 0.37。

### 重點
- 原文引研究效果量：Simulation g=0.85、Self-explanation g=0.55、Case comparison d=0.50、Worked examples g=0.48；測驗為 0.37（Ruitenburg et al., 2026）
- 三模型 8 次測試中，有 7 次正確列出文件事實（第 8 次 Gemini 加入了文件沒有的細節）；三者都沒做出在各種環境都能運作、頁面內呼叫 AI 批改的版本

Hey folks 👋 When I talk to L&D professionals at the moment, one thing is very clear: most of us want to use AI to innovate the learner’s experience , but instead th evast majoiry of us are still using AI to make more click-next courses. Survey data says the same. In 2026, more than half of us in L&D say the gain we’re hoping for from AI is a better experience for learners (Synthesia, 2026), but in practice only 17% of us are (Taylor and Vinauskaitė, 2026). A few weeks ago I looked at one answer: building learning games . This week, in prep for an upcoming bootcamp session on AI-enhanced delivery, I went deeper and wider and asked: what does the research say are the most effective ways to innovate the learner’s experience, and how much of this could LLMs help us build? A 14-page safety guide turned into an interactive simulation designed to improve conceptual understanding and skills, using Claude Opus 5.5 In this week’s post, I share what I found: what pedagogical research says about what sorts of buildable interactives work best, and how to build each one with the LLM you already have. I share the prompts, the models and the examples from my tests so you can (finally!) start to innovate the learner’s experience with AI. Let’s dive in! 🚀 AI Can Now Build, Not Just Write Originally, a chat tool like Claude or ChatGPT gave you text. Ask for an interactive and you got pieces of code that an engineer then had to assemble. That changed in the second half of 2024, when Anthropic launched Artifacts alongside Claude 3.5 Sonnet which didn’t only write the code but could also build it. OpenAI and gemini added the same build capability shortly after in late 2024 and early 2025. Today, things look very different. Which models can code-then-build: all the big chat tools. Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini each do it, and so do many others. Earlier this week, one public leaderboard, WebDev Arena, ranked no fewer than 161 AI models on their ability to build web pages. TLDR: building is now part of what AI models do. What they can build: in 2024, people were making simple diagrams, charts and small games. Today, the same tools make things with moving parts and rules: animations you step through, 3D models you can turn, simulations that work out what happens next, and pages that mark what a learner types. They draw all of it in code, with no image files. You say what you want in plain English, attach a document, and get back a page that works. If you spend any time on LinkedIn, X or TikTok, you’ll have seen the result: post after post of animations, 3D models and “interactives”, each built from a single prompt. Which models build best? Based on more than a million votes on WebDev Arena, where developers compare what each model builds, the top five ranked models for building in November 2026 are: Claude Fable 5.2 (Anthropic) Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic) GPT-6.2 Astra (OpenAI) Claude Sonnet 5.5 (Anthropic) Gemini 4 Argon & Carbon (Google) Where they’re strong, and where they struggle: to see for myself, I tested three models: Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic), ChatGPT GPT-6.2 Astra (OpenAI) and Gemini 4 Argon (Google, one tier below its top model). Each got the same prompt and the same public safety document, and I scored the first thing each one built. Across eight tests, a pattern emerged: They’re strong at logic and rules. Scoring a lifting assessment, deciding whether a ladder slips, checking whether a learner has answered before showing feedback: Claude and ChatGPT got these right first time. They read the source accurately, mostly. All three listed the facts in each document correctly in seven of eight tests. In the eighth, Gemini added a detail the guide doesn’t contain. They struggle with AI inside the page. Marking a learner’s free-text answer needs the page to call an AI model. None of the three built a version that works everywhere (see #2 in section 3). They don’t protect the learner’s thinking unless told to. More on this in section 4. Why it matters for L&D: most of the AI we use speeds up formats we already had. In Taylor and Vinauskaitė’s survey, 77.6% of us use AI for content development, and the AI tools built for L&D (course authoring, video, voice, translation) give us a course, a video or a quiz faster. Coding models change which formats are open to us. The learner makes a decision and the page responds. That used to need a developer and a budget. Now it needs a document, a prompt and the LLM you already have. Building Learning Tools with AI Not all “interaction” is created equal in learning experience design. A click, a drag or a spinning 3D model can keep a learner busy without helping them learn and perhaps even distracting them from suvstantive learning. Lately I’ve seen a LOT of people adding AI-generated interactives to their courses, because they can. Think: quizzes, more drag-and-drops, animations and, above all, more simulations than ever before. Every interaction we build costs the learner attention. If what they’re doing on screen isn’t what they need to learn, that attention goes on operating the page, and less is left for the content. In the wrong place or at the wrong moment, an interactive adds friction and reduces learning: A quiz builds recall, and little else. In one university study, multiple-choice questions without feedback gave no benefit at all ( Greving & Richter, 2022 ). A drag-and-drop adds a movement. When that movement isn’t part of the skill, it can get in the way: for clinical exam skills, a simple click version beat the drag version ( Kalet et al., 2012 ). An animation helps most when the thing being learned is itself a movement ( Höffler & Leutner, 2007 ). For an idea that doesn’t move, a still image does the job. A simulation asks the most of the learner. People new to a topic learned more from simulations that came with worked examples ( Chernikova et al., 2020 ). Dropped in without that support, a simulation leaves a beginner guessing. So before building anything, we must ask: what does the research say are the most powerful ways to innovate the learner’s experience, for engagement and for outcomes? The answer depends and varies most on the goal. The more important question is: what should we build.....? LLMs can now build a broad range of pedagogically powerful interactives, from 3D models the learner can turn & inspect to step-by-step animations, labelled diagrams and worked examples. These six were built by Claude Opus 5.5, each from a public source document. The Top Four Power-Builds for L&D The table above lists everything we could build with code-building models to enhance the learning experience. The more important question is: what should we build, and where should we focus our efforts (and tokens)? To answer this, I went back to the pedagogy research and asked: which of these interactives have the most potential impact on learning outcomes? The answer is that four builds in particular stand out has having most potential when it comes to positively impacting learner engagement and outcomes: Simulation: g = 0.85. Use it when the goal is to make good decisions in complex situations. Simulations had a large effect on complex skills such as diagnosing and deciding (Chernikova et al., 2020). But the support has to fit the learner: beginners needed worked examples alongside, and experienced learners needed reflection. Self-explanation: g = 0.55. Use it when the goal is to understand why, not just what. Learners prompted to explain causes and connections in their own words learned more, across subjects (Bisra et al., 2018). But bolted onto worked examples in mathematics, the prompts reduced the benefit (Barbieri et al., 2023). Case comparison: d = 0.50. Use it when the goal is to recognise what matters in a situation. Learners who compared cases side by side learned more than those who studied them one at a time (Alfieri et al., 2013). But the effect was weaker when the principle came first, and when the test came later. Worked examples: g = 0.48. Use it when the goal is to carry out a procedure step by step. Studying worked solutions improved performance in mathematics (Barbieri et al., 2023). But this is for people new to the task: experienced learners did better with less help (Tetzlaff et al., 2025). For comparison, the interactive I see built most with AI in 2026, the quiz, has an effect size of 0.37 in real classrooms (Ruitenburg et al., 2026). That’s a real gain, but notably smaller than any of the four learner interactions above. Quizzes can also backfire: multiple-choice questions show learners wrong answers, and some learners later remember those wrong answers as facts (Roediger & Marsh, 2005). The risk is highest when the questions are hard and there is no feedback. In one study of young children, those conditions cost more learning than the quiz added (Marsh, Fazio & Goswick, 2012). As the notes above suggest, the four “power-build’ tools I’ve identified here aren’t risk-free either. Like any tool, each can fail if it’s designed poorly or used for the wrong goal. But used well, each has more potential to improve learning than our standard quiz. So, I decided to have a go at building them. Here’s how it went, and how to build them yourself. The Four Power-Builds in Action Each prompt below is the second of two messages. The first asks the model to list the facts in your document. Both are at the end of this section. #1: Simulation What it is: the learner sets the conditions, runs the situation and sees what happens. Use it when: people must make a judgement that depends on several conditions, and practising for real is dangerous, slow or costly. For example: setting up a ladder, choosing an exclusion zone, handling an escalating incident. Why it works: simulations had the largest effect of any build I looked at, for complex skills such as diagnosing, deciding and planning. Support matters: learners with little prior knowledge did better with examples, and experienced ones with reflection (Chernikova et al., 2020). So if your learners are new to the topic, show them one worked example before the first run. Don’t use it when: there’s no decision to make. A simulation of a topic with nothing to judge is an animation with extra steps and more cogitive load. Source document: concrete guidance with thresholds or limits. I used the Ladder Association’s Safe Use of Ladders and Stepladders (LA455) . Best model: Claude Opus 5.5 on high effort, or ChatGPT [GPT-6.2 Astra]. Example Prompt to Try: Prompt 1, with your document attached: “Read the attached document. List every rule, measurement, step and condition in it that bears on [the decision, such as setting up a leaning ladder], with page numbers. List anything the decision needs that the document doesn’t give. Don’t build anything yet.” Prompt 2, once you’ve checked the list against the document: “Using the facts you listed, build a single web page simulation. The learner sets [the variables, such as ladder angle and floor surface] and runs it. Start every control at a neutral position that isn’t the right answer. Before the run, show no rule values, hints or verdict. The outcome must follow the document’s rules. If you add a physics model, state its assumptions on screen and never let it contradict the document. After each run, ask: ‘Which factor decided your result, and why?’ Then show the document’s guidance. Open with one worked example, then three rounds with changed conditions. Use only the facts you listed; label anything you invent as made up. If two of my instructions conflict, or one would give the answer away, stop and ask before building.
