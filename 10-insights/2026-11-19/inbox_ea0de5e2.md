## How did Lovable grow so fast?

Lovable 的爆發成長沒有祕訣，而是身處高速成長市場，加上一艘能承受亂流的組織。

**Why** 作者（Lovable 成長負責人）認為這種規模的成長不是單一人或單一職能能做到，也不是任何 growth playbook 能複製。

**How** 提出 Whitewater Framework：市場是河流（引擎），公司要造一艘能存活的筏。筏的部件包含產品（充氣筒）、信任文化（防撕布料）、出貨速度（打氣筒）、AI 自動化（自動排水地板），團隊面則有小團隊、個人化社群與大膽押注。

**What** 文中數字：ARR 超過 6 億美元、每週新增 120 萬個專案、用 Lovable 做出的 app 每月超過 10 億次造訪。作者延伸自 Lenny 與 Dan Hockenmaier 的 Racecar Growth Framework，並引用 James Currier 的「找到快速流動的水」。原文在中途被截斷，後半段（個人社群、大膽押注）未涵蓋。

### 重點
- Lovable 數字：ARR 超過 6 億美元、每週 120 萬個新專案、月訪問超過 10 億次
- Whitewater Framework：河流決定成長上限，筏決定你能捕捉多少；筏含產品、信任文化、出貨速度、AI 自動化
- Lovable 新人入職要先找出自己工作中重複、可預測的部分並自動化；優化工作分散到各職能，成長團隊更專注發明新體驗與成長迴圈

Lovable turns two this week, and the numbers are unreal: Over $600 million in ARR, 1.2 million new projects created every week, and more than 1 billion monthly visits to apps built with Lovable. Yeah, I’m responsible for growth at Lovable, but I was also responsible for growth at Dropbox, SurveyMonkey, and Amplitude. They did great but they didn’t grow like this. But even more than that... Sad news, everybody: There’s no secret silver bullet. No ‘growth playbook’ is going to get you these results. Because Lovable-level results aren’t something that one person or even one function can deliver. The way that Lovable has grown has been totally different from anything else I’ve ever experienced. So I’m proposing an extension on the legendary Racecar Growth Framework. I call it the Whitewater Framework : In fast-moving markets, the river creates the opportunity for extraordinary growth. Your job is to build a raft that can capture that growth, steer around the obstacles you can avoid, and survive the ones you can’t. James Currier described this as Finding the Fast Moving Water and that’s the mental model I’ve used ever since. You can be smart, you can work hard, you can do lots of smart things, but I don’t think it’s possible to grow at this kind of rate unless you’re in a category that is growing super fast. When I was at Miro, we were also in fast-moving waters during COVID. The whole world was working remotely and everyone needed virtual collaboration space. And then the pandemic ended and the river slowed down. When I was at Amplitude, it was a steady-moving river. Dropbox was more of a sea with a little current. Well Lovable is navigating raging rapids. This is obvious to me because growth techniques that never worked for me before have started to work. The AI category is just expanding so fast that it’s carrying us along with it. But... clearly not every AI company is successful. There are all these companies in these rapidly moving waters, but they’re not all catching the current. Or some of them caught it, but not in a good way. The river determines how fast you could grow. Your raft determines how much of that growth you actually capture. And just because the current is moving fast doesn’t mean everyone makes it downstream. So what separates the companies that ride the current from those that get swept away? That’s where the Whitewater Framework comes in. The whitewater framework One of my all-time favorite models for growth is Lenny & Dan Hockenmaier’s Racecar Growth Framework . If you’re not familiar, the idea is that building a fast-growing company is like building a fast-driving car. Every part has a key job: the Growth Engine that drives the core loops, various forms of Fuel to keep the engine running, Lubricants to improve efficiency, and occasional Turbo Boosts to provide temporary acceleration. This one gets better the more time you spend with it, and I’ve recommended it so many times. But as I’ve been living in the bizarro world of Lovable Growth for the last year, I’ve realized a different metaphor may be helpful. Because it hasn’t felt like we’ve been driving a finely tuned performance racing vehicle. Growth in an AI-native org feels more like paddling a whitewater raft down a raging river. The river is the engine When you’re in fast moving water, the river is actually your engine. You’re not paddling for speed. You just need to survive. You’re avoiding the giant obstacles and bracing yourself because you are definitely going to hit some of those rocks really hard. There are two parts. Building a raft that can survive in these conditions: Inflated tubes: Amazing core product Rip-proof fabric: Culture of trust Pump: Shipping velocity Self-bailing floor: AI automation that handles grunt work And how the crew works: Small crew: Lean, flexible org Individual Paddles: Personality-driven social Steering paddle: Big bets 1. Inflated tubes = An amazing product that people love This is the one that makes everything else possible: you need a fucking amazing product that people will tell other people about. They have to love using it, understand how it works, and actually trust it enough to rely on it and tell other people about it. Without this you sink, you’re dead, it’s over. For AI-native companies, everything is happening so fast that you can’t always get marketing or comms or someone to present the product in the right way at the right time. The person is just going to hear about it online somewhere or (ideally) get it texted to them by a friend, and they’re just going to try it. So it all comes back to the product: Does it work? Is it good? Do people want to get onboard with you? Obviously I didn’t create this product. I was given the gift of this lovable Lovable product to grow. And boy, has this gift just kept on giving. Because the degree of deep, constant care that goes into improving every element of the Lovable product experience makes bouncing along down the chaotic river of AI growth wayyyy easier. 2. Rip-proof fabric = Culture of trust The first time you bonk up against a rock (and you will), think about what’s keeping everything together for your company. I truly hate to say it but... I’m pretty sure it’s your company culture. The idea of ‘culture’ in most orgs makes me wanna throw up a little. It’s usually pure fluff, lip service you hear a lot about when a company is recruiting you or maybe there’s a long list of company values that no one can remember. ‘This team is a family’ blah blah blah. But there’s one thing that is immediately obvious in any company you walk into: Do people here trust each other? Like, will people be totally honest about what’s wrong? Can we actually talk about why that thing didn’t work out, or do we need to do the I-don’t-want-to-get-blamed-for-this dance and have a retro that doesn’t actually lead to any changes? This is maybe the most obvious with how a company treats its employees: Do people have the autonomy to make important decisions? Are you increasing what an IC can do on her own, or just pretty much screaming ‘We don’t trust you’ at them by way of your 15-step approval sequence? I’ve seriously never seen a culture like Lovable’s: Autonomy and individual agency and people just going out and doing stuff. And clearly that didn’t happen by accident. This depends on a lot of decisions: the type of people that you hire, how you equip them, and the level of accountability and ownership you give them, to keep them energized. All that stuff is how you build a culture of trust . Not easy to do and yeah once you tear that fabric, good luck repairing it. I don’t believe that you can revive a company where the culture has gone sour. Trust me, I’ve been there, seen that: people are resentful and irritable and mostly just roll their eyes whenever a new opportunity comes up. You can get as excited as you want, but you’re sinking whether you know it or not. 3. Pump = Shipping velocity A raft is always losing air. Even without the collisions, it just sort of drains over time. Just like a product that has to improve all. the. time. AI-native products have to constantly evolve in order for that word-of-mouth loop to keep growing. You can never stop launching. You have to constantly make noise in the market and constantly give people something to talk about. Rapid, transparent updates build trust. It seems like we’ve got a massive update or announcement going out every week, so of course it’s not going to look like someone polished it for several months. Sometimes the “visuals” for major announcements are just screenshots or a selfie from the team. While the Racecar approach has some limited Turbo Boosts to temporarily accelerate things, in AI world it feels like you have to keep adding these little bursts just to keep up. It’s so different from the cadence (and development lifecycle!) of a traditional software company. We still have some big launch moments, which gives us an extra big burst of air. Whether it’s a product launch or a fundraise or a big new hire, there’s lots of material to keep pumping, but you have to realize that it’s part of the maintenance now, not just a rare thing. Always be launching! 4. Self-bailing floor = Automate with AI all the draining stuff Okay, I didn’t actually know what this was called on a raft, lol. But these boats would get flooded pretty fast if they couldn’t drain water on their own. And in fast-moving companies, if you can’t figure out how to reduce the repetitive grunt work, that’s all your team will ever do. You’ll be so busy keeping the existing business running that you’ll have no capacity to take advantage of the opportunities (currents!) flying past you. At Lovable, people spend little time on things that could be automated with ai. In fact, when you join Lovable, one of the first things you’re expected to do is figure out which parts of your job are repetitive and predictable, and automate them away. And this has fundamentally changed how I do growth. In my previous jobs, an enormous amount of my time went into optimization. Optimize this funnel, improve that conversion rate, fix this broken experience. And don’t get me wrong, that stuff is important! But what’s fascinating is that at Lovable, I’m doing dramatically less of it, yet it’s still happening. AI and automation are taking over more of the incremental improvements, and the responsibility for optimization is distributed across functions rather than sitting with a dedicated growth team. Which means I get to spend a lot more of my time on the stuff that actually requires human creativity and judgment. Inventing new experiences, figuring out new growth loops, and taking bigger swings. I think this is one of the biggest changes AI-native companies are bringing to growth. We’re moving from a world where humans spend most of their time maintaining and incrementally improving existing growth engines to one where machines can handle more of that work, freeing humans to invent the next ones. Because when you’re navigating rapids, the last thing you want is your entire crew spending all their time bailing water out of the boat. 5. When things are moving fast, do you really want to have to keep a few dozen people in the loop just because someone was too lazy to take them off the RACI chart?
