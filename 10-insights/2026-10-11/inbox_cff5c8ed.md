## How agents unlock new markets

AI 代理讓買賣雙方能低成本逐案協商，使過去因談判太貴而從未發生的交易變得划算。

**Why** 傳統市場靠標準化報價降低成本，但買賣雙方真正的需求與彈性被簡化，許多可行交易因探索與協商成本太高而沒發生。

**How** 買方與賣方各有 agent，了解目標與限制，並能連結營運能力（如保留產能、承諾交期）與授權取捨，跨組織互相發現、交換提案、驗證後共同建構原本不存在的報價。

**What** Walmart 用 AI 與 10 萬+供應商個別協商；Honeywell 完成 2,500+ 次自主談判、涵蓋 5 億美元以上支出；Circle Logistics 透過 HappyRobot 讓 18% 貨運訂單免人工完成；Google Direct Offers 讓 Gemini 為購物者組合客製優惠；Anthropic Project Deal 讓 69 名員工的 agent 達成 186 筆真實協議、價值逾 $4,000。作者主張潛在需求釋放（如招聘、旅遊）才是 agentic market 的更大機會。

### 重點
- Honeywell 以 AI 完成 2,500+ 次自主供應商談判、涵蓋逾 5 億美元支出；Circle Logistics 18% 貨運訂單無人介入完成
- Anthropic Project Deal：69 名員工由 agent 代表，達成 186 筆真實交易、總值逾 $4,000
- 三項限制正在鬆動：談判變便宜、報價可客製、交易更易協調執行；關鍵是 agent 需連結營運能力與決策權限

This post is Part 2 of a series titled Agents, and their better half. You can read Part 1 here. Walmart&#8217;s ability to manage its vast supplier network is one of its greatest competitive strengths, but has also so far been a source of considerable complexity. With more than 100,000 suppliers, it was too expensive to negotiate individual commercial terms with every one of them. Many suppliers operated under largely standardized agreements, even though better arrangements might have been possible for both sides. The problem was the cost of discovering and negotiating such arrangements - particularly with smaller suppliers, where the potential benefits often didn&#8217;t justify the time and effort required to negotiate individually. Walmart now uses AI to conduct these negotiations - engaging suppliers individually, exploring trade-offs involving prices, payment terms, and other commercial conditions, and identifying arrangements that work for both parties. This might sound as little more than procurement automation. But there&#8217;s an important shift here - a previously uneconomical activity now becomes economical. Commercial arrangements that were previously too expensive to negotiate became economically worthwhile. The underlying needs and capabilities of the buyers and suppliers hadn&#8217;t necessarily changed. What changed was the cost of discovering and agreeing on terms that worked for both sides. This is the larger opportunity in the emerging agent economy. As I mentioned in part 1 of this series, much of the excitement around AI agents focuses on helping buyers find better offers and negotiate better prices. What if the bigger opportunity is not simply making existing transactions more efficient, but making transactions possible that would otherwise never happen? When agents meet their better halves In the first article in this series, Agents, and their better half , I argued that much of today&#8217;s agent economy remains lopsided. We are building increasingly capable agents to represent consumers and buyers, but the other side of the market has yet to undergo a comparable transformation. No matter how intelligent the buyer&#8217;s agent becomes, it remains largely limited to choosing among the offers that sellers have already made available. When sellers are also represented by agents, they can understand what a buyer is trying to achieve, evaluate what they are capable of providing, and explore different ways of reaching an agreement. For one, buyers and sellers would find each other more effectively, negotiate better terms, and complete transactions with less effort. But there is a larger possibility. When both sides can understand each other&#8217;s needs and constraints, they are no longer limited to negotiating over what has already been offered. They can begin to construct offers that didn&#8217;t exist before the interaction. This takes us beyond the simplistic promise of agents making buying and selling more efficient. The real market inefficiency Consider a manufacturer looking to purchase 5,000 units of a specialized component. The company specifies its requirements, suppliers respond, but none can meet all the requirements. The manufacturer concludes that a suitable supplier isn&#8217;t available. But what if a workable transaction was possible all along? Perhaps the manufacturer only needs 2,000 units immediately and can accept the remaining 3,000 a month later. Perhaps one of its specifications could be relaxed without affecting the product&#8217;s performance. The transaction never happens because neither side has fully explored what the other is willing or able to do. What buyers ask for and what sellers offer are often simplified versions of what they actually need and what they could provide. This is how most markets have traditionally operated. Employers publish job descriptions, hotels offer predefined room categories and rates, manufacturers publish product specifications, and service providers sell standardized packages. Buyers compare the available options and choose the closest fit. There are good reasons for this. It would be enormously expensive for every business to explore every possible combination of products, services, prices, and contractual terms with every potential customer. Standardized offerings make it easier for buyers and sellers to find each other, compare alternatives, and complete transactions. But this efficiency comes with a trade-off. The offers available in a market represent only a portion of what sellers are capable of providing. As a result, many potentially valuable transactions never happen - not because buyers and sellers are fundamentally incompatible, but because discovering and constructing an agreement is too expensive. Agents could make a much bigger difference here. By reducing the cost of exploring alternatives, negotiating trade-offs, and coordinating agreements, they could make transactions viable that are unrealised under conventional market mechanisms. This is already playing out in various ways The changing economics of negotiation and offer construction Individual negotiation is becoming economically viable at a much larger scale . Like Walmart, Honeywell has also used AI in procurement to negotiate with suppliers, completing more than 2,500 autonomous negotiations covering over $500 million in spending. These negotiations involve different combinations of payment terms, rebates, and discounts. Commercial relationships that previously received little individual attention can now be negotiated on their own merits, without requiring procurement teams to manage every discussion. Second, offers are beginning to be adapted to what individual buyers want . Google&#8217;s Direct Offers allow merchants to specify eligible products, promotions, and commercial limits, which Gemini can use to construct offers tailored to a shopper&#8217;s particular search. Rather than simply finding an existing discount, the system could assemble a combination of products and incentives that makes sense for that buyer. This is still an emerging capability, but it illustrates how offers could increasingly be constructed in response to demand rather than being determined entirely in advance. Finally, agents are beginning to negotiate and complete transactions without continuous human involvement . Circle Logistics, a US freight broker, has deployed AI agents that communicate with carriers, negotiate transportation rates, and confirm bookings. According to its technology provider, HappyRobot , 18% of the company&#8217;s freight bookings are now completed without human intervention. Transactions that previously required calls, exchanges of information, and individual negotiations can increasingly be handled automatically. In Anthropic &#8217;s Project Deal experiment, 69 employees were represented by AI agents in a small marketplace. The agents identified opportunities to buy and sell, proposed prices, negotiated with one another, and concluded 186 real agreements involving goods worth more than $4,000. The experiment was small, but it demonstrated that agents representing different individuals could negotiate directly and arrive at agreements without requiring their principals to manage the process. Taken together, these developments suggest that three constraints that have historically shaped market activity are beginning to weaken. Negotiation is becoming cheaper, offers are becoming more adaptable, and transactions are becoming easier to coordinate and execute. When the cost of individual negotiation and coordination falls, these constraints can begin to relax. With that, a larger opportunity emerges when these capabilities allow buyers and sellers to discover and construct agreements that would otherwise never have been reached. Beyond negotiation For this larger opportunity to materialize, agents need to move beyond working with the standard offers to uncover what each party would actually be willing and able to do to make a transaction possible. We can already see how this works in traditional real estate markets, where real estate agents represent buyers and sellers. A buyer may begin with a specific budget and list of requirements, while a seller may have an asking price and certain conditions for the sale. But experienced real estate agents know that these are often starting positions rather than fixed boundaries. A buyer may be willing to stretch their budget and a seller may accept a lower price in exchange for a faster transaction. The role of the agents is to uncover the priorities, constraints, and flexibility on both sides, and use that understanding to construct an agreement that works for everyone. A property that initially appears unsuitable may become a viable purchase once these possibilities are explored. For agentic markets to unlock their larger potential, AI agents need to perform this kind of representation and negotiation at a much broader scale. On the buyer&#8217;s side, an agent needs to understand not just what the buyer is asking for, but what the buyer is trying to achieve. A job seeker looking for a particular role may be willing to consider a different position if it offers greater responsibility. A traveller searching for a particular flight may be willing to change departure dates in exchange for greater flexibility. These possibilities are often lost when demand is reduced to search queries, purchasing specifications, or standard requests for quotations. On the seller&#8217;s side, the transformation is even more significant. A supplier&#8217;s agent needs to understand not just what the company currently offers, but what it is capable of providing. A hotel may be able to offer a different combination of services and conditions. An employer may be willing to reshape a role around an exceptional candidate. Agents must also have the ability to act on this knowledge. A supplier agent that can explain alternative delivery options but cannot reserve production capacity or commit to a delivery schedule has limited ability to construct a new transaction. Similarly, a buyer agent that can express preferences but cannot authorize acceptable trade-offs remains largely a recommendation system. The larger transformation requires connecting agents to the operational capabilities and commercial decisions of the organizations they represent. And these capabilities must be able to interact across organizational boundaries. Buyer and seller agents need mechanisms to discover one another, exchange proposals, verify what is being offered, and arrive at agreements that both parties can execute. The transaction is no longer simply selected from what the market already offers. It is constructed through the interaction between what buyers need and what sellers can provide.
