## Visualizing the AI Education Debate

把 AI 教育爭議拆成 12 個模組地圖，指出多數立場會循環回到先前被取代的位置。

**Why** 作者觀察到 AI 教育討論常在一兩個單點解方之間輪替，教師得到的答案往往只能部分適用。

**How** 作者與 Claude、ChatGPT 協作，將辯論拆為 access、authenticity、AI literacy、teaching profession、governance、evidence、equity、data、curriculum、beneficiaries、development、pace 等 12 個模組，並標出各模組的循環路徑。

**What** 例如 access 模組中，禁止、限制、開放三種立場各自會把壓力推向另一種；governance 模組指出一致性與地方彈性相互取捨。作者說明這份地圖仍在初步階段，希望讀者檢驗，核心問題是「哪些論證改變辯論結構，哪些只是重新生成它」。

### 重點
- 12 個模組涵蓋 access、authenticity、evidence、equity、data、pace 等議題，每個模組都列出循環式立場
- access 模組：禁止會推向重開、開放而無教學重設會推向限制，限制則需要教師難以一致定義的變數
- 作者自陳地圖仍「embryonic」，邀請讀者指出被扁平化的分歧與遺漏的路徑

Thanks, as always, for reading. Each of these pieces takes hours to research and prepare, and if the work is useful to you, consider becoming a paid subscriber. This is a good time of the year to lean into AI-responsive instructional redesign, and I would point you toward my recent book as a guidebook for the work, written for teachers and students both. The overall shape and trajectory of the AI education debate concerns me. On Substack and other social platforms, the discourse is dominated by one-point solutions. &#8220;Cultivate AI literacy.&#8221; &#8220;Redesign instruction.&#8221; &#8220;Track metacognition.&#8221; &#8220;Grade process.&#8221; &#8220;Detect student use of AI.&#8221; &#8220;Return to the analog classroom.&#8221; Every few months, the cycle repeats. Positions give way to counterpositions, which eventually circle back to versions of the positions they displaced. In the end, teachers are left with solutions that apply only partially to their contexts and often yield limited results. The wicked problem framework is an attractive way to grapple with these complexities. The debate unfolds across problems that resist stable definition. We cannot adequately define the problem because the problem changes as we act upon it. Every solution gives rise to new complications. We lack meaningful control groups. We cannot definitively measure the impact of solutions from inside the problem space. The framework gives us good reason not to expect swift progress toward lasting solutions. One-point solutions cannot account for the full complexity of the landscape, so imperfect, provisional, and contextualized responses may have to suffice. As attractive as the framework is, though, I worry about the way it may inadvertently keep us locked inside the rhetoric of thesis and antithesis. If we are truly grappling with wicked problems, why strive toward common solutions at all? What incentive do we have to work with each other when the problems will only ever admit local, contextualized responses? At its worst, wickedness can become its own stopping point: an explanation for disagreement rather than a means of understanding its structure. And yet, I cannot help wondering whether, from inside the context of a wicked problem, we can actually diagnose the problem as truly wicked. I am searching, in essence, for structural breaks: places where the debate stops reproducing itself and opens onto a less fiery, more reflective discourse. Where do apparently opposed positions share assumptions? Where do different arguments run into the same dead ends? Which proposed solutions merely route us back to earlier questions? And where might the structure of the debate itself reveal possibilities for greater dialogue and collaborative engagement? In this article, I turn to visualization as a method for pursuing those questions. I have spent far too many hours working with Claude and ChatGPT to convert my notes and thinking about the overall shape of the debate&#8212;and the local arguments nested inside it&#8212;into a series of visual maps. I am looking for common structures, recurring circularities, shared premises, and openings that are harder to see when each argument is encountered on its own. The work is still embryonic. I am sharing it now because I want readers to help test the map rather than encounter it later as a finished taxonomy. Where have I flattened a real disagreement? Where have I mistaken a contingent problem for a structural one? What important pathway is missing? And, most importantly, where does the debate genuinely break out of its recurring loops? To frame your engagement with the maps that follow, keep one question in mind: Which arguments change the structure of the debate, and which merely regenerate it? Module 1: The Access Question. The access debate appears to offer three distinct positions (ban, restrict, or open), but each trajectory generates pressure toward one of the others. A ban limits sanctioned use in school while students continue to use AI elsewhere, which can eventually create pressure to reopen access. Open access without instructional redesign increases concerns about student offloading and produces pressure to restrict. Restriction appears to occupy the pragmatic middle, but only by specifying variables (grade, tool, task, population) that schools have limited evidence for and uneven capacity to define consistently. The result is less a spectrum of stable policy choices than a recurring circuit. Module 2: The Authenticity Question. The authenticity debate turns on what counts as legitimate student contribution when AI is involved. One position treats AI involvement as a breach of academic integrity and responds with disclosure rules, detection, or prohibition. Another treats AI as a possible collaborator and shifts attention toward the quality of the student&#8217;s judgment, revision, and decision-making. A third route tries to escape the authenticity problem through assessment redesign, but that move can become unstable as AI capabilities expand into newly redesigned tasks. The debate repeatedly returns to the same unresolved question: what evidence of learning should the assignment make visible? Module 3: The AI Literacy Question. The AI literacy debate begins with broad agreement that students need to understand how these systems work and how to use them responsibly. The disagreement emerges over what the ultimate impact of that instruction. One positions frames literacy as a means of improving student judgment and use. Another pushes further toward metacognitive monitoring, asking students to recognize when AI is displacing rather than supporting their thinking. Both approaches depend, however, on students acting against incentives for speed, convenience, and outsourcing. Within these structural limitations, the burden shifts toward teachers to design stronger scaffolds, monitor use, and create conditions that make better judgment possible. Module 4: The Teaching Profession Question. The debate over AI and teaching divides between enhancement, displacement, and transformation. One route treats AI as a tool that can reduce routine work and expand teachers&#8217; capacity. Another emphasizes the risk that automation will erode professional judgment, narrow the role, or justify staffing reductions. A third position argues that AI will change the work itself, shifting value toward the forms of judgment, relationship, and instructional design that are harder to automate. Each path redistributes labor and responsibility differently, and each raises its own version of the same question: what parts of teaching are we trying to preserve, and what parts are we willing to redesign? Module 5: The Governance Question. The governance debate asks who should make decisions about AI use. District-level approaches can respond to local conditions, but they give way uneven rules and capacity across schools. State policy can create clearer expectations and reduce some of that variation, while also imposing requirements that may not fit local contexts well. Federal action can establish broader protections or standards, but it operates at even greater distance from classroom practice. Across all three levels, governance solves one problem by creating another: greater consistency usually comes at the cost of local flexibility, while local control preserves flexibility at the cost of coherence. Module 6: The Evidence Question. The debate over whether AI improves learning is constrained less by a lack of claims than by the limits of the evidence beneath them. Early studies can show promising gains in specific contexts, while critics point to small samples, short time horizons, narrow tasks, and rapidly changing tools. A more cautious position emphasizes that the technology is evolving faster than the research base can stabilize. The result is a recurring evidence problem: schools are being asked to make consequential decisions while the object of study keeps changing and long-term effects remain difficult to isolate. Module 7: The Equity Question. The equity debate pulls in two directions at once. Expanding access can reduce disparities when some students already have private access to powerful tools, paid subscriptions, or informal support. But broader access can also widen other gaps if students differ in the knowledge, guidance, and judgment needed to use AI well. Restricting access presents the inverse problem: it may protect some students from premature dependence while leaving others to learn with AI outside school anyway. The equity question therefore resists a simple access solution because access itself does not determine who benefits. Module 8: The Data Question. The debate over student data begins with a straightforward concern: AI systems can collect, process, and retain information that schools have obligations to protect. One response is tighter governance focused on approved tools, contracts, data-use limits, and clearer rules about what can enter an AI system. But those protections can normalize institutional use even as they reduce some privacy risks, while less-governed use continues outside official channels. Module 9: The Curriculum Question. The curriculum debate asks what students still need to know when AI can perform more of the work that schools have traditionally taught. One position treats AI like the calculator: once a tool can handle a task reliably, instruction should shift toward higher-order work. Another emphasizes capacities that appear harder to automate, such as judgment, interpretation, creativity, and interpersonal reasoning. A third insists that students still need substantial knowledge in order to use AI well in the first place. Module 10: The Beneficiaries Question. The debate over who benefits from AI in education often splits between suspicion of commercial interests and confidence that partnerships improve educational outcomes into schools. One route emphasizes vendor incentives, market expansion, and the risk that educational priorities become shaped by products in search of a use case. Another points to the expertise, infrastructure, and investment that outside partners can provide . Module 11: The Development Question. The debate over AI and young people extends beyond school performance into questions about cognitive and social development. Concerns about companion AI focus on attachment, dependency, and the possibility that systems designed to respond continuously may shape relationships in ways we do not yet understand. Similar concerns surround cognition: repeated reliance on AI may alter how students practice memory, problem solving, and self-monitoring. But the evidence base is thin, especially for long-term effects. The debate therefore repeatedly returns to a measurement problem: the consequences that matter most may be the ones we are least able to observe yet. Module 12: The Pace Question. The final module turns from particular AI policies to the tempo of change itself. One position argues that schools are already behind and need to move faster. Another emphasizes caution, evidence, and the risks of institutional change driven by urgency. A third treats innovation as an imperative in its own right, making continued adoption a marker of responsiveness or relevance. The difficulty is that speed can become self-justifying: delay is read as failure, while movement is treated as evidence of progress. While the circularity of the debate is difficult to miss, several gaps or breaks in that structure begin to emerge across the visualizations.
