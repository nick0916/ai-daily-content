## AI’s Historical Present: What Does Her Future Hold?

一位學校行政主管以四個時間點的個人經驗，記錄 AI 從「寫不出好文章」走到「能代替學生完成作業」。 **Why** 作者在女兒出生時思考，孩子將生活在機器會對話的世界中，因而重新追問 AI 對教育的意義。 **How** 文章以時間序列鋪陳：2023 年同事認為 AI 寫不出好文章；2024 年學生被誤指使用 AI；2025 年 AI agent 能在學習平台上完成課程模組；2026 年作者引述 Punya Mishra 談「教學法債務」（pedagogical debt），認為 AI 讓這筆債到期。 **What** 作者提出核心問題：AI 能完成作業已不是重點，關鍵在於「我們是否建立了值得學生親自完成的學校」；他也擔心若只給學生懷疑而非教育，學校將失去其存在的意義。

### 重點
- 2025 年作者觀察到 AI agent 能開啟瀏覽器、點選選單並完成任務，也能代替學生在 Canvas 上交件
- 作者引用 Punya Mishra 的「pedagogical debt」概念，認為 AI 迫使學校面對長期未修正的教學問題
- 作者的核心提問：我們是否建立了一所值得親自完成作業的學校，而不只是防止 AI 作弊

Today&#8217;s author is Elizabeth Helfant , Associate Head for Academic Affairs at Ravenscroft School in Raleigh, NC. Subscribe now It is February 2026, and I am holding my fourth grandchild. She is a girl, and her middle name is mine. Someone hands me my phone to take a picture, and for a moment I don&#8217;t lift it. I am thinking about the phone. I am thinking about what will live inside it by the time she is old enough to hold one. What is her world going to be? The phone can&#8217;t tell me. Nobody can. But holding her, I realize that isn&#8217;t all I want to know. I want to know who will be in that world with her. I have been asking some version of that question since the fall of 2022, when ChatGPT arrived and every educator I know began a private reckoning. But I have asked it in a professional register. What does this mean for our assessments, our policies, our faculty? Holding her, the question drops into a different key. It is no longer about a school. It is about a person I love who will never know a world without machines that talk back. When I try to name the moment that defined my journey with AI, I can&#8217;t choose one unless it is this one. There has been a moment for every new model, every release that reset what I thought was possible. But holding her, I can see a handful of them lined up like stones across a creek. I am not yet sure where they lead. * * * It is spring 2023. I am sitting across from a department chair, one of the sharpest minds on our faculty, a historian who can take an argument apart the way some people take apart an engine. She is telling me AI cannot write- not well, not really. She has tried it, and the prose is flat, generic, hollow. She is adamant. I nod. And in my head I am thinking: No, you just aren&#8217;t asking it right. I don&#8217;t say it. I&#8217;m not proud of the thought, either. It is the small certainty of someone who has spent a few extra evenings prompting. Over the next months she becomes our resident contrarian on all things AI, and I suppose I become her opposite. We are both sure. What strikes me now is how little either of us is listening. She is defending something real. Writing is thinking, and thinking can&#8217;t be outsourced. She is leaning into the human thing we have both long asserted. We read to learn and write to think. I am defending something real too. The tool will get better, and we had better understand it before it does. We are each holding half of the argument and treat it as the whole. * * * It is 2024. A student is upset. They have been accused of using AI on an assignment, and they convincingly claim they didn&#8217;t. That should be the whole story, the sting of a false accusation. But it isn&#8217;t what troubles them most. What they keep coming back to is the idea that no one is teaching us about it. They are expected to stay away from something everyone knows is everywhere, and no one will show them how it works, when it&#8217;s appropriate, or what it&#8217;s good for. They are also irritated that one set of rules applies for adults and another for students. I don&#8217;t have a good answer. We have detection anxiety and an honor code. We do not yet have a curriculum. The student is asking for what schools exist to provide, guidance through the world as it actually is, and we are offering suspicion instead of education. It is 2025. The agents have arrived. I watch an AI open a browser, click through menus, and finish a task on its own. My first thought, I confess, is about my annual HR training: the videos, the quizzes. It could do those for me. It could sit through the modules and pass the quizzes while I did something else. Then a second thought arrives right behind it, colder: It could do the same for any student: open Canvas, complete the assignment, turn it in. * * * Everything we have built, the redesigned assignments, the in-class writing, the process portfolios, assumes a student at least has to be present for the work. Now the work can be done by something that is not the student, logged in as the student. The question was never really whether AI could do the work. It is whether we have built a school where the work is worth doing yourself. * * * It is 2026. I am listening to a podcast featuring by Punya Mishra. I know Punya from years ago, from TPACK and the long, messy work of rolling out 1:1 laptop programs, when we were all trying to understand how technology, pedagogy, and content fit together. Now he is writing about pedagogical debt. We have had Mind Brain and Education research telling us we need to change- that our practices are not always consistent with what we know about learning but we choose to put off fixing it. AI has made that debt due. In that moment, something in me tilts toward hope. I have known for a long time, as we all have, that school needs to change: the rigid schedule, the assessment that rewards compliance, the essay assigned mainly to prove a student read the book. We have said so at conferences for twenty years. What if AI isn&#8217;t the thing that breaks school, but the thing that finally makes us pay down the debt? A lever long enough to move what we couldn&#8217;t move ourselves. On most days I still believe that. On other days I wonder whether it is the same optimism I felt about laptops, about the internet, about every tool that was going to transform everything. * * * It is February 2026, and she is asleep against my shoulder. I think about the chair who said it couldn&#8217;t write, and I hear the question underneath her certainty, one I wasn&#8217;t ready for. What do we lose when we stop doing hard things ourselves? I think about the student who wanted to be taught, not policed. I think about the HR quizzes, and about students who could automate their way through school, and I wonder whether we will have built something worth showing up for. I think about Punya&#8217;s post and the lever, and whether we will have the nerve to pull it. I don&#8217;t know what her world is going to be. That is the honest answer, and the present tense is the only one I trust to tell it. Right now none of us know. We are all holding something small and new and trying to imagine what it will need. By the time she starts kindergarten, the tools will be unrecognizable. What I hope stays recognizable is a teacher who knows her, who asks what she thinks and then waits for the answer. I hope someone teaches her about the machines instead of only warning her about them. I hope the adults around her, the skeptics and the enthusiasts, have learned to listen to each other in a way the chair and I didn&#8217;t at first. Her middle name is mine. Some of what I carry will go with her. I would like it to be the questions, curiosity and hope, not the certainties.
