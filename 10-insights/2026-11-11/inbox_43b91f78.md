## The Tiger That Outlived the Company

東印度公司博物館的興衰：從收藏東方文物，到隨殖民統治結束而解散。

**Why** 東印度公司不只做貿易，還治理領土、維持軍隊、蒐集資訊，因此累積大量藏品。

**How** 文章以藏品的來源與展示為線索，說明藏品經由戰利品、捐贈與 1851 年萬國博覽會增加。

**What** 文中提到 1842 年展出的 Tipu Sultan 機械虎（1799 年隨戰利品運抵英國），1858 年印度統治權移交英國王室後，1879 年印度博物館解散、藏品分散，機械虎最終留存於 V&A 博物館。

- 東印度公司博物館 1801 年以 Oriental Repository 之名起步，收藏涵蓋佛像、波斯手稿、標本與農具
- 1842 年展出的 Tipu Sultan 機械虎，1799 年在塞林加帕丹戰役後隨戰利品運抵英國

In 1842, visitors to the East India Company’s museum in London encountered something, which many of them havn’t seen in the past. It was an almost life-sized wooden tiger, attacking a European soldier lying helplessly on his back. A handle on the side operated a small organ concealed inside the tiger, producing sounds intended to imitate the animal’s growls and the soldier’s dying cries. Unfortunately, the machinery was not working particularly well, as aspected. It belonged to Tipu Sultan of Mysore, when British forces captured Seringapatam in 1799, killing Tipu and looting his palace, the mechanical tiger was among the objects shipped to England. It eventually became one of the most popular exhibits at the Company’s museum on Leadenhall Street. It was an unusual possession for a trading corporation, although by then the East India Company had accumulated considerably more than spices, textiles and trading privileges. It governed large territories, maintained armies and collected information about the lands under its control. The museum at India House Their museum began in 1801 as an Oriental Repository , intended to preserve manuscripts, specimens and objects collected across Asia. In the next few decades, its employees sent back everything from Buddhist sculptures and Persian manuscripts to stuffed birds, fossils, agricultural implements and musical instruments. Some objects were purchased/donated or just looted during military campaigns/taken after conquest. By the 1840s, the collection had become difficult to manage. Its contents served many purposes. Scholars used manuscripts and natural specimens. Company officials used geographical and botanical information. Visitors came to see all these curiosities. However, public access was limited, since the museum was admitting visitors only on Saturdays between eleven and three. As, the Charter Act of 1833 ended its remaining trading monopoly, and British manufacturers were increasingly interested in obtaining raw materials directly from India . Cotton was especially important, along with indigo, timber, silk and medicinal plants. One person who recognised the commercial possibilities of the collection was John Forbes Royle , a surgeon and botanist who had worked at the Company’s botanical garden in Saharanpur. In 1838, he became its Reporter on the Products of India in London. Royle believed British manufacturers needed reliable information about the materials available across the subcontinent, their properties and how they could be used. London merchants had already learnt the value of organising commercial information, something we explored through shipping news and insurance in The Cup That Built the Market . Royle wanted manufacturers to have something more tangible: samples they could inspect and compare. A museum could help them discover which Indian fibres, dyes or timber might be suitable for their factories. The Great Exhibition of 1851 offered him an unusually large opportunity to put this idea into practice. The Crystal Palace, 1851 On 1 May 1851, Queen Victoria opened the Great Exhibition in London’s Hyde Park. Its temporary home, designed by Joseph Paxton, was an enormous structure of iron and glass that became known as the Crystal Palace. Over the following five and a half months, more than six million people visited to examine machinery, manufactured goods, raw materials and objects from around the world. The building itself was an industrial achievement. Nearly 294,000 panes of glass had been supplied for its construction, making possible a vast covered space filled with daylight. In The Material That Taught Us to See , we followed how improvements in glassmaking made precision lenses possible. At the Crystal Palace, advances in glass manufacturing had found an architectural application on an extraordinary scale. Royle was responsible for organising the Indian section. He circulated extensive lists asking committees across India to send cotton, silk, dyes, minerals, timber, agricultural implements and examples of manufactured goods. His intention was practical. Manufacturers should be able to examine different materials, understand where they came from and compare their qualities. The committees collecting the exhibits had other ideas about what might best represent India. Alongside useful raw materials came elaborate shawls, carved ivory, jewels and ceremonial objects. One committee in Bengal selected a cushion embroidered with the names of Victoria and Albert in diamonds and pearls. The Friend of India newspaper complained that such extravagance did little to explain India’s economic resources. Royle had also wanted materials arranged by type, so that cotton from different regions could be compared alongside one another. Instead, the exhibition was largely organised geographically. Visitors could admire the Indian displays, but a manufacturer trying to compare samples had a less convenient experience. The spectacle proved more attractive to the public than the commercial catalogue Royle had imagined. Nevertheless, the exhibition generated an enormous collection of objects, many of which would now find a second home at India House. A museum without its Company After the exhibition closed, Royle and the museum’s curator, Thomas Horsfield, selected items for the Company’s collections. These included models of Indian trades, agricultural machinery, boats, textiles and raw materials. The directors subsequently approved an ambitious expansion of the museum. The old Tea Sale Room at India House, once central to the Company’s trading business, was converted into an elaborate Indian-style gallery with columns, arches and carved screens. Other rooms accommodated models of buildings, manufacturing processes, village occupations and transport. Visitors could walk past miniature representations of Indian craftspeople at work, then enter galleries containing sculptures, manuscripts and natural history specimens. The expanded museum opened sometime between November 1857 and January 1858. The timing was unfortunate for its owners. The uprising of 1857 had already shaken British rule across northern and central India, and the Company’s ability to govern was under intense scrutiny. In August 1858, the British government formally transferred the administration of India from the East India Company to the Crown. The museum had barely opened in its new form when the institution that owned it lost its principal purpose. India House was demolished in the early 1860s. Its collections were moved first to temporary accommodation in Whitehall, later to the India Office and eventually to South Kensington. In 1879, the India Museum ceased operating as a separate institution, and its collections were divided among other museums. Tipu’s mechanical tiger survived the dispersal. It eventually entered the Victoria and Albert Museum, where it remains on display. Jessica Ratcliff, in her book Monopolizing Knowledge , offers a rather different way of looking at what survived with it. The East India Company’s monopoly extended beyond trade. It influenced who could travel to India, what could be collected, which scholars had access to the material, and how knowledge about India was organised. Much of this work was financed through revenues collected in India, while the resulting manuscripts, specimens and objects accumulated in London, where they helped advance British scientific research. As the museums became more accessible, all the knowledge accumulated through the Company’s privileges remained concentrated in Britain . Ratcliff traces how these arrangements helped shape Britain’s scientific institutions and contributed to a wider imbalance between places where knowledge was collected and places where it was studied , classified and published.
