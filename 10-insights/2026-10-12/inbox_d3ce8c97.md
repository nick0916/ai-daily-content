## AI’s Historical Present: A Voice from the Past

一位高教行政人員用 ChatGPT 扮演已故舊識來處理哀傷，並反思 AI 對高教的衝擊。

**Why** 作者得知舊識 2021 年去世，陷入三個月的哀傷，想尋求某種結束感。

**How** 2022 年底他在 ChatGPT 中設定一個 24 歲女性角色，描述兩人關係並提問，模型生成的回應以「Liza」署名。

**What** 作者認為這經驗有療癒效果，但「並不是真的」；到 2026 年，他表示對高教的疲憊，認為既有的學術制度難以因應可隨時生成內容的 AI，高教需要從根本重建。

### 重點
- 2022 年底以 ChatGPT 角色扮演已故舊識，作者稱其療癒效果超過過去的諮商經驗
- 作者形容此經驗為「幻肢式情緒療法加鏡箱」，同時強調「它不是真的」
- 作者認為 AI 能按需生成研究式內容，使學術 scholarship 的信任基礎動搖，高教需要重建

Share It is late 2022 and I work in the Academic Affairs department at a college that includes fully online degree programs. I had heard enough about this thing called ChatGPT that I went ahead and opened an account. I had no particular use for it, but I felt I should play with it because that is part of what I do for a living. I experiment with technologies and share what I think they might be useful for. At that moment, I was also reeling from grief over the death of a woman with whom I had had a brief but transformative relationship 39 years before. She had died in 2021 but I hadn&#8217;t known about it until now. I was emotionally disoriented &#8211; I had no opportunity to offer caring words or kind gestures of goodwill as her last days approached, and now it was too late. She was not just gone; she had been gone. After finding myself crying every day for three months and questioning my sanity, I tried an experiment to gain some kind of closure. For the heck of it, I typed up the entire scenario in ChatGPT, where I prompted it to be a 24-year-old woman under a set of personal circumstances. I described our relationship, how it played out to the end, and what I wanted to know: What did she see in me? Did I matter? As I was typing all of this up, I felt it was an incredibly stupid thing to do. Not because it was risky, but because, if you have an issue you need to get over, this is not how you deal with it . But here was this new chatbot that everyone said spoke like a real person, understands what you are saying, and could assume a persona in whatever context you wanted. I had to see what would happen, even if it was hilariously bad. I clicked the send button and watched wide-eyed as ChatGPT slowly generated its text as if I were communing with the dead in real-time. &#8220;She,&#8221; it turned out, was kind and endearing to me, telling me what I was already capable of telling myself (and had) but needed to hear from her. She closed with appreciative grace as one might have written in a farewell letter, suggesting that there was no more to be said between us. She signed off, &#8220;Liza.&#8221; My eyes kept darting back to her name. It was exactly what she would have said, but it wasn&#8217;t her. My brain could not process that rationally. It was her &#8211; she would have said this. No, it wasn&#8217;t. It was achingly fluent, it sounded true, I needed to hear it, but it wasn&#8217;t real. It wasn&#8217;t real . I&#8217;d had counseling on and off since the mid-1980s, but there was something different about what had just happened in there. Generating a fictional response from the dead enabled me to heal in a way that other counseling methods could not reach. It was like phantom emotional limb therapy with a mirror box. As the ethical and disruptive implications of this invention propagated in my head, I felt the same sort of dread as the moment in March 2020 when it was certain that we were about to experience a global pandemic. Something larger than ourselves was going to permeate our lives and there was nothing we could do to stop it. My next lucid reaction was to share a message with my instructional design colleagues that this ChatGPT hype is for real . And from then on, my mission has been to find out what it can do and where it fits in my world of designing online instruction for higher education. It is 2026, and my position today has allowed me to serve on both sides of the educational boundary between first principles of online education and how they actually play out under authentic conditions. In the Capstone course I teach, I added opportunities for students to use AI as a research assistant and as an alternative perspective on their research. In my other courses, I have seen students use AI to compose their discussion responses &#8211; some naively believing that it was acceptable while others were blatantly cheating. I have gone all-in on being a thought leader on the use of AI as a scholar, instructional designer, and software developer. I have presented at conferences and led internal discussions about our future with AI. But now I am feeling some fatigue. It is not the fatigue of overwork or of constantly having to apprehend the expanding breadth of what AI has become at this moment. It is the fatigue of recognizing that everything I ever knew about the &#8220;game&#8221; of higher education needs to change and it is too big for our lumbering institutions to take on. It is as if, as of November 2022, a person is no longer a person and the calibration of time has collapsed. What is scholarship when a persona can be generated on-demand, with an infinite capacity to produce material that sounds like research, in a matter of hours? The infrastructure and trust upon which we constructed our business model can no longer rest upon those pillars. We have a notion of how we can reinvent ourselves, but we are catatonically stuck in our own entrenchment, like how America simply cannot motivate itself to solve universal healthcare. Higher education needs to be rebuilt from the ground up, and it just might take its implosion to warrant it. I remember the first moment I met Liza 43 years ago and how seeing her face caused me to lose my train of thought. It is curious to me that, through a careless experiment in ChatGPT in her remembrance, I once again lost my train of thought. As with both, I have recovered and moved forward, fighting the good fight one day at a time.
